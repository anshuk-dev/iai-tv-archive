# Gender fluidity is not a new phenomenon

*Virginia Woolf, Hirschfeld, and the fluidity of the self*

**By Pamela Caughie** | *23rd February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,547 words*

![Gender fluidity is not a new phenomenon](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/sackville-west-portrait.webp)

---

*We often look back at previous generations with a sense of moral superiority. Yet throughout history, ideas about sex, gender, and identity often challenged the assumptions of their time. Professor **Pamela Caughie** argues that by moving past our patronizing view of the past, we can see that today’s debates are not a contemporary crisis, but a continuation of a much older, ongoing conversation about what it means to be human.*

For those who study the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, a period known in literature and the arts as the modernist era, the concept of transgender is nothing new, even if that term wasn’t coined until around 1990. Before then, what we call transgender went by many terms: transsexualism, transvestitism, sexual intermediacy, contrary sexual feeling, even sexual identity, broadly conceived. Yet from social media to social policy, one would think transgender recently burst onto the scene, perpetrated by the forces of “gender ideology.”

An early [Executive Order (#14168)](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/defending-women-from-gender-ideology-extremism-and-restoring-biological-truth-to-the-federal-government/) by Donald Trump, entitled “Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government,” warns that “gender ideology” imperils “the validity of the entire American system” by erasing “sex in language and policy.” “Gender ideology,” the EO claims, replaces “the immutable biological reality of sex” with “an internal, fluid, and subjective sense of self unmoored from biological facts,” thereby “invalidating the true and biological category of ‘woman’.” President Trump calls this “[transgender lunacy](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-protocol/id1817731112).”

\_\_\_

Even those who support transgender people, such as the gay rights activist Andrew Sullivan, disclaim “gender ideology.” Once a triumph of civil rights legislation, the LGBTQ movement is now focused on “queering the sex binary,” [Sullivan writes](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/06/26/opinion/gay-lesbian-trans-rights.html?smid=nytcore-ios-share&referringSource=articleShare), “replacing the fact of biological sex with the phantasms of gender ideology” and thereby dissolving “natural distinctions between men and women.”

Yet modernist scholars know that “the immutable biological reality of sex” and the “natural distinctions between men and women” have been disputed for well over a century.  At the turn of the twentieth century, psychiatry and medical science, social media and social movements, literature and the arts were all upending long-held beliefs about the [irrefutable stability of sex](https://iai.tv/articles/gender-is-decided-by-experience-not-biology-or-choice-auid-3143). Transgender, then, understood as a change of sex, emerged out of early-twentieth century scientific discoveries and technologies, such as synthetic hormones and advances in plastic surgery, but it was also engendered by new movements in modern philosophy and modernist aesthetics.

In philosophy, anti-realist thinking rejected Enlightenment universalism and understood concepts of the real as embedded in a particular time and culture. In the arts, formal innovations responded to and shaped a changing social discourse of sexuality and subjectivity. The nexus of scientific experimentation with the body, philosophical skepticism of Cartesian dualism, and aesthetic experimentation with representation reconceived notions of gender and identity in the modernist era. Transgender instantiates the modernists’ philosophical revolt against the idea of a [fixed human nature.](https://iai.tv/video/the-rise-and-fall-of-sex-is-biological-sex-an-illusion)

The early decades of the twentieth century witnessed profound changes in concepts of sexual and gender identity.  Radical social movements, such as suffrage and reforms in marriage and divorce laws; and the ubiquity of the “modern girl,” who cut her hair, dressed in pants, smoked in public, and rode the subway, aroused anxiety about [“masculine women and feminine men,”](https://www.google.com/search?q=masculine+women+feminine+men&rlz=1C2GCEA_enUS1198US1198&sca_esv=109423f6832a2638&sxsrf=ANbL-n75yeiU2MjNzp1Z5JY6UQZ8sP_-HA%3A1768849330715&source=hp&ei=sn9uabiOKoPKkPIP3bjh8Qo&iflsig=AFdpzrgAAAAAaW6NwpdSAR5l-kO-hFmwpfHGsJI8itxf&gs_ssp=eJzj4tVP1zc0TE_LKsvILjc1YPSSyU0sTi7NycxLVSjPz03NU0hLzc3MA3GBHABb9Q_U&oq=Masculine+Women%2C+&gs_lp=Egdnd3Mtd2l6IhFNYXNjdWxpbmUgV29tZW4sICoCCAAyBRAuGIAEMgUQABiABDIFEAAYgAQyBRAAGIAEMgUQABiABDIFEAAYgAQyBRAAGIAEMgUQABiABDIFEAAYgAQyBRAAGIAESPY-ULkMWPAucAF4AJABAJgBcKABpAuqAQQxMy40uAEByAEA-AEBmAISoALwC6gCCsICDRAjGIAEGCcYigUY6gLCAgcQIxgnGOoCwgITECMY8AUYgAQYJxjJAhiKBRjqAsICBBAjGCfCAgoQIxiABBgnGIoFwgILEAAYgAQYkQIYigXCAgsQLhiABBiRAhiKBcICDhAuGIAEGLEDGIMBGIoFwgIOEC4YgAQYsQMY0QMYxwHCAgsQABiABBixAxiDAcICEBAuGIAEGMcBGCcYigUYrwHCAgoQABiABBhDGIoFwgIREC4YgAQYkQIYxwEYigUYrwHCAhAQLhiABBixAxhDGIMBGIoFwgILEAAYgAQYsQMYyQPCAgsQABiABBiSAxiKBcICERAAGIAEGLEDGJIDGIoFGI0GwgIIEAAYgAQYsQPCAg0QABiABBixAxhDGIoFwgIIEC4YgAQYsQPCAhIQABiABBixAxhDGIoFGEYY-QGYAwXxBedCf_f5QuuzkgcEMTQuNKAHyKABsgcEMTMuNLgH6wvCBwYwLjguMTDIBziACAA&sclient=gws-wiz#fpstate=ive&vld=cid:fed11d45,vid:JXqAwFcwKMg,st:0) the title of a 1926 popular American song. Psychoanalysts, sexologists, and endocrinologists challenged the sacrosanct nineteenth-century belief in sexual dimorphism in positing a universal bisexuality.

Anthropologists wrote about the tradition of the man-woman, men dressing and living as women, in various cultures. Newspaper and later radio accounts of women living as men, and men as women, were widespread. In 1936, the British journal *Urania*, which began publication in 1916 with the mission to abolish the false distinction of sex, published an article entitled “Another Extraordinary Triumph” about a sex-change operation, the word “another” signaling that this extraordinary event was not singular. In the early stages of writing *Orlando: A Biography* (1928)—that delightful romp through the centuries in which the protagonist lives over three hundred years, changing from a man to a woman somewhere in the seventeenth century—VirginiaWoolf was fascinated by a newspaper article about a young woman who became a man. That revelation provided not so much the subject for her fictionalized biography as the occasion for re-conceptualizing the self as mutable, protean.

It is no wonder that so many modernist literary works feature transgender themes: Aldous Huxley’s *The Farcical History of Richard Greenow* (1920), Sherwood Anderson’s *The Man Who Became a Woman*, James Joyce’s *Ulysses,* and T. S. Eliot’s *The Waste Land* (all 1922), Radclyffe Hall's *The Well of Loneliness* (1928), Hanns Heinz Ewers*’ Fundvogel: Die Geschichte einer Wandlung* (Found Bird: The Story of a Transformation) (1929), [Lucie Delarue-Mardrus](https://www.womenandchildrenfirst.com/search/author/%22Delarue-Mardrus%2C%20Lucie%22)’ *L'Ange et les Pervers* (The Angel and the Perverts) (1930), Djuna Barnes’s *Nightwood* (1936), and Denton Welch’s *Maiden Voyage* (1943), to name a few. Modernism signified its radicalism, its break with nineteenth-century realism, through new expressions of gender in art and in life.

In the early nineteenth century, “sexual identity” referred to the difference between male and female based primarily on anatomical features. Over the course of that century, the term came to refer to sexuality, understood in terms of the object and aim of one’s sexual attraction. By the early twentieth century, sexual identity as a dimorphic concept (male/female, homo/hetero) began to give way to a range of body morphologies, gender presentations, and social experiences, a conceptual shift that gave rise to the language of sexual intermediaries. The transmogrification of the sexed body was being instantiated in the 1920s at Magnus Hirschfeld’s Institute for Sexual Science in Berlin, a popular tourist spot for modernists, where “sex-change” surgeries were performed as early as 1926. Sexual intermediaries became a key focus of Hirschfeld’s research. Initially used for homosexuals, that term soon expanded to include cross-dressers, intersex individuals, and transsexuals, whom Hirschfeld called “transvestites” in his [1910 book by that title](https://transreads.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2022-08-12_62f6791d32d2c_TheTransvestites.pdf).With that term, Hirschfeld was the first to distinguish sexual orientation from sexual identity. Transvestitism was no longer a signifier of homosexuality but a discrete identity.

\_\_\_

Scientific experimentation with bodies and aesthetic experimentation with genres are reciprocal cultural forms that work together to reshape notions of gender and sexual identity.

\_\_\_

In *Tranvestites*, Hirschfeld writes, “The separation of humanity into male and female halves belongs to the doctrines and guiding principles that have crossed over into the flesh and blood of every person.” Such deeply held truths “have become foundations of government, tradition, society, and religion” and thus are “doubly uncomfortable” when one is forced, through evidence of nature and science, to abandon them. Here, Hirschfeld voices a common strain of modernist-era thought: namely, that our concepts of sex and gender are embedded in our particular time and culture, enforced and reinforced through institutions, laws, and customs. The sexologists’ naming of new gendered and sexed subjects fractured the traditional gender regime rooted in a sexual binary, a binary that the current U.S. administration, like the Nazis in the 1930s, has sought to restore by proclaiming there are only two genders.

Hirschfeld’s firm belief that sexual intermediaries could be explained biologically, in terms of hormone imbalance, informed his early use of the [Steinach procedure](https://web.archive.org/web/20090131072539/http:/www.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,883971,00.html). Eugen Steinach’s “sex gland” therapy was thought to be a cure for homosexuality, but Hirschfeld quickly grasped its significance for “transvestites” who wished to change their sex, not just to cross-dress. For Hirschfeld, though, the concept of gender was as or more significant than the physical transformation itself. The evidence that undoes the naturalized belief in a sexual binary for Hirschfeld is based on clients’ case histories as much as on doctors’ physical and psychological examinations, on narrative as much as on anatomy. Scientific experimentation with bodies and aesthetic experimentation with genres are reciprocal cultural forms that work together to reshape notions of gender and sexual identity. As [Anna Katarina Schaffner has remarked](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9780230358904), sexologists made “sexual perversion” attractive to modernist writers because of its challenge to “established conventions of the natural,” and the normative.

Yet Hirschfeld’s work shifted the focus of study from sexual perversions(based on behaviors) to sexual types (based on identification). His theory of sexual intermediaries undid the binary opposition of man and woman, positing an endless range of variants between these extremes. The pure man or pure woman are, he writes in *Transvestites*, “only abstractions, invented extremes; in reality they have not as yet been observed.” His theory dovetails with modernist-era philosophies, such as Ludwig Wittgenstein’s later writings, where the philosopher rejects the idea that our concepts are somehow imposed on us by reality. Recently, [Harry Oosterhuis](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/118341284/Oosterhuis_2000_Stepchildren_of_Nature.pdf), [Chris Waters](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9780230501805), [Laura Doan](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/palatable-poison/9780231118743/), [Julia Epstein](https://academic.oup.com/jsh/article-abstract/29/4/976/940022?redirectedFrom=fulltext), and [Heike Bauer,](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1wf4dmd) among others, have countered Foucauldian readings of sexologists as imposing categories on unwitting and unwilling individuals and instead have explored the confluence of sexological discourse, modernist writings, and individual experience.

Today’s “transgender” should not be conflated with gender concepts from the modernist era. Yet tracing the evolution of such terms, situating them in the cultural and philosophical innovations of their origin, can restore historicaltruth to the government’s insistence on the biological truth of only two sexes. As biologist [Anne Fausto-Sterling](http://www.portalenazionalelgbt.it/bancadeidati/schede/the-five-sexes-why-male-and-female-are-not-enough/file/Articolo%20ENG) declared over thirty years ago, if the state and legal system have an interest in maintaining only two sexes, our collective biological bodies do not. Hirschfeld would agree.
