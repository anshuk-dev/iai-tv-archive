# New study finds governments that kill their own people can be more popular

*The psychology behind extrajudicial murder*

**By Rodwan Abouharb** | *27th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,721 words*

![New study finds governments that kill their own people can be more popular](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/new-study-finds-governments-that-kill-their-own-people.webp)

---

*It seems obvious that governments arbitrarily killing their own citizens would make them less popular. But a recent study across Latin America found that higher rates of state killing were associated with increased popularity of the president. This is especially unexpected as less harsh forms of punishment, like imprisonment, do make the president less popular. UCL's **Rodwan Abouharb** argues the psychology of this can be explained by cognitive dissonance among those who have been victims of crime. This has troubling implications for democracy, as it shows a perverse set of incentives which, in certain circumstances, could encourage leaders to kill their citizens without recourse to the law.*

In 2022, El Salvador’s parliament handed the country’s president Nayib Bukele sweeping emergency powers to crush the country’s street gangs. This meant due process rights were suspended and tens of thousands were thrown into already overcrowded prisons, whilst death squads carried out scores of extrajudicial killings with the backing of the government. Bukele has since taken to calling himself the “world’s coolest dictator,” only half-jokingly. And more than half the country loves him for it.

This is not supposed to happen. Political science has a simple and intuitive model of how citizens judge their governments: a state that serves and protects you earns your trust, and a state that turns its guns on its own people loses it. Corruption erodes trust, and so does incompetence. You would therefore think that killing your own citizens without a trial erodes trust too.

Yet a [new study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14754835.2026.2696310) by Rodwan Abouharb, Sam Bell and Risa Kitagawa has found something more unsettling. The research draws on more than a decade of survey data from eighteen Latin American countries and shows that when governments carry out extrajudicial killing, trust in the president doesn’t fall—instead, it actually rises. Furthermore, the people whose trust in the president rises the most are not those who are indifferent or uninformed, but victims of crime, who you might expect to be more alert to the dangers of a state no longer obeying the law.

The situation becomes clearer when you stop treating “state violence” as a single tool. Governments have many different tools of repression at their disposal. These include arbitrary arrest, censorship, torture, forced disappearance, extrajudicial killing, and more. Previous research has tended to lump all of these together rather than splitting them up and studying them in isolation. Abouharb, Bell and Kitagawa pulled them apart, using a dataset that separately tracks four distinct categories of state violence, and asked “does it matter, to public opinion, which [kind of violence](https://iai.tv/video/violence-vengeance-and-virtue) a government uses?”

It turns out it matters enormously. Political imprisonment, which involves locking up dissident or alleged criminals without a real trial, does reliably corrode trust in the executive, which is what the textbook model would predict. But extrajudicial killing does the opposite. Across the eighteen countries in the study, higher rates of state killing were associated with higher, rather than lower, presidential trust. If you were to move a country from no reported killings to the highest level recorded in the data, the probability that a citizen expresses confidence in the executive rises. If you moved the same country to the highest level of political imprisonment, then confidence collapses by more than ten percentage points. Two forms of state violence are met with two opposite verdicts from the public.

The authors’ answer for why this happens rests on a mechanism most of us recognise from far smaller moments in our own lives: cognitive dissonance. First described by the psychologist Leon Festinger in the 1950s, cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort we feel when a piece of new information clashes with a belief we hold dear—and the surprisingly long lengths we will go to in order to make that discomfort go away, usually not by changing our behaviour, but by changing our story about it.

The classic illustration is the smoker who knows cigarettes cause cancer but keeps smoking anyway. Rather than quit, many smokers quietly revise their beliefs: they decide the health risks are overstated, or that their grandmother smoked forty a day and lived to ninety, or that everything is dangerous eventually. The belief bends so the behaviour doesn’t have to.

Abouharb and his colleagues argue that citizens do something structurally similar when confronted with especially brutal state violence when the violence in question is irreversible. Political imprisonment, however unjust, leaves a door open. Prisoners can, at least in principle, be released or exonerated. There is a legal channel, however imperfect, through which the wrong could be righted. Because these theoretical “exit options” exist, citizens don’t need to talk themselves into approving of it; they can simply condemn it and move on.

A killing forecloses that possibility entirely. There is no reversing death. According to a substantial body of psychological research, cognitive dissonance is at its most intense when a harmful act cannot be undone. That’s when the observer has no “exit option” that would let them avoid confronting what happened. Faced with a lethal act by their own government that cannot be appealed or reversed, and which they are, in practical terms, powerless to stop, citizens can do one of two things. The first option is to hold onto the belief that the government they’ve elected has committed something monstrous and illegitimate. The second, called motivated reasoning, is to reframe the killing in their minds as necessary and deserved, even a form of protection. For many people, the second is a far less onerous psychological task. And so trust in the killer state goes up, rather than down.

A striking part of the study is who drives this phenomenon, as it isn’t the general public, evenly. It’s specifically people who have themselves recently been victims of violent crime, or had a family member victimised.

This makes sense once you follow the psychology through. Trauma research consistently finds that people who have experienced violence develop heightened threat sensitivity, a kind of hair-trigger vigilance to danger, along with more intense emotional reactions when they perceive a threat being addressed. For someone who has been assaulted or lost a relative to violent crime, the sight of the state executing suspected criminals may invoke an understandable hunger for protection at the same time as an unease about the lack of due process and the rule of law. These emotions are hard to reconcile and are what lays the foundation for cognitive dissonance. The psychic cost of rejecting a policy that promises to make you safer is higher for someone who has already been made to feel deeply unsafe than it is for someone who has not.

The study finds that, among people with no recent experience of crime, extrajudicial killings had no measurable impact on trust in the president one way or the other. Among crime victims, the effect was large, positive, and statistically robust. Political imprisonment, by contrast, dented trust regardless of whether someone had been a crime victim or not. It’s only killing that harnesses the psychology of the traumatised into supporting the apparatus that is bypassing the courts.

While El Salvador is the paper’s opening case study, it’s far from the only one. The authors point to the Philippines under Rodrigo Duterte, whose “war on drugs” killed thousands of alleged drug users and small-time dealers. Many of those killed had no connection to organised crime at all, and were often killed in extrajudicial raids that bore little resemblance to law enforcement. Nonetheless, a 2019 Reuters poll found Filipinos gave the campaign a resounding thumbs up. In Ecuador, President Daniel Noboa has followed Bukele in declaring his own states of emergency against gang violence to considerable public approval. What unites these cases is not the specific target—political dissidents in one context, drug users in another, gang members in a third—but the form the violence takes. Killing, it seems, is oddly good politics.

Interviews gathered by civil society groups from Salvadorean neighbourhoods swept up in Bukele’s crackdown are telling. Residents describe genuine relief at being able to move freely again after years of gang control—“we are happy because we can go wherever we want,” one told a local outlet. Yet those same communities voice far sharper criticism of the mass, arbitrary imprisonment happening alongside the killings: families searching for jailed relatives, complaints about poorly treated detainees, a sense of injustice that people readily articulate about imprisonment even as they stay quiet about, and even approve of, the deaths.

The implications for democracy reach well beyond Latin America’s crime politics. Democratic theory generally assumes that voters are the guardians of rights, and that a government which starts trampling on due process, and, worse, taking lives without trial, will pay an electoral price for it. This assumption is meant to be one of democracy’s core safeguards. [Rights violations](https://iai.tv/video/the-power-and-politics-of-shame) are supposed to be unpopular, precisely because most citizens, most of the time, benefit from living under a government that is constrained by law.

But this study suggests that the safety mechanism can misfire: if the psychological conditions are right (such as a public being primed by fear of crime and a leader who is willing to present killing as a decisive means of protection), a government can find that violating the most basic human right of all, the right to life, is counterintuitively rewarded at the ballot box. Under these same conditions, comparatively milder tools of repression, like locking people up, are electorally punished. This creates a perverse incentive structure, where if a leader wants to both look tough and to keep public trust high, the data suggest killing to be a more efficient tool than imprisonment, at least in the short term.

None of this means citizens in El Salvador, the Philippines, or Ecuador are indifferent to human suffering, or secretly cheering executions. The mechanism the researchers describe isn’t cruelty so much as a coping strategy, the mind’s way of living with something otherwise unbearable, aimed at a government that cannot easily be dislodged or appealed to. But the political consequences are unsettling. [Voters are not the guardians of rights that democratic theory believed them to be](https://iai.tv/video/the-iron-fist-vs-the-invisible-hand), and once a leader discovers that lethal repression is a winning strategy rather than a losing one, there is little reason to expect them to give it up.
