# Putin, Xi, and the mistaken quest for immortality

*The dangers of eternal life*

**By Andy Owen** | *9th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,252 words*

*After a hot-mic caught Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin discussing organ transplants and even immortality last week, the two leaders joined the long list, stretching back through history, of those in search of eternal life. An immortal dictator would have overcome their last political opponent – time itself. But author and former intelligence officer Andy Owen argues the denial of death can only lead to more suffering, the loss of meaning and the self, and the loss of all that is truly valuable in life. The quest for eternal life is a dangerous mistake.*

While new technologies designed to end life on a massive scale rolled past them, Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin were [overheard in Beijing last week discussing organ transplants as a means of prolonging life](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cr70rvrd41ko). According to comments caught on a hot mic, Putin suggested eternal life could be achievable as a result of innovations in biotechnology. Xi's translator was heard saying in Russian: “In the past, it used to be rare for someone to be older than 70, and these days they say that at 70 one's still a child.” Putin’s Mandarin translator was heard translating: “With the development of biotechnology, human organs can be continuously transplanted, and people can live younger and younger, and even achieve immortality.”

[Putin and Xi have been in power for 25 and 13 years respectively](http://iai.tv/articles/the-banality-of-putin-and-xi-auid-2158). Neither has given any indication of a desire to step down. Both autocrats are 72 years old. They also share a belief in their respective destinies to lead their ancient civilisational powers to new glories and have tight control over the political apparatus in their countries to enable them to be around to do so. The one thing left for them to control is time itself, or, perhaps more feasibly, the aging process. They are not the first powerful leaders to search for eternal life.

One of the earliest attempts at immortality we have a record of is by Gilgamesh, the Mesopotamian king of the city-state Uruk. [*The Epic of Gilgamesh*](http://www.ancienttexts.org/library/mesopotamian/gilgamesh/), whose earliest components can be traced back to Sumerian poems from around 2100 BCE, details his search for everlasting life. After the death of his companion, Enkidu, Gilgamesh becomes determined to avoid the same fate. In the epic’s conclusion, after failing two tests to gain literal immortality, he realizes instead that mortals can attain immortality through lasting works of civilization and culture. This ancient insight is found in the later theories of the cultural anthropologist Ernest Becker, outlined in his 1973 book [*The Denial of Death*](https://www.ernestbecker.org/lecture-texts/practice-of-dying). Becker believed that human activity is largely driven by unconscious efforts to deny the inevitability of our demise. We invest in activities, institutions, and belief systems that we think will allow us to transcend our brief time in the world.

Before Xi, Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of China (259 BC–210 BC), sought immortality in his old age. Twice, he sent hundreds of people under the direction of Xu Fu to find the legendary elixir of life, but to no avail. Providing a warning to those today seeking out extreme and scientifically questionable medical elixirs, Qin Shi Huang allegedly died of mercury poisoning from mercury pills, prescribed by his court doctors to indefinitely extend his life. He was not the only emperor to die of elixir poisoning. In the sixteenth century, Emperor Zhu Houcong fixated on a concoction called “red lead” made from alchemical ingredients that included the menstrual blood of malnourished teen virgins. His combination of physical and sexual abuse of women led to a failed attempt to kill him by over a dozen of the women he held in slavery, but, in the end, it was his immortality potions that likely led to his death.

Before Putin, there were the Russian “[God-builders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God-Building).” In his book [*The Immortalization Commission*,](https://archive.org/details/immortalizationc0000gray_x0m8) John Gray tells us the story of the curious body that gives his book its title, set up to not only preserve Lenin’s corpse, but to ensure that one day he would be brought back from the dead. Russian Orthodox Christian philosopher, religious thinker, and futurologist Nikolai Federov claimed: “Our duty, our task, consists in bringing back to life all who have died.” After his death, instead of returning from the dead, in 1930, as a part of the Soviet Desecration of Graves, Federov’s grave and all other graves in the monastery where he was buried were destroyed by the Soviet Union. Gray concludes that the Bolshevik project, therefore, was to abolish death, not only in the future but also in the past. In their utopian quest to create a new society that would be a heaven on earth and develop the power to bring the dead back to life, the leaders of the revolution secularized religion’s comfort of immortality through salvation and the afterlife, and used this to justify in their own conscience the murder of unnumbered millions.

\_\_\_

This ancient insight is found in the later theories of the cultural anthropologist Ernest Becker, outlined in his 1973 book The Denial of Death. Becker believed that human activity is largely driven by unconscious efforts to deny the inevitability of our demise.

\_\_\_

The first part of Gray’s book concerns the lives and activities of members of the British Edwardian and Victorian elite who, through the use of spiritualists, mediums, automatic writing, and other forms of occultism, sought to prove it was possible to survive death and communicate back from the beyond. Gray traces the involvement of these individuals (which included former Prime Minister Arthur Balfour and Sherlock Holmes author Sir Arthur Conan Doyle), many of whom were tormented by grief and loss in their personal lives and sought solace in contemplating a happy afterlife and reconnection with lost loved ones. Physically linking these two worlds at the heart of an intricate web of connections was the affair between the English writer HG Wells, who began as a utopian Darwinist before becoming increasingly disenchanted with the future, and the Russian siren Moura Budberg, the third wife of Russian writer and friend of Lenin Maxim Gorky. Gray links the activities of the two groups philosophically through the impulses in humanity's reluctance to accept its own tragic finitude.

[Today in Silicon Valley](http://iai.tv/articles/silicon-valleys-quest-for-immortality-is-a-mistake-auid-2945), there is an increasing number of the wealthiest builders of the new gods of technology who suffer from the same reluctance. When rich Americans seeking immortality previously failed in their quest, they turned to cryogenics to freeze their physical body, awaiting future medical advances that could reanimate them. Now, assuming that consciousness depends only on the organisation of a system and does not depend on the substrate, wealthy techno-immoralists are looking at [preserving their mind by uploading it](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-cant-be-uploaded-auid-3352) and converting it to silicon, ensuring an afterlife in the digital clouds rather than beyond the clouds.

The most basic version of these already exists in the form of Griefbots, an emerging technology designed to mimic [deceased individuals’ speech, behaviors, and personalities](http://iai.tv/articles/the-dangers-of-digital-immortality-auid-2881). Powered by increasingly sophisticated artificial intelligence, they are trained on data such as text messages, social media posts and recorded conversations of the deceased, and benefit from technology used in the latest large language models. An algorithm is created that can answer you as if you were speaking with your missed loved one.

Hope still remains that we can preserve our biological selves, too. Jeff Bezos and Peter Thiel were both early investors in Unity Biotechnology, a company devoted to developing therapeutics to slow or reverse diseases associated with aging. The centimillionaire tech entrepreneur Bryan Johnson told TIME Magazine that he is working on what he calls “the most significant revolution in the history of Homo sapiens.” Over the last few years, he has spent more than $4 million developing a life-extension system called Blueprint, in which he outsources every decision involving his body to a team of doctors. They use data to develop a strict regime to reduce what Johnson calls his “biological age.” This includes downing 111 pills every day, wearing a baseball cap that shoots red light into his scalp, collecting his own stool samples, and sleeping with a tiny jet pack attached to his penis to monitor his nighttime erections. Believing [death is optional](http://iai.tv/video/living-forever), he is attempting to turn his body over to an anti-aging algorithm. The goal is to get his 46-year-old organs to look and act like 18-year-old organs. He claims his experiment is “reframing what it means to be human.” You too can now buy some of the supplements he takes from his website, that, next to a picture of a pale-looking Johnson, claims he is “the healthiest person on the planet.”

Putin would like to use the latest in scientific research to be able to claim the same. However, due to the increasingly debilitated scientific institutions within Russia, as many scientists have sought to further their careers and lives abroad, Putin has resorted to placing his faith in men like Mikhail Kovalchuk, the brother of a close ally in prominent scientific positions, with questionable credentials. Kovalchuk, who was rejected for higher offices by the Russian Academy of Sciences (which led to Putin further undermining the institution), claims that nanorobots could make people immortal and fears that Americans are stealing Russians’ precious bodily fluids.

Putin and Johnson assume immortality is desirable (Xi, who Taiwanese intelligence believe secretly smokes behind his wife’s back, may be less literal and, like Gilgamesh, be aiming for immortality through his achievements, such as a future reunification with Taiwan). Philosopher Shelly Ian Kagan disagrees, arguing that any form of immortality would be undesirable, based on the dichotomy that either our characters remain essentially the same in an immortal afterlife, and therefore over an infinite stretch of time, with the same desires and interests we would get bored and find eternal life unbearably tedious—or they do not, in which case our characters would over such a time period become so radically changed that such a future version of you would be too different from your current self for you to care much what happens to them. As American writer Saul Bellow claimed in his novel [*Humboldt’s Gift*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/353233/humboldts-gift-by-saul-bellow/), “Death is the dark backing that a mirror needs if we are to see anything.” Our awareness of our mortality and our fear of the fleetingness of human life shape so much of how we see ourselves and how we see others, forging our psychology, our relationships, and our values. It is our defining human characteristic. To become immortal would be to cease being human.

Whilst the search for immortality remains as chimerical as Qin Shi Huang’s vain quest for elixir, any significant progress in extending life will likely come at great cost and only be available to the few. There are already significant gaps in life expectancy between the world’s richest and poorest. Extending this gap would create serious ethical and political dilemmas at a time when power is becoming more concentrated. Would a leader with a lifespan twice that of their people be content with a fixed term of rule that was just a tiny sliver of their extended life? How would they value the shorter lives of their citizens whose time would pass at a rate that would become hard for them to imagine?

Putin and Xi’s comments are part of a long and ongoing history of secular attempts to solve enduring human problems that we used to look to religious beliefs to solve. They will fail as others have done, and all they have built and clung onto will eventually pass. Neither religion nor science can ultimately solve the human condition without changing it to the point where we are no longer recognizable. [We are told in the Psalms](https://www.bible.com/bible/compare/PSA.103.15-18) that, “As for man, his days are like grass; he flourishes like a flower of the field; the wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place knows it no more,” yet we still refuse to accept this is the case. Time and again, our efforts to achieve everlasting life only result in the shortening of life, our own or others.

As Gray concludes in the final lines of his book, “Immortality is only the dimming soul projected onto a blank screen. There is more sunshine in the fall of a leaf.” That none of this lasts forever should be a source of beauty and wonder, not fear. The feeling of sun of your face, the first sip of a good wine, the live performance that will never happen in that exact same way ever again, the feel of the warmth and fragility of another’s skin, the unexpected recall of a happy memory of a time you can never return to, the first time, the last time, growing old together, the changing of the seasons, the letting go of the child who is a child no more—it is such sensations and processes of life that, through their impermanence and irreversible progression, make it worth living.
