# Beauty privilege and the shame that keeps us quiet

*Beautiful people earn more, but no one calls out this discrimination*

**By Heather Widdows** | *13th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,603 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Beauty privilege and the shame that keeps us quiet](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/beauty-privilege-and-the-shame-that-keeps-us-quiet.webp)

---

*Attractive people get better jobs, better pay, and better treatment. Lookism is basically the ugly sister of racism, sexism, and homophobia, yet hardly anyone fights it. Philosopher **Heather Widdows** here argues that shame is the reason. Being unattractive stings our own pride or has too much baggage to allow as to defend others, so we keep quiet. Until we move past this shame, lookism will continue to run rampant.*

Whether we call it lookism, appearance discrimination, the halo effect, or pretty privilege, lookism is real. Good-looking people are more likely to be employed, earn more, are treated better by teachers and other professionals, and are thought to be kinder and more competent. But, although we’ve known this for decades, we’ve done little about it. Why is lookism ignored when other forms of discrimination are taken seriously in policy, practice, and everyday life? I consider three possible explanations. First, that it’s natural to treat good-looking people better; second, that appearance is trivial; and third, that shame stops us from recognizing lookism. I reject the first two, accept the third, and consider how to address it.

First, what exactly is lookism? Lookism, pretty privilege and the halo effect name the fact that attractive people get treated better than the unattractive. Lookism, like its sister terms sexism and racism, calls out unjust discrimination, not just different treatment. Just as it isn’t racism to test for sickle cell disease in black populations, it isn’t lookism to put height restrictions on jockeys, or weight restrictions on boxers.

\_\_\_

Who is going to call out lookist behavior if you have to admit to being ugly?

\_\_\_

Study after study shows that lookism is prevalent and impactful. Unattractive people are [less likely to be selected for interview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176512005599), [less likely to be appointed to positions](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1890/), and [paid less](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/209886). Famously, Daniel Hamermesh in his book [*Beauty Pays,*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158174/beauty-pays?srsltid=AYCX9vKzpfpJVmEg2UYwc2PlgWLKQqyeiqAMgjObYS0Z_k9z8RMRR4gg)identified “a beauty premium” and an “ugly penalty.” More recently, a [large-scale study based on data from 119 669 UK Biobank participants](https://www.bmj.com/content/352/bmj.i582) found that tall men and slim women are paid more than their shorter and fatter peers. The headlines reported men getting £1600 a year for every 2.5 inches, women losing £1500 a year for every stone. [In philosopher Andrew Mason’s words](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/whats-wrong-with-lookism-9780192859792?cc=gb&lang=en&), lookism “in employment seems to be as pervasive as other forms of discrimination, including racial discrimination, and its effect on earnings seems to be just as pronounced.”

And it’s not just the workplace. Teachers [treat attractive students better](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0033-2909.126.3.390), [think they are cleverer](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543062004413) and [have more friends](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0033-2909.126.3.390). Good-looking criminals [are less likely to be found guilty](https://www.sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/540) and [are given lighter punishments](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00224545.1985.9922900). Remember #FreeLuigi, the campaign around the “hot assassin” Luigi Mangione who killed Brian Thompson, the CEO of UnitedHealthcare? Lookism is real, it happens all the time, and we’ve known this for decades. Why, when other forms of discrimination are taken seriously, do we ignore lookism?

One reason we ignore and accept lookism is thinking it is natural. Psychologist Nancy Etcoff [makes this argument:](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/47150/survival-of-the-prettiest-by-nancy-etcoff/)

*Our extreme sensitivity to beauty is hard-wired, that is, governed by circuits in our brain shaped by natural selection. We love to look at smooth skin, thick shiny hair, curved waists, and symmetrical bodies because in the course of evolution the people who notice these signals and desired their possessors had more reproductive success. We are their descendants.*

But does it matter that something is hard-wired? Human beings are profoundly social animals. We don’t leave children to grow up naturally, if that means without guidance. Imagine how it would be if we did: we teach children how to behave, so that they fit in, so they conform to shared values, so they can cope with life. Imagine applying the claim to other forms of discrimination, arguing that racism or sexism are natural and therefore should be ignored or put up with. At times, these arguments have been made. For example, many religions have deemed males naturally superior, and [Darwinism was once used to support racial hierarchies](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0013124510380723). But these, most of us agree, are not good arguments. Simply claiming something is natural is not a reason to shrug and accept there is nothing to be done.

A second reason for ignoring lookism is because we don’t believe appearance matters or we don’t think it should. For example, some people might deny lookism is real, despite the data, and think they are “looks blind.” This is unlikely. What people believe about their own prejudice is often wrong. Implicit bias is well documented and widespread, with early work focusing on racism and sexism. Such bias is unconscious, automatic and involuntary, operating below the level of conscious awareness. Significantly, “[the theorized ordinariness of implicit stereotyping is consistent with recent findings of discrimination by people who explicitly disavow prejudice](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0033-295X.102.1.4).” Thinking it doesn’t exist is a poor reason for ignoring lookism.

Alternatively, you might know lookism exists, but deliberately ignore it. You might think that taking lookism seriously risks making appearance even more valuable. Whether this is true is questionable, and we don’t have the data to know. But the data does show that ignoring lookism does not stop appearance from mattering. The annual [*Girls Attitude Survey*](https://www.girlguiding.org.uk/globalassets/docs-and-resources/research-and-campaigns/girls-attitudes-survey-2024.pdf) has repeatedly reported that [pressures to look perfect](https://iai.tv/articles/were-risking-it-all-for-beauty-auid-3495) are huge, causing 80% of young women anxiety. And it’s not just young girls. [A third of adults in the UK report feeling anxious or depressed because of their body image and one in eight have experienced suicidal thoughts](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/our-work/research/body-image-how-we-think-and-feel-about-our-bodies). If this is the reason, it’s not working.

The most important reason for ignoring lookism is how it makes us feel. Who is going to call out lookist behavior if you have to admit to being ugly? The victim of lookism does not feel anger but shame. Shame is ubiquitous in beauty discourse and practice. You can be shamed for anything, for being too fat or too thin, for wrinkles, cellulite, body hair, bingo wings, cankles, and the list goes on. Consider some of the [Everyday Lookism](https://www.heatherwiddows.com/everydaylookism.html) stories:

*Whilst having my legs waxed, the beautician attempted to shame me into waxing everything else too. Left feeling disgusted with my body and ashamed about my decision not to remove more hair.*

*My friends called me “finger toes” when I wore sandals to an evening out. I never buy or wear open shoes because of that and it was 15 years ago.*

*I had a “friend” tell me I looked like a stuffed sausage in a pair of size 28 jean shorts. The summer I only ate cucumbers, watermelon and SlimFast bars. I was 5’2 and 122lbs, and 15 years old. 17 years later I still haven’t forgotten, I can still hear the words, I remember exactly what I was wearing. I never wore shorts again.*

*As a teenager, perhaps 12 or 13, a boy pointed at my bare legs and laughed at how hairy they were. Everyone around me joined him laughing at me. I felt utterly ashamed. I had up until this point not even noticed. That was the last time… I’ve probably thought about my body hair every day for 20 years.*

Shame makes us blush, avert our gaze, shrink and compress our bodies, and is “[consistent with wanting to disappear or to hide](https://parents.simonandschuster.com/9780684823119).” Shame is a very powerful way to ensure we ignore lookism. Changing this requires changing how we feel. We have done this with other forms of discrimination—remember we used to think sexism and racism were natural.

Consider one example: sexual harassment. Women used to feel shame: Was it my fault? Did I lead him on? What was I wearing? Why me? Over time, women gradually felt less ashamed of themselves, their clothes and their behavior. Shame now—mostly—attaches to the perpetrator, not the victim. This is shown by how willing women are to share their #everydaysexism stories and [their #MeToo experiences](https://iai.tv/articles/weinstein-westminster-and-philosophy-structures-of-abuse-auid-978) openly and in their own names. Women do not feel shame but rage. As Sarah Brown writes in the foreword of [*Everyday Sexism*](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250100184/everydaysexism/): “This book provides plenty of stories and evidence that will shock and enrage you. But it will also, I hope, reaffirm your belief that change can happen and we can win if we try.” A similar change is needed if lookism is to be addressed.

[Change like this is collective](https://iai.tv/video/the-politics-of-beauty). As individuals, we can’t magically change how we feel. But we can start talking about lookism, taking it seriously, sharing our lookist stories, and calling people out for being lookist. When schools take lookism seriously, change happens quickly. Imagine if we all started sharing lookist stories and pointing fingers at body shamers? It wouldn’t end lookism; sadly, naming sexual harassment has not ended it, but gradually it would change how we feel and make it shameful to be overtly lookist. Next time you see body shaming, name it lookist, name it discrimination. You might find your anger awakened, and a passion to address lookism stirring. Each story is a drop in the ocean, but together they are powerful, showing how cruel and wrong lookism is.

Lookism, pretty privilege and the halo effect are real. Lookism means we fail to appoint the most qualified and competent people, fail to teach pupils according to their capacity, and the list goes on. Arguments that we should do nothing about lookism because it is natural don’t stand up to scrutiny. Arguments that we should ignore lookism because appearance is trivial or drawing attention to lookism will make it worse are equally poor. Lookism does come with shame, but, just as we’ve changed how we feel about other forms of discrimination, so we can with lookism. We should talk about lookism and start to address it!
