# Trump, China, and the end of Western dominance

*The rise of Authoritarian Welfarism*

**By Rana Mitter** | *13th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,714 words*

![Trump, China, and the end of Western dominance](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Trump-China-and-the-end-of-Western-dominance.webp)

---

*Some are arguing that with the US-China tariff deal now in place, this is a victory for Trump. But Harvard China specialist, Rana Mitter, warns this is not necessarily the case. Instead, we will move to a multipolar world. Mitter warns that while the deal may have shifted trade dynamics, relations in both Europe and Asia have been further strained. Mitter argues that we are witnessing a shift toward a world where the US is just as vulnerable to retaliatory economic threats as China.*

Washington and Beijing seemed locked in an ideological duel. [Pundits spoke of a new Cold War](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/americas/us-politics/trump-china-tariffs-cold-war-b2736957.html). Tariffs piled up, diplomatic barbs flew, and each side cast the other as a civilizational adversary. And yet, more recently, a subtle shift has been underway. The United States and China are gingerly t[ransitioning from open conflict toward a mode of cautious pragmatism](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/05/12/business/china-us-tariffs.html), the uneasy coexistence that has, in truth, underpinned much of their relationship over the past thirty years. Just yesterday, the two powers stepped back from the Trump-initiated trade war, agreeing to a temporary tariff truce that slashed reciprocal duties by hundreds of percentage points.

This breakthrough caught many by surprise, but not Rana Mitter. The Harvard historian of modern China, in an interview conducted last Friday for the [HowTheLightGetsIn festival](http://howthelightgetsin.org/), anticipated exactly this turn of events, though as he admits, not at this speed. Mitter suggested that despite deep ideological rifts, both nations would eventually recognise the need to live with each other, not as friends, but as functional rivals. His insight now appears prescient. If diplomacy is war by other means, recent US-China policy resembles strategic sulking followed by hurried compromise.

**War Memories and Worldviews**

To understand China’s current posture, Mitter urges us to look backward. Historical memory, particularly of World War II, looms large in Beijing’s worldview. “China was also a participant in World War Two,” he notes, suffering millions of casualties and tens of millions of refugees. These experiences form a proud and traumatic national narrative that still shapes China’s foreign policy. On May 9th, in Moscow’s Red Square, [Xi Jinping](http://iai.tv/articles/understanding-china-means-understanding-xi-steve-tsang-auid-2736) stood shoulder-to-shoulder with Vladimir Putin, reviewing troops in commemoration of WWII’s end. This spectacle, as Mitter observes, was “pushing back against the idea that somehow the [US could pick Russia away from China](http://iai.tv/articles/how-the-west-got-russia-and-china-wrong-auid-2094),” asserting Beijing’s role as both victor and victim in the war, never a mere junior partner in a Western narrative.

Mitter explains that [the memory of WWII in China and Russia](https://www.reuters.com/world/russia-china-mark-victory-ww2-ukraine-war-grinds-2025-05-08/) acts as “a profound counterargument” to the dominant Western framing of the war. While the West sees WWII as a fight for democracy and freedom, the Chinese and Russian narratives cast it as “a war of liberation against invasion,” with little emphasis on liberal ideology. The ideological mission of spreading liberal democracy abroad never shaped Beijing’s understanding of its wartime legacy. “The war experience has a very different sort of meaning,” Mitter concludes.

That historical consciousness still informs Beijing’s view of the US. Chinese leaders carry a deep sense of grievance from the “[Century of Humiliation](https://www.asianstudies.org/publications/eaa/archives/the-national-humiliation-narrative-dealing-with-the-present-by-fixating-on-the-past/)” and pride in their wartime role. They bristle at lectures on democracy and human rights, hearing echoes of historical condescension. As Mitter suggests, each side proceeds from a fundamentally different historical mindset: America seeks to uphold a liberal order; China seeks to avoid subjugation and assert its own narrative. These irreconcilable memories fuel an enduring ideological divide.

**From Trade War to Trade-Offs**

If ideological dissonance set the stage, trade became the battlefield. Under the Trump administration, Washington has launched a tariff offensive that plunged relations into crisis. Tariffs peaked at 145% on Chinese goods, gouging consumers and exporters alike. [Nearly $600 billion in two-way trade froze](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/us-china-reach-deal-slash-tariffs-officials-say-2025-05-12/). For a time, decoupling seemed inevitable.

But reality intervened. “[W]e’ve pulled back from the brink,” according to Mitter. Both sides found themselves in “a new and in some ways quite difficult situation,” and had to “navigate it very carefully.”

Rather than seeking total decoupling, [Trump’s Washington pivoted toward a transactional, short-term strategy](http://iai.tv/articles/trump-tariffs-and-the-lessons-to-learn-from-machiavelli-auid-3134). “In the case of the US,” Mitter observed, “it’s becoming clearer that the real aim [of US tariffs] is to get a large number of bilateral deals.” Vietnam, initially hit with steep tariffs, entered “emergency negotiations” and “appears likely to yield a result that will suit both sides.” The UK too was among the first to strike a deal. This wasn’t a grand realignment but a patchwork effort to salvage growth amid chaos. Call it free trade, call it friend-shoring, or just call it what it is: economic speed-dating with legally binding consequences.

China, meanwhile, looked to diversify trade ties in ASEAN, Europe, and the Global South. But these efforts met limits. “Many of the ASEAN countries already have China as their major trading partner,” Mitter notes, “and they’re not keen to get even more dependent.” Europe remained cool, wary of both Beijing’s leverage and Washington’s favour. Worse still, China’s reputation as an “unreliable trade partner” haunted its diplomacy. Australia’s wine exports, mysteriously held up after political disagreements, served as a warning: Beijing, like Washington, weaponises trade.

Ironically, both superpowers began to resemble each other. “People [said] that’s what the United States is doing now,” Mitter remarked, as Washington mimics the coercive economic tactics it once decried. “It just puts the US and China on a kind of even keel with their other trading partners.”

The recent 90-day tariff pause signals reluctant compromise. Both sides want to claim victory; both need to keep trading. Mitter’s analysis, “international trade is what grows economies”, underscores the inconvenient truth: pragmatism trumps ideology when the bills come due.

**Taiwan: Flashpoint in a Fragile Equilibrium**

Taiwan remains the most volatile front in US-China relations. It is the one issue that could turn cold rivalry into open war. And yet, as Mitter notes, it is also the area most tightly bound by continuity and caution.

Despite mounting tension, military drills, US visits to Taipei, and bellicose rhetoric, Mitter advises observers “not to over-read some of the signs that we’ve seen.” [The policy architecture remains intact.](http://iai.tv/articles/war-in-taiwan-more-shocking-ukraine-nigel-ingster-auid-2202) Strategic ambiguity endures, perhaps the only doctrine where vagueness is a feature, not a bug. “Most of the people… tasked with seeing that the status quo of Taiwan doesn’t change have made it very clear that they think maintaining that status quo is an important core task.”

China, for its part, continues to speak of reunification. But it knows that military action remains “a gamble.” Economic coercion and political influence offer less risky paths. So long as American resolve appears credible, Beijing is unlikely to act rashly.

This is the paradox of Taiwan: the more precarious the situation appears, the more carefully both sides tread. Mitter captures this delicate dance with his characteristic restraint: the next 5-10 years will bring friction, but “outright war can be avoided.”

**Imagining a Liberalising China**

One of Mitter’s more cautious speculations concerns the idea that China could become more open—if not democratic, then at least less closed. In *Foreign Affairs*, [he writes of a future where China remains authoritarian but develops more space for dissent and debate](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/once-and-future-china-xi-jinping-rana-mitter). “Becoming more liberal within their authoritarian system,” he notes, “is something that’s happened before.”

This is not starry-eyed idealism. Mitter stresses that full democratisation is “very unlikely.” Instead, he envisions a China that moderates its internal rigidity, not out of benevolence, but pragmatism.

Cultural and educational exchanges, he argues, are essential. “Only around 500 students from the US are in China at the moment. That’s far too low a number.”

But the political model Mitter describes, sometimes dubbed "authoritarian welfarism", must not be seen as benign or neutral. It risks becoming the international standard-bearer for a form of governance that fuses repression with populist promise. It is a model that appeals to regimes both left and right: technocratic in delivery, paternalistic in tone, and often brutal in dissent suppression. The fact that it could be exported, as liberal democracy once was, is not cause for comfort. It is cause for urgent attention.

**A Post-Hegemonic Order?**

Finally, as I asked Mitter to reflect on whether this moment is a flash point for future historians. He challenged us to reframe our understanding of the current moment. “We may well mark a moment at which we move away from a world in which there’s a liberal hegemon,” he says. For 75 years, the US. set global norms. That era is fading.

Now, “great powers compete… for maximisation of power.” But power means many things: “not just military power… it can mean economic power… affinities and values.” Liberal democracy, Mitter suggests, is no longer the default setting.

The Chinese model, centralised control with selective liberalisation, has appeal in parts of the world disillusioned with Western democratic dysfunction. Mitter notes that “much of the wider world is turning in that direction.” The rise of authoritarian welfarism offers a challenge to the liberal order, not in the name of revolution, but of stability and control.

The US, if it wants to preserve influence, must demonstrate that its model can still deliver security and dignity. China, if it wants to rise peacefully, must prove that its ascent won’t become a template for repression.

We are entering an age of pluralism, not harmony, but unhappy coexistence. The test is whether these clashing systems can share a planet without burning it down. Rana Mitter doesn’t offer guarantees. But he does suggest that history may look back on this moment as the time when we learned, at last, how to live with difference. Global order may no longer have a conductor, but at least the orchestra hasn’t set fire to the stage... yet.
