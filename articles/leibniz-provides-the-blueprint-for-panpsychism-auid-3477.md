# Leibniz provides the blueprint for panpsychism

*Reality is made of solitary islands of consciousness*

**By S. Siddharth** | *27th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,791 words*

![Leibniz provides the blueprint for panpsychism](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/leibniz-monads.webp)

---

*The panpsychist claim that reality's fundamental constituents are conscious is increasingly popular, but it faces an unanswerable question: how can conscious fragments fuse to make up complex minds? Philosopher **S. Siddharth** argues that the solution requires venturing where those sympathetic to panpsychism, from William James to David Chalmers, have feared to tread: the weird metaphysics of German philosopher Gottfried Leibniz. Reviving Leibniz’s “monads,” Siddharth envisages a reality composed of indivisible centers of experience, which neither fuse nor split, but are rigorously singular, sidestepping the question that plagues popular versions of panpsychism.*

Consciousness is at once what we are most familiar with, and a persistent mystery. The endurance of the [mind-body problem](https://iai.tv/video/the-mind-body-problem-and-animal-consciousness-with-peter-godfrey-smith) across time and cultures, and the [wide variety of contemporary philosophical theories of consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/no-theory-of-consciousness-is-scientific-auid-2610), are a testament to this. Some say [consciousness is an illusion](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/imp/jcs/2016/00000023/f0020011/art00005), others say [it is merely brain processes](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1956.tb00560.x). Still others think of it as [abstract functions implemented by the brain](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4319824); as something that [emerges](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-emergence) from the complexity of brain processes; as a [global workspace](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-6123(05)50004-9); as integrated information; as a substance distinct from the body; and so on. Amidst these, a view of ancient vintage has regained prominence in recent decades within analytic philosophy of mind—[*panpsychism*](https://iai.tv/articles/conscious-universe-panpsychism-idealism-goff-kastrup-auid-1584?_auid=2020), the view that consciousness is both [fundamental](https://iai.tv/articles/annaka-harris-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3136) and ubiquitous.

A major motivation for contemporary panpsychism has been dissatisfaction with both physicalism and dualism. Equally important have been the insights of the British philosopher Bertrand Russell and other philosophers of science who showed that the sciences are successful precisely because they limit themselves to delivering a certain kind of knowledge of the physical world—its *structural* properties—without concerning themselves with the stuff that implements this structure—its *intrinsic* properties. Now, if our knowledge of the physical world known through the sciences is limited to its structure, and if consciousness cannot be reduced to mere structures of fundamentally non-conscious-physical stuff, couldn’t it be that consciousness (or something like it) is the non-structural, intrinsic aspect of physical stuff? Panpsychists think that this likely is the case.

\_\_\_

Subjects, as the American philosopher William James famously noted, seem to be the “most absolute breaches in nature,” not the sort of thing that can combine.

\_\_\_

The resulting view, while elegant in integrating the advantages of both physicalism and dualism, is weird to say the least. It entails that consciousness is everywhere: if fundamental physical entities are all intrinsically conscious, every physical entity is constituted of conscious entities. This is not the same as saying that *every* physical thing, including rocks and spoons, is conscious—no contemporary panpsychist seriously thinks that it feels some way to be a spoon—but only that the fundamental entities that constitute all physical entities, including rocks, spoons and human beings, are each conscious *subjects-having-experiences*.

This makes the job of accommodating consciousness in a physical world easier, but only just. Critics of panpsychism contend that while panpsychism tells us that fundamental physical entities—quarks, strings, electrons, whatever our best physical theories tell us are fundamental—are conscious, it does not tell us how these micro-conscious entities combine to form the consciousness of macro-entities such as human beings. Indeed, it seems impossible that micro-subjects-having-experience can combine at all. Subjects, as the American philosopher William James famously noted, seem to be the “most absolute breaches in nature,” not the sort of thing that can combine. Given this, panpsychism is no better than physicalism in explaining the kind of consciousness that needs to be explained—the complex, human experiences of the sort we are intimately familiar with.

Faced with this [*combination problem*](https://books.google.co.uk/books?hl=en&lr=&id=VzrKDAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA179&dq=combination+problem+for+panpsychism&ots=yWH-oqOOXY&sig=mhaVWIvT_fsjxxzXqQYjl1FIGuE&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q&f=false), many panpsychists look to invert the structure of fundamentality—perhaps it is the cosmos as a whole that is the fundamental conscious entity, with everything else including human consciousness deriving from it. Does such a view—[*cosmopsychism*](https://doi.org/10.1093/monist/onab019)—help us get around the problem of combination of subjects-having-experience? I am not so sure. After all, if the combination of subjects into bigger subjects is impossible, so would the de-combination of the cosmic subject into smaller ones be. Any solution to the problem would surely have to do away with both combination and de-combination of subjects.

Independent of consciousness and panpsychism, some metaphysicians hold that combination never occurs, and composite entities don’t really exist. This view—[*mereological nihilism*](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Material_Beings.html?id=mE38_qz5BUAC&redir_esc=y)*—*paints a picture of reality populated by mereological *simples*, or entities with no further parts. Macro-objects, such as chairs, rocks and planets, are just simples arranged *chairwise*, *rockwise* and *planetwise*, that appear to us as single, unified individuals. Faced with the combination/de-combination problem, I think that adopting mereological nihilism seems to be a panpsychist’s best bet.

The marriage of panpsychism and mereological nihilism can give rise to two kinds of views. First, one can hold that the cosmos-as-a-whole is a conscious entity and the only real individual, with everything else, including human selves, somehow subsumed in this single individual. Or one can hold that there are zillions and zillions of microphysical particles—quarks, strings etc.—each a conscious subject-having-experience. The former option has its defenders, including those propounding certain forms of [idealism](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-not-what-it-seems-auid-2312). I myself am sympathetic to this option, and have [defended a version](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0034412525100838) of it inspired by the views of the eleventh-century Indian philosopher Ramanuja. Nevertheless, I think that the latter view is equally possible and something that hardly gets considered in contemporary discussions.

There is some support from illustrious figures in history, though. [Gottfried Leibniz](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/leibniz/), the seventeenth-century German philosopher and mathematician, famously propounded such a view. In his *Monadology*, he contended that reality consists of conscious monads—similar to microphysical particles—that never combine or even interact with each other—*windowless*, as he called them. He further thought that there is one monad within each human body that was “dominant” and constituted that human’s self.

Not everything Leibniz says seems agreeable to us today. One could agree with him that conscious simples never combine. The idea of a dominant monad, on the other hand, seems problematic (though it has its [supporters in contemporary panpsychism](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-023-04464-0)). If one also holds (as do so-called [Russellian panpsychists](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-024-00877-6)) that these conscious simples instantiate the structure known to us through the various sciences, one cannot also hold that any one subject-having-experience, that is also a fundamental particle among the zillions in their body (or even a particular region of their brain), is somehow privileged or dominant. More importantly, we cannot assume that these simples are “windowless.” Fundamental particles interact with each other in unimaginably complex ways. The structure of these interactions is captured, often in the language of mathematics, by our physical sciences. As we shall see, allowing for “windows” works in the view’s favor in other ways.

In addition to its obvious weirdness, such a *Russellian-Leibnizian* view would likely face multiple objections. First, it could be objected that the view cannot explain why the experiences of some physical simples—the ones we call human selves—are much more complex than simples present in a rock or a table; after all, physics tells us that any two fundamental particles (say electrons) are quite similar to each other in their properties. What then explains the difference in the complexity of their experiences?

It is true that any two electrons are similar in their monadic properties (such as charge, spin etc.), but they differ in the relations they are enmeshed in. For example, an electron that is part of my brain is enmeshed in far more complex causal relations than an electron that is in a table. [In my research](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/imp/jcs/2024/00000031/f0020011/art00009;jsessionid=34nei09d1vil1.x-ic-live-02), I contend that it is the complexity of the relations that a simple is party to—its causal neighborhood—that explains the complexity of a simple’s experiences, not just its properties. This allows us to explain why a simple that is in my brain experiences the kind of complex experiential content that I do, while a simple in a rock likely has a much more boring phenomenology.

\_\_\_

Russellian-Leibnizian panpsychism is a strange and counterintuitive view, to say the least. Sometimes, when I am half-awake in the mornings, I wonder if this really is the view I have defended on record.

\_\_\_

Such a Russellian-Leibnizian view also has the bizarre implication that there are an unimaginably high number of simples—and subjects—in my head that have experiences similar to what I call “my experience.” After all, any small region of the brain has zillions of fundamental particles in similar causal neighborhoods. We have no reason to think one among them is somehow dominant, or is the one that is my-self or the real “me.” Rather, it is these zillions of simples, each with complex experiential content similar to “my experience,” which together have the influence on our actions and behavior that we naively attribute to a single subject.

One might wonder if all this is based on a rather naive understanding of fundamental particles as individuals. Such a conception, it could be objected, misses the advancements of physics in the last century and the [field-theoretic understanding of particles](https://iai.tv/video/the-substance-of-the-world). Some philosophers have further contended that findings in quantum physics show that our bottom-up understanding of reality is off the mark; rather, these findings point to a sort of [holism wherein the cosmos-as-a-whole is a composite single entity](https://read.dukeupress.edu/the-philosophical-review/article-abstract/119/1/31/2871/Monism-The-Priority-of-the-Whole?redirectedFrom=fulltext), from which the rest of the universe derives. Indeed, such arguments have also been marshalled by some panpsychists to support top-down cosmopsychist views. Even if one isn’t attracted by the idea of [the cosmos as a single entity](https://doi.org/10.1215/00318108-2009-025), there are other instances in various sciences that seem to posit composites—“species” in biology, “society” in the social sciences etc.—that render mereological nihilism in general untenable.

These objections have some merit. It is true that theories in fundamental physics *seem* to indicate that the universe is one single, interconnected entity, and the various other sciences seem to posit other entities such as regions of the brain, organisms and societies as unified wholes. However, this need not necessarily entail that these are real composite unities. Proponents of mereological nihilism, such as the philosopher Andrew Brenner, have shown that scientific theories that seem to posit composites could have [equivalent mereological-nihilist-friendly articulations that do away with such composites](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-016-1234-6), and that scientists may often not consider such alternatives on account of the convenience of working with composites in their theories. The upshot is, if we have other good metaphysical reasons to reject the reality of composites, composites posited by the various sciences need not deter us from doing so.

Russellian-Leibnizian panpsychism is a strange and counterintuitive view, to say the least. Sometimes, when I am half-awake in the mornings, I wonder if this really is the view I have defended on record. When I am fully awake, though, I remind myself that it does not matter if a view is counterintuitive, least of all when it comes to consciousness. I remind myself that the “hard” problem of consciousness is hard for a reason, and that any true theory of consciousness is [likely to seem weird](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-cosmology-and-the-collapse-of-common-sense-auid-3272). The Russellian-Leibnizian view offers an elegant way around panpsychism’s stiffest challenge. Maybe ours is a world of conscious simples.
