# Mistrust of science isn’t always irrational

*The doctor-patient gap must be closed*

**By Katherine Furman** | *14th October 2024* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,676 words*

![Mistrust of science isn’t always irrational](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Mistrust-of-science-isnt-always-irrational.webp)

---

*The medical establishment often dismisses skepticism of vaccines and treatments as irrational fear. But, as Katherine Furman argues, it can sometimes be rational to trust your friends and neighbours over distant experts – for example, in cases when one lacks information about the aims and values of those experts. Instead of manipulating people into compliance or emphasising the authority of expertise, the real solution lies in bringing scientists and medics closer to the communities they serve.*

**1. Trusting friends and neighbours**

We often share healthcare information and advice with our fellow non-experts. We don’t just talk to our healthcare providers about health; we also talk to our friends, neighbours, and that guy at the coffee shop. A study in France found that mothers who are undecided or uncertain about whether to vaccinate their children would trust a healthcare provider’s advice on vaccination, but only once the provider had been recommended by other women. We value peer-testimony – information we receive from people we judge to be the same as us. And we include that testimony in our medical decision-making.

There are classic examples of relying on those we judge to be similar to ourselves when making healthcare decisions. In the 1970s, a feminist collective in Boston put together the handbook, *Our Bodies, Ourselves*, which provides a collection of information, often anecdotal, on how to navigate the healthcare system. Their message was simple: we cannot rely on the doctors and we need to share this information between ourselves to survive. [*Our Bodies,* Ourselves](https://ourbodiesourselves.org/) has been repeatedly updated and reissued to keep pace with women’s health needs, with the most recent version published in 2011, indicating the longevity of this self-reliance intervention.

There are lots of ways that we share and receive information in our peer networks. We put together and purchase handbooks, like that produced by the Boston Women’s Health Collective. We chat with the baristas in our local coffee shop, like a conversation I overheard this morning between a café owner and a customer about cancer care pathways. We also share rumours.

Rumours closely resemble our casual chatter about shared experience, but they have a more menacing reputation. During World War 2, rumours were so troubling to the powers-that-be that they were deemed a threat to American national security. In philosophy, rumours don’t have a much better reputation. Australian philosopher, CAJ Coady describes rumours as “[pathological testimony](https://academic.oup.com/book/43179/chapter-abstract/362368652?redirectedFrom=fulltext)” – testimony that is diseased. At their core, rumours are just unofficial pieces of peer testimony that travel through peer networks, where their “unofficial” status means that there is nothing to underwrite their credibility. This is in contrast to scientific testimony, which has the whole scientific edifice to support it. Importantly, rumours are a form of *peer* testimony. We typically only share rumours between those we judge to be like ourselves and we don’t share across social boundaries. We share rumours with fellow pub-goers and not with the priest. Today, much of our rumour sharing happens online, where we don’t always know *exactly* with whom we are sharing information, yet we often treat them as people we know well.

Why all the fuss about rumours? Scientific testimony is more likely to get us closer to the truth on topics that matter to us. Science has been produced by people with advanced qualifications, who have access to equipment to perform experiments, and their results go through the rigours of peer review before we hear about them. Surely, in a clash between scientific testimony and rumours, science would win the fight for people’s trust. But it doesn’t always.

Rumours are important in medical spaces because they have the power to undermine public health interventions. In the West African Ebola outbreak (2013–2016) an assortment of rumours inhibited global health containment efforts. Rumours were that medics were stealing the bodies to harvest organs for sale on the global black market; that Ebola was part of a racist plot from the West; and that Ebola was a plot from local governments to take out troublesome political minorities. How do we make sense of the power of rumours, assuming that people are trying to do the best for themselves and their loved ones?

**2. What kind of information are we trying to get from our peers?**

It matters that rumours are a form of *peer* testimony, shared with us by people we might already know and trust. Or in the online case, people who we feel like we know. This gestures to one feature of why rumours gain traction, which is that we are trying to get different information from our friends and from experts. I might turn to science to get truths about the universe, but I turn to my girlfriends for advice on how to do hard things in my everyday life. In the latter case, what I am relying on is that my girlfriends are relevantly similar to me and have useful advice to share. If they have successfully gone through a situation, like giving birth, they have done the experiential homework and it makes sense to consider their advice on what to do. The obstetrician’s life experience might appear too far removed from my own. While obstetricians know true things about the science of delivering babies, they may not know what matters to *me*.

The potential of the obstetrician not knowing what matters to me points to a deeper problem, which is the problem of *value* disagreements between the patient and the medical practitioner. Even deeper, there might be value disagreements between the patient and the underlying science.

**3. The trouble with values**

Philosophers of science are largely in agreement that science is thoroughly value-laden. Values are used to choose which projects to pursue, what methods to use, how to interpret data, how to decide when there is enough data to draw conclusions, and how to communicate those conclusions. Recognising that there are values involved in science does not diminish the sciences. Scientific knowledge is still the best access we have to information on a variety of topics that we care about, like the causes and treatments of diseases, how to build bridges, and how to send videos of our cats with increasing efficiency. Scientists have training to gather the information we need and institutional checks, like peer review, to dampen the effects of any individual scientist’s values on the overall result. There are values in the science, but it is still substantially more likely to get you a better treatment for Covid-19 than your uncle and his homemade remedy.

But the values still pose a worry, which is that the values that have been used might run counter to my own values and might even be harmful to people like me. The tangle between values and facts in the sciences, coupled with the difficulty of assessing scientific claims as a layperson, makes it difficult to check the values.

Once the science gets to us in the form of science-based policy, things get even more complicated. There is no direct route from lab-coat wearing scientists to us. Rather, the science needs to go through a range of intermediaries. These include policymakers and those implementing science-based policy, like medics. If we are worried about the values at any of these points – the scientists, the policy makers, or the medics – trust in the entire scientific enterprise can fail. I might trust my friends over the experts because their values are likely to be closer to my own. Or, at the very least, my friend’s values are more transparent to me than those of some distant scientist or policy maker. If Bob at the pub is a racist, I probably know that and take what he says with a pinch of salt. I don’t know if Robert the scientist is a racist.

**4.  What do to?**

Jonny Steinberg, a South African ethnographer, tells an [instructive story](https://academic.oup.com/afraf/article/116/462/60/2605666). It takes place in South Africa in the mid-2000s, when treatment for HIV/AIDS finally became available. A white doctor from [MSF](https://msf.org.uk/?gad_source=1&gclid=CjwKCAjw6c63BhAiEiwAF0EH1OoY6NuiVb_KXf5C1yAci8k4duhGbhdmqTnVnOAQlXdc-tuL_7PhMRoCpkwQAvD_BwE&gclsrc=aw.ds) (Médecins Sans Frontières or Doctors Without Borders) was travelling between villages administering HIV tests, an intervention that was badly needed in the midst of the AIDS crisis. Rumours spread about the doctor, speculating that he was an agent of the old apartheid regime and that he was using his needle to infect people with HIV rather than testing them. When the doctor reached one particular village, the rumours had already arrived, and the situation was hostile. The doctor’s response was to stand on the table and take some of his own blood, while explaining the science behind the tests. On Steinberg’s telling, this defused the tension.

The story is intriguing because it is really a story about reducing the gap between the doctor and the people. When he takes his own blood, he literally has skin in the game. Importantly, when he describes the science to the crowd, he treats the community members as people whose worries should be taken seriously. A worrying feature of popular discourse around those who are suspicious of medical interventions is that there is something wrong with them that needs to be corrected. More worrying, in academic circles the emphasis is often on how to subtly manipulate people into compliance. All of this is more likely to expand the distance between citizen and scientist, rather than closing it.

What to actually do at the policy level is more mysterious. Asking doctors to publicly perform blood tests on themselves is not a scalable intervention. One route might be to take seriously the value concerns discussed earlier. If I trust my friends more than the scientists because my friends’ values are more transparent to me, then perhaps promoting value transparency is the way to go. But given the pre-existing climate of distrust between laypeople and experts, this might not work. If I already don’t trust the experts and am then told about the messy tangle of facts and values in the science, this may not improve my attitude toward science and the medical establishment. There is unlikely to be a silver-bullet solution to these challenges. But however we move forward, we need to take people’s worries seriously.
