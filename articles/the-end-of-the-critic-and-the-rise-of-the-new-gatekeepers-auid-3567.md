# The end of the critic and the rise of the new gatekeepers

*Who's gatekeeping the gatekeepers?*

**By Guy Dammann** | *7th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,660 words*

![The end of the critic and the rise of the new gatekeepers](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-end-of-the-critic.webp)

---

*The decline of the professional reviewer is widely mourned as the death of criticism. While it’s tempting to celebrate this as a democratic victory for the internet, **Guy Dammann** and **Elisabeth Schellekens** argue that cultural gatekeeping hasn't disappeared - it's just changed shape. Authority has shifted from broadsheet editors to a tribal web of algorithms, podcasters, and niche forums. Criticism isn't dead; it has just evolved. Our modern gatekeepers look entirely different, but our reliance on them proves we still desperately need criticism to process new ideas and bind our culture together.*

Few deaths have been more often foretold, or better chronicled, than that of arts criticism. Recent decades have seen commentators queuing up to mourn the demise of both [academic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-20dZxUAfu0) and [journalistic](http://www.frieze.com/article/death-becomes-them) arts criticism.  The mourners—for the most part critics themselves, understandably keen to write their own obituaries before their pens are taken away completely—cite mounting evidence of the crisis of criticism in the form of the steady disappearance of arts reviews from the pages of regional and national newspapers and their replacement by the frenzy of social-media-enabled opinion-mongering. In academia, too, scholars in disciplines such as literature and musicology, for the most part founded as domains of humanistic criticism, only rarely nowadays think of themselves as critics. Where arts scholarship in the first two-thirds of the twentieth century kept keenly in view the desire and duty to make and defend value judgments, judgments which often had significant impact on the way the arts were appreciated and practised, scholars today patiently excise [all talk of aesthetic value](https://iai.tv/video/trash-trends-and-the-transcendent) both from their own work and that of their students. No wonder, given the decades of research assessment in which humanistic scholarship has been squeezed into the categories of scientific progress. But why do we subject the arts to historical and systematic study in the first place, if not to remind ourselves why we value them so highly?

\_\_\_

Is criticism dead, or is everyone alive a critic?

\_\_\_

There is no doubt that the power and prestige once accorded to a small number of mostly white, male high-profile critics has largely evaporated. Figures such as F.R. Leavis or Harold Rosenberg, who could not only make or break individual reputations but shape the understanding and taste of entire artistic epochs, are evidently long gone. Sceptical commentators argue, however, that arts criticism has not disappeared but is, rather, [flourishing in its newly found democratization](https://hyperallergic.com/the-fabricated-crisis-of-art-criticism/). According to this view, the plethora of individual voices which the internet and social media platforms have enabled brings a diversity of perspective and erudition which is entirely to be welcomed, echoing as it does more closely the everyday experience of readers, spectators and listeners. Where before the gatekeepers of communication were kept at one remove from consumers via the one-way street of newsprint communication, the tweed jackets have now been replaced by smart math, as algorithms react in real time to the habits and preferences of real individuals. We’re all gatekeeping together now, every time we search, click and spend.

So which is it to be? Is criticism dead, or is everyone alive a critic?

Even the briefest glance at the history of criticism reveals that these questions have been asked and answered for as long as criticism established itself as a practice. Indeed, the scholastic witticism, *de gustibus non est disputandum,* was never more frequently cited than during the 1700s, when the concepts of *bon go**û**t* and artistic genius loomed large enough to shake up established social hierarchies. If matters of taste are not to be disputed, it was certainly remarkable how the century’s intellectual elites devoted themselves, and the countless pamphlets and periodicals founded for the purpose, to making a go of it.

In the nineteenth century, which saw the rise of professional critics contracted by newspapers, the stakes rose and the debates heated up. One debate in particular spanned almost the entire century of English letters, beginning in 1800 with Wordsworth’s dismissal—penned, ironically enough, in the critical essay on poetry which prefaces his [*Lyrical Ballads*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/9622/9622-h/9622-h.htm)—of the “superlatively contemptible” breed of critic who prizes tradition over innovation, canonicity over contemporaneity. Wordsworth returned often to his theme, claiming throughout his life that the efforts of critics had been much better directed toward genuine creativity.

\_\_\_

If the spirit of criticism is best exemplified by the spoken commerce of ancient Athenians, why can it not survive the transition from newspapers to social media, painful though it may be to those trying to make a living from it?

\_\_\_

The most powerful response came only after Wordsworth’s death in the form of another preface, this time by the revered Oxford critic and poet Matthew Arnold. The essay, published in 1864 and entitled “[The proper function of criticism at the present time](http://public-library.uk/ebooks/24/100.pdf)”, identifies the aims of criticism with truth. Arnold tasks the critic with “seeing the object as in itself it really is”. This did not mean that there could not be bad criticism of the kind so heartily despised by Wordsworth, nor that criticism lacked creativity. Indeed, for Arnold, the creativity of the critic, though inferior to that of the artist, consisted in bringing to the mind of the reader the mixture of sensibility and intelligence required by a poem—and by extension any work of art—to be understood. Criticism, in other words, creates the ground on which great art can flourish, and in this sense is no less essential a part of culture than the arts themselves.

He pursued the argument further in [*Culture and Anarchy*](http://public-library.uk/ebooks/25/79.pdf), in which he focused on the vital role played by the arts in providing coherence to expanding industrial societies. Without good criticism, [art’s power to bind culture together](https://iai.tv/video/books-and-the-future-of-civilisation) quickly wanes and descends into anarchy, which for Arnold represented a kind of cultural atomization in which individuals value pleasure over beauty, profit over truth—a state in which, in modern parlance, everyone prefers their “own truth”. Arnold’s fears, while couched in language that may seem quaint to modern readers—indeed, he was perceived as such already in the 1860s—have been amply borne out in our own time.

The figure of Arnold looms large in Oscar Wilde’s irreverent contribution to the debate, “[The Critic as Artist](https://www.wilde-online.info/the-critic-as-artist.html)”, in which two aesthetes argue over the importance of criticism while smoking Egyptian cigarettes and playing Chopin on the piano. Wilde’s text in large part contradicts Arnold, denying that art has moral value, or that criticism aims at truth: “the proper aim of Criticism”, he states in a witty parody of Arnold, “is to see the object as in itself it really is not.” Nonetheless, Wilde is eager to reaffirm, and indeed radicalize, Arnold’s contention that criticism is the engine of culture. Indeed, for Wilde’s aesthete Gilbert, the creativity of the critic exceeds that of the artist: “without the critical faculty, there is no artistic creation at all, worthy of the name.”

A strikingly contemporary feature of the discussion arises when, echoing Wordsworth, it is asked whether artists wouldn’t be better off without the “shrill clamor of criticism”. Surely ancient Athens, then as now the paradigm example of a culture in which the arts flourished, got along fine without the intervention of critics. Tellingly, Wilde, instead of disputing the premise (written art criticism did indeed exist in Athens), argues instead that the “Greeks were a nation of art critics.” Athenian culture flourished, Wilde observes, not because it lacked criticism, but because Athenian society was shot through with the “critical spirit.” In Athens, a society which exemplified the Arnoldian idea of cultural coherence, it wasn’t that arts criticism as we know it had not yet been born. It was that everyone was a critic.

Historical debates about the idea of criticism serve as a powerful reminder that our current preoccupation with a crisis in the practice is nothing new. At the same time, Wilde’s point that criticism, “in the best of days”, was thoroughly democratic offers grounds for optimism. If the spirit of criticism is best exemplified by the spoken commerce of ancient Athenians, why can it not survive the transition from newspapers to social media, painful though it may be to those trying to make a living from it? Many examples, from fairly traditional-looking arts review websites such as [*Hyperallergic*](https://hyperallergic.com/) and [*The Arts Desk*](https://theartsdesk.com/) to the more innovative [BookTok](https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://www.tiktok.com/tag/BookTok&ved=2ahUKEwiLsZ_C4IiTAxUvIBAIHaM9B9cQFnoECEoQAQ&usg=AOvVaw2-R0tgx6JB0s2aZ3QqabdV), not to mention the innumerable and often astonishingly erudite threads on Reddit, seem to suggest that the age of the internet has found the spirit of criticism to be in rude health. These fora lack the normative power enjoyed by the traditional organs of critical opinion, of course, which affects their cultural function. But it’s certainly hard to mourn the death of reviewing in an age when one can scarcely book a train ticket, or make enquiries about one’s electricity bill, without being asked to review the experience.

The picture seems less rosy, however, when we recall Arnold’s account of what is at stake. For while, in Wilde’s view, art was undoubtedly that which made life, which of course he conceived of as a form of art, worth living, Arnold’s analysis proceeds along the line that the arts are primarily what binds our societies together: they provide the gravitational building blocks of culture, giving sensuous form to our thoughts and social life its coherence. And while he may well have agreed with Wilde about the Greeks being “nation of art critics,” as a theorist whose target was the distinctly modern phenomenon of the nation state, he would have been the first to point out that for a city-state like Athens to flourish without institutions of art criticism is one thing; for extended industrial nations like 1860s Britain to do the same is quite another. The added complexity of culture’s fast-expanding context, in other words, required arts criticism with genuinely national reach. Which is, of course, exactly the demand that newspaper-based arts criticism was created to meet.

Today, of course, the picture is far more complex even than in Arnold’s day. The cultural spheres we inhabit today oscillate between the resolutely international contexts of the “West” and “World” to localised centres of activity. And just as critical narratives extended during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries to reflect our changing individual and communal identities, so too does internet-based criticism today, with its ostensibly global reach, largely operate in restricted orbits of influence. Nor do these shifts necessarily represent meaningful increases in democracy: the authority which previously resided with newspaper critics and their editors has merely passed to an equally select group of self-appointed gatekeepers whose niche status—think, for example, of the *Modern Art Notes* podcast, or the *Manhattan Art Review*, now instructions of considerable cultural weight—in many respects self-selects their audience.

If the chief characteristic of the age of the internet, aside from its persistent tribalism, is a heightened capacity for innovation, perhaps rather than bemoan the decline in power and prestige of traditional arts criticism we should focus less on what we take criticism to be—its institutions, language, and standard practices—and more on what we want it to do. That, at least, would be closer to the spirit of Arnold and Wilde.

In our research project, [Aesthetic Cognitivism and the Prospects of Criticism](https://www.aestheticcognitivism.com/), we are trying to channel questions about the idea and purpose of arts criticism toward philosophical and psychological debates about the cognitive benefits of art, and the idea that there are strong grounds, both a priori and empirical, for thinking that aesthetic experience enhances our understanding of the world. Philosophers, from Plato onwards, have long expressed skepticism toward the idea that we can learn anything valuable from our artistic encounters. Psychologists are more optimistic, but there are, so far, few studies in which the contribution of criticism plays a role. As part of the project, we are also assembling a large database of contemporary and recent arts criticism in order to study the ways in which the language used by critics develops in relation to particular works of art and artists, helping us get a clearer picture of how criticism has shaped not just our experience of art, but our expectations in respect to its value and significance.

A favorite example of what criticism is capable of is the review of Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony penned by the critic, fairy-tale author, composer, and lawyer, E.T.A. Hoffmann. Written over several months following the symphony’s appearance in 1809, [the review was published](https://books.google.se/books?id=UmYZzMF1oiUC&lpg=PA98&ots=JIuXcsgEvt&dq=%22Mozart+takes+more+as+his+province+the+superhuman,+magical+quality+residing+in+the+inner+self.%22&pg=PA234&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q&f=false) in the Leipzig-based *Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung,* preeminent among the numerous music-critical periodicals of the day, with correspondent-critics spread out across Europe.

The significance of Hoffmann’s review lies not in any claim to provide an objectively correct appraisal of Beethoven’s symphony, but rather in how it provided a new language with which contemporary audiences could make sense of a work that pushed violently at the limits of musical possibility. The article is immediately striking for its colorful and, it’s fair to say, sometimes wildly extravagant descriptions of the music (“Glowing rays shoot through the deep night of this realm”), but also for the way these descriptions are carefully woven around a closely argued technical analysis of the score. In cyclical rhetorical bursts, Hoffmann takes the master-trope of “infinite yearning,” which for the German romantics encapsulated the essence of human being, and identifies it with the way in which Beethoven restlessly reworks his thematic material so that it appears to give form to itself through the sheer act of struggle. This was revolutionary music for a revolutionary age, and though Hoffmann doesn’t actually use the term “revolution,” the concept does begin to be applied to music and other art forms in the following decades, to the extent that art’s perceived capacity to effect revolutions of style and sensibility becomes an established yardstick of artistic genius.

In this sense, the success of Hoffmann’s article—which was so long it had to be serialized across two issues of the *AMZ*—doesn’t simply lie in the brilliance of his evocative style or the acuity of his perceptions. Rather, it lies in how it created for its readers a sense of what can be heard in the symphony, marrying philosophical profundity to musical beauty with a force that still ripples through concert halls today. In other words, the review *realizes* the potential of the work before it; and does so moreover with a force sufficient to make that potential real for generations of audiences.

Perhaps this is akin to what Arnold had in mind in his rather obtusely worded formulation, “to see the object as in itself it really is”? At the same time, it also clearly exemplifies Wilde’s rebuttal: the philosophical significance Hoffmann ascribed to the Fifth Symphony was primarily something Hoffmann wrote, not something Beethoven composed, and in that sense, Hoffmann was seeing the “object as in itself it really is not.” But perhaps that is the true mark of successful criticism: that by whatever literary or other means, it manages to make, and keep, art “real.” Certainly, Hoffmann’s review is a prime example of how the creative aspect of criticism acts to shape the way we make sense of art, and thus shape [what artists themselves seek to realize through their own creativity](https://iai.tv/articles/art-is-never-a-representation-of-reality-auid-3492). And while it is not possible today to write about music in the same language as Hoffmann, it wasn’t possible to write about music that way before Hoffmann did so either. That is, after all, what creativity is.

Whatever devices the critics of tomorrow find to carry out the work of realizing artistic potential, those same critics will still surely be busy debating—and lamenting—the death of criticism. Perhaps it’s not surprising that criticism is perpetually found to be in a state of crisis. Indeed, the term “criticism” itself—which derives from the Greek for judging or determining something—has the same root as the word “crisis”, a turning-point or situation which calls for judgment. At bottom, the two concepts are one. Moments of crisis are moments in which reality must be confronted and creative solutions found. Moments of crisis, in other words, are moments for criticism.
