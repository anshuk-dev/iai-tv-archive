# Many minds, not many worlds, constitute quantum reality

*The view from nowhere is leading quantum physics astray*

**By Nadia Blackshaw** | *5th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,073 words*

![Many minds, not many worlds, constitute quantum reality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/many-minds-not-many-worlds-constitute-quantum-reality.webp)

---

*A century after the birth of quantum mechanics, many are still puzzled by the idea that Schrödinger’s cat is simultaneously alive and dead. The mystery drives some of our most prominent physicists to embrace the bizarre idea that reality constantly splits into a near infinity of parallel worlds, of which ours is just one. Philosopher of physics **Nadia Blackshaw** argues that this “many worlds” interpretation goes wrong not only in its extravagant multiplying of entities, but in its attempt to adopt a “view from nowhere,” describing reality from no particular perspective. She proposes instead a “many minds” interpretation, in which the cat is alive from one perspective and dead from another. It’s time physics took conscious perspectives seriously.*

We like to tell the story of Schrödinger’s cat as if it is a puzzle about physics. There is a sealed box, a quantum event, a cat that is (according to the mathematics) both alive and dead until someone looks. But what is it like to be the cat?

Inside the box there is no equation, no observer, no paradox, just an experience. And it seems very unlikely that the cat is thinking or experiencing being in that multiple dead-and-alive superposition state. Experience, as far as we know, never feels like two incompatible things at once. We never remember a day when the cat was both alive and dead or when the coin both landed heads and tails. Quantum physics, meanwhile, describes the world as having [these strange indefinite states, where coins or cats are two (or more!) things at once](https://iai.tv/articles/the-many-meanings-of-schrodingers-cat-auid-2057). Of course, this might also be saying that the cat is *neither* dead nor alive. But that still opens the question of what the cat is. So, the tension appears where the world, according to the theory, contains many possibilities at once, but each mind seems to encounter only one.

This gap between our physics and observations or experience of the world encapsulates the [quantum measurement problem](https://iai.tv/articles/the-many-answers-to-the-quantum-measurement-problem-auid-2871). And for nearly a century, physicists and philosophers have been trying to bridge it.

One popular solution is the [Many Worlds Interpretation](https://iai.tv/video/in-a-parallel-universe) (MWI), first proposed by [Hugh Everett III](http://www.weylmann.com/relative_state.pdf) in 1957. If quantum mechanics says the cat is both alive and dead, the MWI takes this seriously: the cat really is both. The catch is that the universe splits or branches. In one branch, you open the box to find a living cat. In another branch, you find a dead one. Every quantum event creates new branches, and so we have an ever-expanding multiverse of parallel realities.

This is bold physics and philosophy, and it is also, to many, wildly extravagant. The universe doesn’t just split occasionally, because quantum events are ubiquitous in nature. The world is therefore constantly branching with every quantum interaction throughout the entire universe, generating uncountable parallel worlds. Yet, despite its radical nature, [the MWI is taken to be a serious contender](https://cerncourier.com/the-minimalism-of-many-worlds/) in the debate, and has many [advocates](https://www.preposterousuniverse.com/blog/2014/06/30/why-the-many-worlds-formulation-of-quantum-mechanics-is-probably-correct/). Setting aside specific concerns about parsimony or other questions surrounding probability in a theory where every outcome occurs, here I want to highlight a different drawback.

\_\_\_

There is one underlying physical reality, but within that we can explain the quantum description of the cat being both dead and alive, as pointing towards multiple, branching minds.

\_\_\_

The MWI approaches quantum problems like Schrödinger’s cat from an outside "[view from nowhere](https://iai.tv/video/the-quest-for-a-final-theory-michio-kaku)" perspective. It gives the overall picture but does not correspond to any particular observer’s experience. From this cosmic vantage point, all outcomes happen. A universal quantum wavefunction describes everything, and it evolves smoothly and deterministically. There’s no collapse, no randomness, no mystery. The problem is that we don’t occupy this viewpoint. We are not floating outside the universe. Instead, we are inside it, experiencing one definite outcome at a time. The MWI explains everything from the outside, but it only addresses indirectly what we encounter from the inside, that when you open the box, you always see either a living cat or a dead cat, never both.

There is an alternative that starts from a different place, from the only thing we have direct access to, which is our experience itself. This is the Many Minds Interpretation (MMI), first [articulated by Albert and Loewer](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00869434) and developed by Lockwood ([1989](https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=E7FvQgAACAAJ&printsec=front_cover&redir_esc=y)), [Donald](https://www.mjdquantum.uk/) and others.

The physical description of the universe evolves just as in the Many Worlds picture. What differs is where we locate the multiplicity, not in separate physical worlds but in the plurality of observer experiences associated with a single quantum state. There is one underlying physical reality, but within that we can explain the quantum description of the cat being both dead and alive, as pointing towards multiple, branching minds. From the outside, the cat exists in a superposition. From the inside, there are different experiences of being the cat, one where things continue normally, another where they don’t. Each experience is perfectly ordinary, perfectly definite. The cat never feels like it’s in two states at once.

\_\_\_

Are multiple minds really better than multiple worlds? The advantage is that the MMI brings into focus the first-person perspective of how we actually experience the world.

\_\_\_

What about inanimate objects like electrons or atoms? They undergo quantum branching too, but (unless you subscribe to panpsychism) they don’t have minds, and so we might be tempted to return to the Many Worlds picture, at least with respect to such non-mental objects. However, difficult questions about definiteness primarily matter for perspectives, or conscious experiences. An electron in superposition doesn’t wonder which state it’s in—the conscious observer does. The measurement problem is fundamentally about reconciling the definiteness of our experience with quantum theory’s mathematics, and that’s where perspectives become essential. For the vast majority of quantum events throughout the universe, there’s just physics, no minds required. We can allow that there is simply indeterminacy in the universe, up until the point that minds get involved, which is precisely when the measurement problem becomes pressing.

Many minds might sound just as extravagant as parallel universes. Are multiple minds really better than multiple worlds? The advantage is that the MMI brings into focus the first-person perspective of how we actually experience the world. Ultimately, we only have access to reality through a specific perspective. This is a recognized aspect of feminist philosophy and also perspectivalism. There is of course use in defining (as far as we can) a third-person “view from nowhere” like the MWI. But there is lots to gain philosophically from also understanding how our experience of the world relates to quantum theory.

Why understand quantum mechanics through a multiplicity of minds? Well, first, it dissolves the original measurement problem by showing it rested on a false assumption. The standard puzzle asks: “How do we get one definite experience from quantum theory’s indefinite superpositions?” But this assumes there’s one experience that must somehow become definite. The MMI rejects this assumption and instead argues that there isn’t one experience that needs explaining—there are multiple experiences, each already definite. No mystery about how indefiniteness becomes definite, because each perspective is definite from the start.

The question that remains is different and arguably more tractable: “How do these multiple definite perspectives relate to the unified quantum state?” This isn’t the measurement problem (which asked how we get definiteness) but a question about personal identity and metaphysics (wrestled with, for example, [by Christian List in the context of the hard problem of consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-reveals-reality-cannot-be-described-auid-3151)). This is a hard question, certainly, but it’s the kind of question philosophers have tools for addressing. We’re no longer stuck with the conceptual paradox of indefinite physics somehow producing definite experience. Instead, we’re asking about the metaphysics of branching persons, which connects to existing philosophical frameworks rather than being a unique quantum puzzle.

\_\_\_

Perhaps most importantly, the MMI keeps the question of consciousness open.

\_\_\_

Second, the MMI offers a natural account of quantum probability. In the MWI, all outcomes occur with certainty in different branches, so what does probability even mean? Various technical proposals exist (for example, [decision-theoretic arguments](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1999.0443), appeals to [branch counting](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/axn029), etc.) but they remain controversial. In the MMI framework, probability can be understood through self-locating uncertainty. Before a measurement, you know that multiple branches of experience will emerge, each seeing a different outcome. The quantum probabilities determine the distribution of future experiences across minds. You don’t know which branch you will find yourself in, but you can apply the standard quantum probability rule (the Born rule) to explain this uncertainty in your branch location.

There’s also something epistemologically satisfying about the MMI. We have direct, immediate access to our own conscious experience, whereas multiple worlds are entirely theoretical. While both interpretations posit multiplicity, starting from what we directly know (consciousness, experience, perspective) rather than purely abstract structure has a certain methodological appeal, even if the idea of one person branching into multiple minds is no less strange than branching worlds.

But perhaps most importantly, the MMI keeps the question of consciousness open. The MWI implicitly assumes that consciousness and experience can be cleanly reduced to a physical branching structure and multiverse. But this is a substantive philosophical claim and not currently a settled scientific fact. For instance, recent work in [quantum biology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119132363.ch21) has challenged longstanding assumptions that quantum effects are not present in biological systems like the brain. Research has even suggested that [quantum models of cognition](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2025/1/niaf011/8127081) are required. Again, this is not definitive, but such work reopens questions about how these fields intersect. The MMI gives room for this wider research on the connection.

Some dismiss the MMI as doing the opposite and forcing us to be dualists. But this is a misunderstanding of the flexibility in defining “mind” and “perspective.” You can develop a framework that’s perfectly compatible with physicalism, where minds are ultimately constituted by physical processes, but where the perspectival structure of experience is taken as a fundamental feature that needs to be accounted for and not explained away. But the framework does allow for wider views on mental phenomena, including more recent [dualist accounts](https://mindmatters.ai/2023/01/philosopher-i-accept-dualism-but-dont-believe-in-the-soul/). And it should not be that a specific interpretation of quantum measurement decides the mind-body problem.

Perhaps the measurement problem and the [hard problem](https://iep.utm.edu/hard-problem-of-conciousness/) of consciousness are connected, and this approach leaves room for this intersection. And if they aren’t, and consciousness is fully describable by physical mechanisms, this still does not remove the fact that we experience the (quantum) world via a perspective. The advantage of the MMI is precisely this flexibility. It doesn’t assume that we already know how consciousness relates to physics. It suggests that understanding might require us to take seriously that we are not describing the world from outside but participating in it from within.

A natural question is whether emphasizing perspectives means abandoning realism about the physical world. Not at all. The MMI is realist: there is an objective physical reality that exists independently of us. Where it differs from Many Worlds is in its *type* of realism. The MWI aims for pure objective realism—a description of reality, independent of all observers. The MMI endorses perspectival realism, where reality is objective (the universal wavefunction, the branching structure, all the physics is real and observer-independent), but complete understanding requires acknowledging that observers with perspectives are part of that reality too. We’re not stuck behind a veil unable to access the “real world.” We can and do describe the physical structure fully. But we also recognize that first-person experience is real and can’t be eliminated from the complete picture. Reality includes both the objective quantum structure and the perspectives experiencing it from within. This is realism all the way down, just realism that takes seriously that we’re inside the reality we’re describing, not floating outside it.

Now, back to that poor cat. When we open Schrödinger’s box, we see one outcome. The physics describes both. The MWI multiplies realities to preserve the physics, whereas the MMI multiplies perspectives to preserve our experience.

Quantum mechanics points towards the connection between third-person and first-person. From our hypothetical view looking down on the entire multiverse, the cat is both dead and alive. But as humans within that structure, our experience navigates just one path at a time. Each perspective encounters a different definite story, and all of these are equally valid perspectives. That we can only ever see one outcome might not be a limitation to be explained away. It might be telling us something profound about the nature of experience and perspective within a quantum world. The MMI takes that insight seriously, and in doing so, it might help us understand not just quantum mechanics, but ourselves.
