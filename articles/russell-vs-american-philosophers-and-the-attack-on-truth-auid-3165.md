# Russell vs American philosophers and the attack on truth

*Truth is a negotiation with reality*

**By John Kaag** | *12th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,597 words*

![Russell vs American philosophers and the attack on truth](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Russell-vs-American-philosophers-and-the-attack-on-truth.webp)

---

*Bertrand Russell was famously critical of American philosophers like William James and their radical theories of truth. According to Russell, James and the other American pragmatists defined truth in terms of what is momentarily useful or comforting, when truth is really about correspondence to an objective reality. But Russell’s attack rests on a caricature, argues philosopher John Kaag. James and the American pragmatists had a far more sophisticated account, one grounded in the recognition that we can never arrive at a final truth or confront reality as such. Instead we engage with reality through experience, in a continual negotiation with a world that resists us.*

The quarrel between William James and Bertrand Russell over the nature of truth is a spectacle in the history of philosophy, the drama of which rests on a caricature: James, the American [pragmatist](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/), supposedly reduces truth to whatever beliefs happen to be useful or comforting, while Russell, with the sharp edge of analytic clarity, exposes the absurdity of such a position. Truth, Russell tells us, must be something more than what works. It must correspond to the facts, indifferent to the accidents of human interest or need. It is an elegant objection, and I have come to think that in its elegance lies its seduction. But like many philosophical objections that survive mostly by repetition, it fails to take its opponent’s best case on its own well-reasoned terms.

\_\_\_

Human beings never confront reality as such—they confront it through experience, through a gradual negotiation with the world that resists them.

\_\_\_

Let’s get something very clear straight away: James never claimed that truth was a matter of mere convenience or momentary utility. [John Dewey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/truth-pragmatic/#DewePragTheoTruthttps://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey/) might have made this mistake occasionally, but James did not. James’s pragmatism, at its core, is a philosophy of experience—not experience in the fleeting, subjective sense, but experience extended, socialized, and tested across the rough surfaces of reality. It is not unlike [C.S. Peirce’s conception of truth](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/truth-pragmatic/#PeirPragTheoTrut) as an approximation to the facts in the infinite long run, tested scientifically by observation and experience. Once this is understood, Russell’s critique begins to falter.

Russell’s own account of the matter is clear. In [*The Problems of Philosophy*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5827/5827-h/5827-h.htm), he writes: “The correspondence theory of truth consists in the view that a belief is true when there is a corresponding fact and is false when there is no such corresponding fact”. The attraction of this view is obvious: it promises to keep truth objective, to insulate it from human frailty, from the flux of feeling and fashion. But this insulation is purchased at a steep price. It ignores, or at least underestimates, the fact that human beings never confront reality *as such*—they confront it through experience, through a gradual negotiation with the world that resists them.

\_\_\_

Truth is not manufactured out of preference; it is discovered in the labor of life.

\_\_\_

James’s challenge is not that [correspondence is wrong](iai.tv/articles/truth-isnt-correspondence-with-the-facts-auid-1960), but that it is epistemologically idle unless fleshed out by the ways in which human beings can verify*—*or, better and more realistic*—*falsify it. In [*Pragmatism*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5116/5116-h/5116-h.htm), he famously writes: “The true… is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as the right is only the expedient in the way of our behaving.” He immediately qualifies this, saying that “expedient” here must not be confused with the merely agreeable or fleetingly useful. It is not the immediate payoff of a belief that makes it true, but its capacity to work “in the long run and on the whole of life’s experience.”

This is what Russell misses in his parody of James, a parody that helped to sink twentieth-century pragmatism from view as a serious epistemological position for nearly a hundred years. His objection about pragmatism’s supposed short-sightedness and Columbus is well-known. “According to the pragmatist theory,” he writes in *The Problems of Philosophy*, “we should have to inquire whether it is more useful to believe that Columbus crossed the Atlantic in 1492, or in 1491, or in 1493; and the date which turns out most useful would then be true.” The rhetorical effect is powerful, but the philosophical point aimed at a strawman. James nowhere suggests that beliefs about the past can be manufactured by convenience. Rather, he insists that beliefs must face the tribunal of experience, that “experience says that’s so” is the only solid ground for truth claims.

Truth is not manufactured out of preference; it is discovered in the labor of life. This is why James can say, without inconsistency, that “we live in a world of realities that can be infinitely useful to us if we know how to touch them rightly, to handle them by our thoughts aright.” The standard of truth is reality’s own resistance, disclosed not in detached contemplation—from an armchair and comfortable smoking jacket—but in the patient testing of belief through experience.

\_\_\_

“Experience,” James writes in The Meaning of Truth, “is the final arbiter of truth, and every theory must submit to its test.”

\_\_\_

Russell’s second objection to the pragmatic conception of truth, involving Santa Claus, has a similar structure. If truth is what works, why not claim that belief in Santa is true, so long as it is satisfactory to the believer? Russell drives the point home with characteristic wit: “If I believe that I am Emperor of China, and the belief has the customary effects of comforting me and enabling me to do my work better, is that sufficient to make my belief true?”

Of course not. But this objection again ignores James’s insistence that what “works” must do so in an [enduring, social, and reality-bound](iai.tv/articles/why-the-news-can-never-be-neutral-and-what-we-can-do-about-it-auid-3115) way. “Experience,” James writes in [*The Meaning of Truth*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5117/5117-h/5117-h.htm), “is the final arbiter of truth, and every theory must submit to its test.” A belief that shields itself from communal verification over the long term, that cannot withstand the growing pressure of evidence or the corrective experiences of others, is not pragmatically true—it is fragile, transient, and doomed to collapse.

James’s epistemology is therefore not a license for wishful thinking but a commitment to the disciplines of inquiry. Beliefs are tested not only against the private needs of individuals but against the shared practices of human life. This is why James can claim that “truth is made largely out of verification-processes”—not invented at whim but made *visible* and *credible* through the social and experiential practices of testing, refining, and correcting belief.

[Russell’s clarity as a logician](iai.tv/articles/the-return-of-idealism-russell-vs-hegel-auid-2066) obscures this deeper point. He presents truth as a fixed relation between a proposition and an independent reality. But James is less interested in defining truth statically than in showing how human beings, finite and fallible, approach it. Pragmatism does not reduce truth to private satisfaction; it recognizes that truth lives in the world of practice, subject to the continuous negotiation between belief and reality.

James’s emphasis on the sociality of experience is crucial here. The testing of belief is not an individual affair; it is embedded in the practices of science, history, and everyday reasoning. Beliefs that cannot survive this communal testing are exposed for what they are: errors, fantasies, or temporary conveniences. In this way, James’s pragmatism preserves objectivity—not by denying the human situatedness of inquiry, but by insisting that the long-run testing of belief in experience is our best approximation of [access to reality](iai.tv/articles/kant-vs-hume-can-we-access-reality-auid-3125).

\_\_\_

Truth, in the end, is not given to us. It is not something we possess once and for all.

\_\_\_

Russell’s worry is legitimate in one respect: the pragmatist must avoid collapsing truth into momentary usefulness or local agreement. But James is fully aware of this danger. He explicitly distinguishes the “temporary subjective expedients” of belief from the harder, slower process by which beliefs become *verified* in the broad experience of life. His pragmatism demands patience, discipline, and openness to correction.

Philosophy, at its best, speaks to the conditions of human life. James’s philosophy of experience is built for creatures who live within time, who learn through trial and error, and who depend on others for the refinement of belief. Russell’s conception of truth (in a way that has always struck me as slightly arrogant) floats above this human condition. Like a man tethered well above the earth in a balloon.

Truth, in the end, is not given to us. It is not something we possess once and for all. It is something we pursue, something we test, something we negotiate with the resistant facts of the world. “Truth lives,” James writes in *Pragmatism*, “on a credit system. Our thoughts and beliefs ‘pass,’ so long as nothing challenges them, just as bank-notes pass so long as they are not refused.” But the moment experience calls in that credit—when reality refuses our comforting fictions—the reckoning is bound to come.

A last word about unfair philosophical critique. The pragmatists were not the helpless victims of Russell’s attack. They could be equally biting and uncharitable. James’s longtime friend C.S. Peirce, who I can only think was a better logician that Russell in almost every respect, wrote to [Lady Welby](https://www.london.ac.uk/news-events/news/17000-pages-digitised-19th-century-philosopher-lady-welbys-personal-library) of Russell: “As to Bertrand Russell’s book, I have as yet made but a slight examination of it; but it is sufficient to show me that whatever merit it may have as a digest of what others have done, it is pretentious and pedantic, attributing to its author merit that cannot be accorded to him.” Philosophers can be backbiting lot, but when truth is truly under attack (as it is today), it might be best for the rational folks among us (of which James, Peirce, and Russell once held rank) not to spend too long quibbling about particulars, and notice that there are more disastrous matters afoot when it comes to the status of truth.
