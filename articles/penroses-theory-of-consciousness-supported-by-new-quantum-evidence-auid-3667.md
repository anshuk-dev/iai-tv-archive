# Penrose’s theory of consciousness supported by new quantum evidence

*New neuroscientific study showcases warm quantum states*

**By Mike Wiest** | *1st September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,969 words*

![Penrose’s theory of consciousness supported by new quantum evidence](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/penroses-theory-of-consciousness.webp)

---

*Roger Penrose argued that human thought does things no computer can, so the mind must make use of non-computable quantum mechanics. The idea remains fringe. Most scientists think that in warm, wet environments like the brain, quantum states collapse into classical states far faster than neurons fire, and so cognition must be governed by classical physics. But neuroscientist **Mike Wiest** argues that new evidence challenges this assumption: stable quantum states are now being observed at far higher temperatures than previously thought possible. And consciousness, he suggests, has structural features that make quantum mechanics a natural place to look for an explanation, so critics should stop accusing Penrose of simply piling one mystery on top of another. The time has come to give this neglected proposal another look.*

**I. Introduction: quantum consciousness**

Quantum physics is generally understood as the physics of the very small. The idea that it might be relevant to understanding how the brain generates our conscious experience has been considered laughable since it was proposed by Nobel Laureate Roger Penrose and others in the 1990s. A popular way to ridicule its proponents, to this day, has been to say they are jamming two unrelated mysterious subjects together, namely quantum physics and consciousness, and declaring them both solved. As I will explain below, this is far from the truth, because there is a clear and decisive conceptual motivation for pursuing a quantum model of biological consciousness.

But first I must address the main *legitimate* reason for skepticism regarding the plausibility of this “quantum consciousness” hypothesis, which is that the brain is too *warm* to sustain a delicate quantum process long enough for it to be useful. To be sure, processes at the atomic level are governed by quantum physics in all matter including brains—this is uncontroversial. But for many practical purposes, these atomic-level quantum effects represent tiny random fluctuations that tend to average out at larger scales, such that classical physical descriptions are adequate.

To harness quantum physics for computational purposes (beyond as a mere source of randomness) requires *coordinating* the quantum activities of very large numbers of atoms. Even though Penrose argued that consciousness requires non-computable physics (see Section III below), he does not deny that neurons perform computations. In other words, Penrose argues that conscious understanding is not reducible *solely* tocomputation. His theory still requires biological structures to “orchestrate” many molecular activities so that the non-computable, conscious part of the process is meaningful and useful to an organism.

Since higher temperatures reflect more violent random jostling at the atomic level, our attempts so far to engineer quantum technology like quantum computers or superconductors have usually required ultra-cold temperatures far below zero. This is to prevent the random molecular movements at warmer temperatures from destroying the quantum coherence—that is, coordination—among parts of the system. This is the source of the intuition that the brain is too warm to sustain *useful* quantum coherence. This [temperature objection](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.61.4194) was [never conclusive](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.65.061901), but in recent years experimental evidence has accumulated from multiple sources to [support the physical plausibility of the quantum consciousness hypothesis](https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niaf011).

**II. Experimental evidence**

*Cellular microtubules are quantum antennas at room temperature*: I will focus here on the theory of [Penrose](https://iai.tv/video/roger-penrose-on-the-blatant-contradiction-of-quantum-mechanics) and [Hameroff](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-is-quantum-mechanical-with-stuart-hameroff) called [Orchestrated Objective Reduction](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-4754(96)80476-9) (Orch OR). They proposed that the quantum effects underlying consciousness happen in microtubule proteins inside neurons. Microtubules (MTs) are stiff hollow tubes made of repeating polymerized subunits of tubulin protein. They are found in all cells, but in neurons they have additional specialized functions. Each tubulin protein is an electric dipole that can join into collective vibrations with the other dipoles. These collective MT vibrations are what Penrose and Hameroff [postulate to underlie conscious experience](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-pre-dates-life-stuart-hameroff). I will return to the meaning of the name Orch OR in Section IV.

Now we are reviewing evidence that MTs can support stable quantum states without ultra-cold temperatures. [Babcock et al. demonstrated in 2024](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpcb.3c07936) a laser-like quantum effect called superradiance in MTs *at room temperature*, which got stronger as they were joined into larger structures, rather than weaker. A few years earlier, in a series of papers, [Saxena et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/fractalfract4020011) (and see also [Singh, Sahoo et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/sym13050821) and [Singh, Saxena et al](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00478.2020).) showed that MTs act as antennas that can couple electromagnetic resonances across multiple living neurons *and* control action potential activity on the neural membrane. Meanwhile, [Gutierrez et al.](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnmol.2021.727025) demonstrated that behaviorally-relevant neural voltage oscillations in the honeybee brain are generated by MTs. Collectively these results establish the plausibility of quantum MT resonances spanning many neurons *in vivo*, modulating neural activity and behavior.

*Anesthesia targets microtubules*: The idea that consciousness is a property of the quantum state of intraneuronal MTs is corroborated by results from my lab demonstrating that the commonly used volatile anesthetic isoflurane acts at least in part on MTs to cause unconsciousness [in rats](https://doi.org/10.1523/eneuro.0291-24.2024) and [mice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropharm.2026.110834). Our findings are consistent with similar but weaker effects [reported in human surgery patients](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2015.05.001) treated with MT-stabilizing anti-cancer drugs that penetrate relatively poorly into the brain. Relatedly, [we recently reported](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yexcr.2026.114938) that isoflurane targets MTs to reversibly slow swimming of the single-celled ciliate *Tetrahymena*. These results support the plausibility of MTs as the physical substrate of biological consciousness, and the evolution of consciousness from older cellular functions of MTs as integrators of sensory information and coordinators of movement. One need only remember the awesome dance of the chromosomes during mitosis—choreographed and effected in large part by MTs—to begin to appreciate dynamic cellular functions of MTs. They are not mere “tent-poles” or passive “bones” of the cell.

*Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) evidence in humans*: But do we have any evidence that the substrate of consciousness is essentially *quantum*, in a living animal? We do. Kerskens and Perez [used an unconventional MRI protocol](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2399-6528/ac94be) designed to isolate signals from entangled quantum states. They reported a [putative macroscopic entanglement signal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjs/s11734-022-00696-2). Moreover, its fidelity [correlated](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1140/epjs/s11734-022-00696-2) with short-term memory performance and the conscious state itself [in sleep vs waking](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2399-6528/ac94be), supporting that the macroscopic quantum state in the human brain is indeed related to consciousness.

**III. An inconvenient truth: a classical model is ruled out**

Even before this accumulation of experimental evidence, multiple authors had noted strong theoretical reasons for considering a quantum model of consciousness. In *The Emperor's New Mind*, [Penrose emphasized an argument based on Gödel’s incompleteness theorems](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-emperors-new-mind-9780198784920), that human conscious understanding must involve a non-computable process which could only manifest upon quantum wave-function collapse, understood as a real physical process called objective reduction (OR).

Here I want to emphasize a more straightforward, accessible argument, based on the irreducible holism of quantum states. Essentially the same argument has been articulated previously—by [Marshall](https://doi.org/10.1016/0732-118X(89)90038-X), by [Stapp](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/9502012), and [in my own recent work](https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niaf011).

To start, notice that every conscious experience involves multiple perceptual features and concepts in relation to each other, such that they [form a single whole](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-breaks-from-the-physical-world-by-keeping-the-past-alive-auid-3447) experienced from a single point of view. This essential property is called the “unity of consciousness.” The problem of how to account for this complex unity neurally is called the phenomenal “[binding problem](https://iai.tv/articles/dreams-show-us-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3654),” because we seek a physical mechanism for “binding” multiple perceptual features into a whole.

Secondly, note that our conscious experiences appear well-adapted to guide our behavior. For example, conscious pain appears to play a role in motivating us to withdraw our hand from a flame. This strongly suggests that our conscious states provide a behavioral advantage that drove their evolution.

A quantum model naturally provides a solution for a fundamental problem—the phenomenal Binding Problem of the causally efficacious unity of consciousness.

\_\_\_

If we take this for granted, it means the unity of consciousness must be mirrored, or implemented, by a unified physical state. In other words, it must be a “causally efficacious” unity. Otherwise, the unified wholes that we experience consciously could have no physical, behavioral consequence by which to provide an evolutionary advantage. They would be impotent and epiphenomenal, and their evolution would be scientifically inexplicable.

To clarify this point, consider the brain as a system of neurons described by the classical physics of electromagnetism. We hypothesize that the physical substrate of each complex but unified moment of conscious experience is a *distributed pattern* of neural electrical activity.

Now, classical physics is entirely *local*. That means everything that happens—everything that *exists*—can be *reduced* to the interactions of localized parts of the system with their immediate neighbors, without losing any content or predictions of the theory. That in turn means that we can in principle *eliminate* all reference to the larger-scale patterns that we hypothesized as the substrate of consciousness. In other words, there are no irreducible wholes in classical physics that could correspond to efficacious, evolutionarily advantageous conscious states.

In classical physics, all such larger-scale entities are like the mythical creatures that an observer imputes to the “pattern” of stars. The hunter Orion does not control the movements of stars in the sky, because there is objectively no hunter Orion in the sky.  If we want our theory of consciousness to be able to account for its evolution, a classical theory is ruled out. This is an inconvenient truth for researchers committed to classical models of consciousness.

In contrast, quantum states are irreducible wholes, and quantum physics is the physics of irreducible wholes. The technical term for the objective holism of quantum physics is “quantum nonlocality,” and it has been [conclusively verified experimentally at macroscopic scales](https://doi.org/10.1063/PT.3.3039), resulting in the 2022 Nobel Prizes. A quantum model [naturally provides a solution for a fundamental problem](https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niaf011)—the phenomenal Binding Problem of the causally efficacious unity of consciousness. In my view, this is the *primary* motivation for pursuing a quantum theory of consciousness. It shows precisely why the “two mysteries” jibe (from Section I) is misguided.

The holism in quantum physics manifests *only* at the moment when the quantum state or wavefunction collapses from an indefinite state of possibilities to a definite outcome. Although quantum nonlocality depends on quantum entanglement, they are not equivalent, because the pattern of entanglement among parts of a system develops completely locally, just as in classical theory. The nonlocal coordination only appears upon wavefunction collapse, also known as objective reduction (OR). This is not widely appreciated among neuroscientists, but it is why the Orch OR theory of Penrose and Hameroff stipulates the OR collapse event as the substrate of a conscious moment, not just an entangled state.

**IV. Quantum active inference: a general theory of sentient behavior**

The conscious experiences of humans are objectively unified, but mere unity is not sufficient for experience like ours. Conscious agents like ourselves have complex purposes, desires that can result in success or disappointment, feelings of pleasure or suffering. Thus, the Orch OR theory proposes that OR collapse events must be “orchestrated” to generate complex meaningful experience like ours. In the biological context, that orchestration is accomplished by evolved structures like microtubules and neurons.

In parallel with the development of Orch OR, a rigorous mathematical theory of optimal sentient behavior has been [developed by Friston](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston) and colleagues, called [active inference](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/12441.001.0001). Consciousness can be formulated operationally within the active inference theory as an implicit planning process that considers alternatives before selecting a near-optimal one. The theory is supported by copious behavioral, anatomical, and neurophysiological data, but it is unclear how classical neurons could perform the requisite sophisticated computations *fast enough*, as [my colleague and I have argued](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csbj.2025.09.017). We pointed out that the active inference description of a conscious agent is mathematically equivalent to the path integral governing quantum dynamics. This means that if the brain has an orchestrated quantum system to work with, it could provide the missing neural *implementation* of active inference. Adopting Orch OR has the further advantage of [accounting for our discrete non-overlapping cycles of perceptual inference and the subjective arrow of time](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csbj.2025.09.016).

The Orch OR theory identifies the quantum physical basis of biological consciousness, and the active inference theory provides the cognitive and psychological interpretation of the physics. We are entering a new epoch in our understanding of ourselves as conscious beings in a physical universe.
