# Time may pass even in a universe that doesn't change

*Beyond the block universe*

**By Brian Kierland** | *29th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,679 words*

![Time may pass even in a universe that doesn't change](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/physicists-are-mistaken-time-is-not-an-illusion.webp)

---

*Many physicists argue that time is just physical change, which since Einstein they see as differences and relations between objects in the so-called “block” universe. But philosopher **Brian Kierland** suggests that this increasingly fashionable view may be too quick. Not only can we imagine time passing without anything changing in the physical world, the claim that time flows independently of physical change may be empirically verifiable, at least in principle. If so, the growing push among some physicists to eliminate time from fundamental physics deserves more skepticism than it gets.*

“Can there be time without change?” is an ancient question. Many, from Aristotle and Leibniz to McTaggart and Mach, have thought that time (in the sense of time passing) requires change. It’s undeniably a natural thought, as all direct measurements of a duration of time use or involve change: change in shadow positions (sundials), change in clock-hand positions (old-fashioned clocks), change in energy levels of electrons in cesium-133 atoms (atomic clocks), etc. Relatedly, our experiential awareness of the passage of time surely involves some kind of change in mental states.

Besides being a fascinating metaphysical issue in its own right, whether time requires change connects with several other issues in the metaphysics of time:

(1) [McTaggart famously argued that time is unreal](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2248314), and a crucial premise in his argument is that time requires change (although McTaggart had a somewhat special conception of change, one on which genuine change specifically involves change in temporal features like *being present* and *being past*).

(2) Paralleling the famous debate between Newton and Leibniz [about whether space is absolute](https://www.jstor.org/stable/688084), there is the debate between *absolutism* about time (time is an entity in its own right, independent of the objects and events in time) and *relationism* about time (time is nothing more than certain relations between, or some kind of ordering of, the objects and events “in” time); absolutism implies the possibility of time without change (so that, if time requires change, absolutism must be false), whereas whether relationism does so depends on the particular version in question.

(3) If time requires change because time just *is* change (i.e., time is nothing more than an ordering of objects and events in terms of change), then certain views in physics and metaphysics become live options, such as (a) certain versions of [the block-universe conception](https://iai.tv/video/back-from-the-end-of-time) (reality is a “static” four-dimensional spacetime block), a conception that some take to be supported by Einstein’s Theory of General Relativity, and (b) [physicist Julian Barbour’s interpretation of quantum mechanics](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_End_of_Time.html?id=TpzEqWEGYoMC&redir_esc=y), on which Schrodinger’s *time-independent* wave equation in effect describes the independent creation of physically unconnected “instants of time” (although he himself appeals to change, or at least something in the near neighborhood, not to give an account of time but, instead, the *illusory* *appearance* of time).

In recent decades, one of the most-discussed arguments in philosophy bearing on the issue of whether time requires change centers on an exceptionally creative thought experiment given by [the American philosopher Sydney Shoemaker in 1969](https://www.pdcnet.org/jphil/content/jphil_1969_0066_0012_0363_0381). In what follows, I present and critically assess Shoemaker’s argument.

Rather than directly arguing that there could be time without change, Shoemaker argues that there could be *empirical evidence* that a period of time has passed without any change. This claim is obviously important: if there couldn’t be evidence of time without change, then even if time can exist without change, we could never know about it!

Furthermore, when Shoemaker was writing, it was commonly thought that, if we can’t have empirical evidence that there has been time without change, then time without change is itself impossible. This was due to the popularity, when Shoemaker was writing, of *verificationism*, according to which if it is impossible to have evidence for a claim, then it doesn’t really say anything, and so can’t be true. Although few philosophers these days endorse verificationism, it can seem attractive: understanding is something that can be *manifested*, and in the case of the understanding of a *sentence*, such manifestation consists in taking certain states of affairs to *verify* the sentence (and taking other states of affairs to undermine or disconfirm it). (This simplifies a line of thought [due to the philosopher Michael Dummett](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Truth_and_Other_Enigmas.html?id=ln_olVVLPm8C&redir_esc=y).)

So, could we have evidence that there has been time without change? Before Shoemaker, most people would have said “No.” One clearly could have evidence that there has been time without change *in a certain localized region*: one could simply observe that region carefully throughout the given period and note that no changes were detected. But could one have evidence that there has been a period in which nothing in the entire universe has changed?

To see why the negative answer is so tempting, imagine that you are in a universe in which everything stops changing—“freezes.” How can you find out about the freeze? You can’t walk around and inspect the hummingbirds frozen in mid-flight, since your body is frozen too. You can’t look out of your frozen body, since your optic nerves are frozen. You can’t even consciously attend to time’s duration, since your brain and mental states are not changing. You cannot have any evidence about the freeze, at least until the freeze is over. But by then, it seems, everything will be as if the freeze hadn’t happened; the most recent events for which you will have evidence will be pre-freeze events. The freeze itself will be hidden. Something like this reasoning led many to conclude that we could never have evidence that there has been a period of time without change.

Shoemaker shows that this reasoning is much too quick, for it faces a potentially devastating counterexample involving an imaginary universe governed by different laws of physics. Here is Shoemaker’s argument:

*Stage One*: There is a universe divided into three regions, A, B and C. Each of these regions undergoes a periodic *apparent* *local freeze* in the sense that observations made by inhabitants of the three regions naturally incline them to say that, for some period of time, all change ceased in the region in question. For example, if the region in question is A, then, for some period of time, inhabitants of regions B and C seem to observe a cessation of change in region A; for example, while inhabitants of regions B and C seem to observe flowers bloom, thrive and then decay in their regions, they seem to observe “frozen” flowers in region A. Additionally, inhabitants of region A seem to observe a sudden “jump” in the states of regions B and C; for example, while inhabitants of region A seem to observe flowers in region A blooming in a relatively seamless way, they seem to observe flowers in regions B and C “jump” from a state of blooming to a state of decay.

*Stage Two*: The apparent local freezes of each region last exactly one year and appear to occur in perfect cycles: every third year for region A, every fourth year for region B, and every fifth year for region C.

Shoemaker argues, first, that in Stage One it is reasonable for the inhabitants of the world to conclude that, when a region undergoes an apparent local freeze, it undergoes a *genuine local freeze* in the sense that all change in that region genuinely ceases for a certain period of time while everything continues as normal in the other two regions. And he argues, second, that in Stage Two it is reasonable for the inhabitants of the world to conclude (given what he has already argued about local freezes) that (because 3x4x5=60) every sixtieth year there is a *genuine global freeze* in the sense that *all* change throughout the world genuinely ceases for exactly one year. Call this the *Global Freeze Hypothesis*. It is illustrated by Figure 1 below.

A first objection to Shoemaker’s argument is that there is an alternative to the Global Freeze Hypothesis, namely, that, as Shoemaker puts it, “freezes occur with these frequencies [those described in Stage Two above] *with the exception* that all three regions skip a freeze every fifty-nine years”. But this alternative hypothesis is clearly less simple than the Global Freeze Hypothesis (as Shoemaker himself notes) and so, following standard scientific practice, the Global Freeze Hypothesis is (at least other things equal) to be preferred.

A second objection comes from [Ken Warmbrōd (2004: 270-271)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0031-8094.2004.00351.x): he claims that, in Shoemaker’s example, the empirical evidence equally supports a generalization that *contradicts* the Global Freeze Hypothesis. The basic idea is that every third year *for which observational data is available*, at least one region is *unfrozen* (region A is or at least appears frozen every third year, but at least one of regions B and C has to be unfrozen for anyone to observe that); similarly, every fourth year for which observational data is available, at least one region is unfrozen, and every fifth year for which observational data is available, at least one region is unfrozen. The three generalizations supposedly supported by these three sets of observational data together imply that, every 60 years, at least one region is *unfrozen*, which contradicts the Global Freeze Hypothesis.

But Warmbrōd is wrong in thinking that the three generalizations in question are in fact supported by the observational data. The fact that at least one region is unfrozen whenever an observation is made is an *observational selection effect*. Consider the classic example of a fish net with holes of size X. Someone fishing with this net in a certain lake catches hundreds of fish and observes that all of them are larger than size X. Would it be reasonable for this person to conclude that *all* the fish in the lake are larger than size X? Of course not. Even were there fish smaller than size X in the lake (even if *most* of the fish were smaller than size X), this person would not catch any of them with this net; so failure to catch such fish with this net is no evidence that there aren’t such fish in the lake.

In this example, the observational selection effect results from limitations in an instrument used to make the observations. But features of observers or observations themselves can produce observational selection effects. For example, no one ever observes a time when everyone is asleep (since, at minimum, a person observes that he or she is awake). Should we, for this reason alone, infer that, at all times, at least one person is awake? Of course not. Returning to Shoemaker’s example, the very act of observing anything requires that at least one region be unfrozen, as a person can’t make an observation when frozen. So, failure of anyone to observe a time when all regions are frozen is no evidence that there aren’t times when all regions are frozen; there could be many times when all regions are frozen, it’s just that no one could observe such a thing.

A third objection, this time targeting Shoemaker’s argument in Stage One, is that there is an equally good alternative hypothesis to genuine local freezes, namely, that, when there is an apparent freeze in a region, *time actually stops* in that region. (See [Corish (2009)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy/article/could-time-be-change/AC360900B59E70EB9C47316EE9E58DC9), [Schlesinger (1970),](https://doi.org/10.2307/2024389) and [Warmbrod (2017)](https://philpapers.org/rec/WARTCA-7).) What is the alternative hypothesis, more exactly? A genuine local freeze is when *time* *passes* in a region but there is *no* *change*. On the proposed alternative, there still is time (the proposal isn’t that time disappears) but it *doesn’t pass*; that’s exactly what an *instant of time* is. So the hypothesis that time stops in a region when there is an apparent freeze is as follows, illustrated in terms of an occasion when region A appears frozen but regions B and C are unfrozen: a year in regions B and C corresponds to a single instant in region A. Here, “corresponds to” means “is simultaneous with”: that year in region B (and every part of that year) is simultaneous with that instant in A, and similarly for region C.

It’s worth making explicit how this alternative hypothesis for apparent local freezes implies that the Global Freeze Hypothesis is false: every sixtieth “cycle” is just *a single instant in all three regions*, not a year during which there is no change in any of the regions. (I say “cycle” because, on this alternative hypothesis, since time does not always pass at the same rate in all three regions, one can’t straightforwardly talk about how much time has passed *period* or how much time has passed *in the whole world*. Plausibly, as Warmbrod 2017 in effect argues, it’s because Shoemaker implicitly assumes that time passes at the same rate everywhere that he doesn’t see the need to consider this alternative hypothesis.) It’s also worth noting, since many wonder how it could be that time has a *rate*, that all we need here are *relative rates*, such as ten seconds in one region per 30 seconds in another region.

Does this time-stoppage objection succeed? In support of this objection, the hypothesis it describes is no more complicated than what Shoemaker proposes. Granted, we normally think that time passes at the same rate everywhere, but we also normally don’t think regions are subject to freezes, so both hypotheses are contrary to common sense. To be clear, the proponent of this objection does not claim that the time-stoppage hypothesis is better than what Shoemaker proposes, merely that the former is as good as the latter (when focusing just on simplicity and fit with empirical data).

Against the objection, it appears that it is committed to denying the transitivity of simultaneity (“if x is simultaneous with y and y is simultaneous with z, then x is simultaneous with z”), which is a serious cost. Consider an apparent freeze-year for region A during which region B is changing. According to the time-stoppage hypothesis, the apparent freeze-year in A is really just an instant (call it *TA*), and that instant is simultaneous with *every* instant in region B in that year. So consider two instants in region B in that year, *TB1* and *TB2*, where *TB1* is earlier than *TB2*. According to the hypothesis, each of *TB1* and *TB2* is simultaneous with *TA*, which means that, *if* simultaneity is transitive, *TB1* and *TB2* are simultaneous. But they’re not; so the hypothesis implies that simultaneity is *not* transitive.

Those familiar with Einstein’s Theory of Special Relativity (STR) will note that the relation “x is simultaneous with y in some frame or other” is not transitive in STR. However, the time-stoppage objection would be forced to reject the transitivity of simultaneity for reasons having nothing to do with STR. Furthermore, even in STR, the relation “x is simultaneous with y *in frame F*” is transitive, for each frame.

A proponent of the objection might respond that the time-stoppage hypothesis should instead be formulated so that it only claims that the *entire year* in region B is simultaneous with the relevant instant in region A (and does *not* additionally claim that parts of that year in region B are simultaneous with that instant in region A). Then a denial of the transitivity of simultaneity cannot be derived in the above fashion.

So, whether Shoemaker’s argument succeeds turns on detailed questions about how to formulate the time-stoppage hypothesis and how it compares with what Shoemaker proposes, questions that I do not claim to have fully resolved here.

I thus also, to return us to where we started, do not claim to have resolved whether Shoemaker’s argument defeats that verificationist way of arriving at the conclusion that time without change is impossible. For all that I have said, it might be that time does indeed require change, with its associated consequences: at least one of McTaggart’s premises is secure, absolutism about time is false, and (if time just *is* change) certain versions of the block-universe conception, and Barbour’s interpretation of quantum mechanics, are live options.
