# Thinking is a kind of dancing

*Plato, Nietzsche, and why philosophy begins with movement*

**By Kimerer LaMothe** | *11th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,493 words*

![Thinking is a kind of dancing](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/thinking-is-a-kind-of-dancing.webp)

---

*Philosophy is usually treated as a purely mental pursuit. But philosopher and dancer **Kimerer LaMothe** argues that the core skills of thinking—paying attention, imitating, experimenting, and practicing—grew out of the rhythmic, social movement we call dance. Drawing on evolutionary theory and figures from Plato to Nietzsche, she suggests that dancing is not an object for philosophy to analyse but the activity that makes philosophical thought possible. To understand how we think, she argues, we need to pay attention to how we move.*

When it comes to philosophy, it is worthwhile to consider the Delphic maxim: [know thyself](https://iai.tv/articles/between-good-and-evil-auid-887). As critics observe, the enterprise often resembles the actions of a dog who buries a bone, only to search for it and, when finding it, celebrate his new discovery. The way philosophers ask questions may hide the answers they set out to retrieve.

When it comes to dance, the metaphor is apt. Philosophers intent on studying “it” tend to ask questions that bury the bone. Such questions include: What is *it*? How do we identify its [essence and attributes](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691606118/off-the-ground?srsltid=AfmBOorz3Kn4QO5cY-iC0bV8YD7Ee7_xSbdaC9Sh3W5HHWViOewfnLow) (Sparshott)? In what way does dancing [qualify as art](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/critique-of-judgement-9780199552467?cc=us&lang=en&) (Kant), [religion](https://academic.oup.com/jaac/article-abstract/12/3/396/6332042?redirectedFrom=fulltext) (Langer), or non-intellectual [social praxis](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/hegels-aesthetics-lectures-on-fine-art-9780198244981?cc=de&lang=en) (Hegel)? How does dancing make meaning; communicate knowledge; affect social bonding, or otherwise function in relation to perceived social or personal goods?

Yet, philosophers who treat dancing as if it were an object to analyze ignore the very patterns of rhythmic bodily movement that enable them to consider dancing as an object in the first place.

\_\_\_

Dancing ensures, in the words of Nietzsche that the values and ideals we create “remain faithful to the earth.”

\_\_\_

For a philosopher who knows herself, as this essay argues, dancing is the enabling condition of philosophizing itself. The inborn, trainable capacities—that humans activate and educate when they dance—are the very capacities that enable philosophical thinking.

Dancing is a human universal. As anthropologists and psychologists document, every human infant before the age of one already responds to patterns of sounds and movement by moving their own bodily selves [in tempo with what they hear](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12488047/), and [in imitation of what they see](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674060326).

So too, every human culture, from hunter-gatherer to post-modern, features [culturally specific forms of dancing](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/557099/exercised-by-daniel-e-lieberman/). Dancing, among the earliest forms of human culture, most likely generated [the cultural forms](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/316135/how-religion-evolved-by-dunbar-robin/9780241431795) of [religion, morality, law, and art](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/302323/the-faith-instinct-by-nicholas-wade/).

Notably, for the ancient Greeks, dancing is unremarkable. Humans dance. The question is not whether but how, when, where, and why. For both Plato and Aristotle, the practice of [ordered, rhythmic bodily movement](https://www.amazon.com/Dance-Theory-Plato-Aristotle-Essays/dp/1952627028) is essential to [the education of human reason](https://www.amazon.com/Tango-Metaphor-Life-Philosophy-Through/dp/3032148766/ref=sr_1_1?sr=8-1). Such bodily action promotes the development of ordered, rhythmic rational thinking. The practice of dancing channels the force of emotions and ensures an optimal balance between mind and body.

Even more importantly, for these philosophers, dancing cultivates grounds for discernment: philosophical choices must serve an inner and outer harmony, as well as the balance between. And in order to *know* this inner/outer harmony, philosophers must have a sensory-kinetic awareness of it within ourselves.

By practicing dance, the ancients claim, philosophers guard against the possibility that their musings drift into abstract fantasy realms; they hold their thinking accountable to [actual experiences](https://iai.tv/video/the-dance-of-life) of sensory-kinetic bodies on Earth. Dancing ensures, in the words of [Nietzsche](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/321142/the-portable-nietzsche-by-friedrich-nietzsche/), that the values and ideals we create “remain faithful to the earth.”

Any attempt to provide a philosophy of dance must account for this dance of philosophy.

What are the capacities exercised in dancing that enable and regulate philosophical thinking? In my work, I identify four. They include: sensorimotor synchrony (a capacity to make bodily movements in time with a repeating beat); motor mimicry (a capacity to recreate another person’s movements with one’s own bodily self); play (a capacity to move otherwise than expected); and practice (a capacity to isolate a specific sequence of movements and repeat them in order to improve).

These four capacities are inborn—every human has them. They are trainable—they develop as a function of how a human moves as a bodily self. They are life-long and open-ended—there is no single or ideal form they achieve at any point in a life span. Further, each one is tightly wired into our reward systems—engaging any one of them is highly likely to produce feelings of pleasure. Finally, based on archeological and anthropological evidence, these four capacities probably [evolved and coalesced into a recurring practice of social dancing](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674644847) during the time of Erectus (Donald)—hundreds of thousands of years before Sapiens appeared in the African savanna.

While each capacity is shared to some extent with other animal species, the combination and degree of all four in Sapiens is unique. In a forthcoming book (2027), I make the case that a regular practice of dancing drove the evolution of Sapiens by generating the social, psychological and physiological matrix from which language use, religion, and conceptual thought all emerged.

As a result, any attempt to do philosophy necessarily animates these capacities, to whatever form they have been trained by the patterns of bodily movement we have made and become. What we can notice, name, analyze, and evaluate is not simply our choice; nor is it a fundamental feature of reality. Rather, the phenomena that appear to us as real or relevant do so as a result of the patterns of bodily movement we have created and become.

\_\_\_

Philosophy is, at a fundamental level, a play with rhythmic bodily movement.

\_\_\_

Because humans are dancers, we can *perceive* an object as worthy of attention based on how it activates our inborn, trainable capacities to sense and respond. “It” moves us, as we move our bodily selves—our eyes, ears, heads, hands, torsos, and legs—in relation to it, so that it appears to us more clearly.

Because humans are dancers, we can *play* with objects—and [play with ideas](https://iai.tv/video/seriously-playful) as if they were objects—approaching them from various angles to handle, flip, swap, juxtapose, compare, turn inside and out, break and recombine, and, in the process, generate new patterns of sensing and responding.

Because humans are dancers, we can identify the specific set of sensory-kinetic movement patterns by which objects appear and then repeat those patterns. We can thus grow in the direction of our sensory-kinetic efforts, inviting related phenomena to appear to us in the shape of new ideas, insights, and action potentials.

These descriptions are not metaphors. When we do philosophy we cannot *not* engage capacities rooted in an ancient, inherited, ongoing, full-body, lived experience of dancing. Philosophy is, at a fundamental level, a play with rhythmic bodily movement.

Implications follow. For one, any philosophical idea may be viewed as nothing more or less than a pattern of movement potential—that is, it represents a potential for isolating, analyzing, and privileging some element of sensory-kinetic experience via specific patterns of neuro-muscular coordination, however subtle. An idea orients our attention; it educates our inborn trainable capacities to notice and respond to rhythm and visual shapes. An idea delimits a space of cognitive-sensory play and sets up a node of neuro-muscular animation to which we practice returning.

For this reason, philosophers court danger when they perceive their work as an exercise in self-motivated mental action. In doing so, they inadvertently privilege certain spans of sensory-kinetic awareness (namely, those associated with thinking, reading, and writing) over others, taking them as fixed and fundamental, rather than embracing philosophical work as a temporal, emergent expression of their own bodily becoming.

Finally, as Plato, Aristotle, and Nietzsche all aver, philosophers keen on retaining the flexibility and relevance of their thinking are well-advised to engage in practices of rhythmic bodily movement. Such practices quicken their sensory-kinetic awareness; exercise their perceptive range; and cultivate the discernment needed to understand their own experiences as well as experiences beyond one’s own. As Nietzsche affirms, “I would not know what the spirit of a philosopher might wish more to be than a good dancer.  For the dance is his ideal, also his art, and finally also his only piety, his ‘service of God.’”

According to the great American modern dancer [Martha Graham](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9781403977267), dancing reveals “the human.” And what dancing reveals as ‘human’ is an ongoing, life-long, process of creating and becoming patterns of bodily movement. Dancing enacts and expresses the insight that how we move our bodily selves matters to who we become—to what we think, feel, value, and imagine. Even when doing philosophy.

Rather than approach dancing armed with philosophical questions, then, philosophers may be better served to perceive and practice philosophy as a form of dancing. In this light, philosophy is most effective when the patterns of bodily movement it entails and prescribes guide those who do it (and read it) to cultivate a greater sensory-kinetic awareness of their own movement-making, and of what they are creating in themselves and the world.

As a form of dancing, philosophy is most effective in so far as it discovers and elaborates potentials for movement-making that align with the health and well-being of ourselves, our communities, and the Earth that sustains us. Rather than bury dance as an object to discover, philosophers can embrace dancing as an ongoing feature, always already present in their ability to ask “what is…” at all.
