# Reality is not a controlled hallucination

*Consciousness is not predictive processing*

**By Evan Thompson** | *9th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,533 words*

![Reality is not a controlled hallucination](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/NEW-reality-is-not-a-controlled-hallucination.webp)

---

*The idea that 'reality is a controlled hallucination' has been recently popularised by figures such as neuroscientist Anil Seth. But this claim, which purports to be hard, down-to-earth science, is, in fact, bad philosophy. Philosopher and author of* The Blind Spot: Why Science Cannot Ignore Human Experience*, **Evan Thompson**, here argues the 'controlled hallucination' hypothesis brings nothing new to the table regarding the problem of consciousness. And there is a circularity problem: if the theory claims that reality is a hallucination, then that theory itself is part of the hallucination.*

“Reality is a controlled hallucination” may look like cutting-edge science but actually is debatable philosophy from the nineteenth century. Popularized recently by neuroscientist [Anil Seth](https://www.anilseth.com/being-you/), it traces back to [Hermann von Helmholtz](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hermann-helmholtz/) (1821-1894), a brilliant German scientist and philosopher who made fundamental contributions to physiology, physics, mathematics, epistemology, and the philosophy of science. In the twentieth century, AI scientist [Max Clowes](https://cogaffarchive.org/sloman-clowestribute.html), psychologist [Chris Frith](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Making+up+the+Mind%3A+How+the+Brain+Creates+Our+Mental+World-p-9781405160223), and neurophysiologists [Humberto Maturana](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-94-009-8947-4) and [Rodolfo Llínas](https://books.google.ca/books?hl=en&lr=&id=7TWDYUYSce0C&oi=fnd&pg=PA111&ots=DcyfPKE9oF&sig=SMd-hYKb2winkpgMjqi_BBj9K1M&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q&f=false) put forward versions of the idea. Unfortunately, it’s replete with problems. A better viewpoint is that perception discloses and gives us access to how things are in the environment tailored to our bodily agency.

Today’s version of the reality-as-controlled-hallucination idea comes from the “predictive processing” theory of perception. Suppose you wake up in the night and it’s pitch black in your room. (This example comes from [Alva Noë](https://academic.oup.com/mind/article-abstract/127/506/611/4797567).) You can’t see anything and there’s a power outage. As you’re moving around, looking for a flashlight, you stub your toe on a piece of furniture. Your mental map of where things are isn’t quite right, so you update it and try to carry on without bumping into things again. You predicted the layout to be a certain way but were forced to revise your map, and now you try to minimize the likelihood of making another mistake. That cycle of prediction, updating, and acting, while trying to minimize prediction error, is how perception in general works, according to the predictive processing theory.

Of course, we hardly ever experience perception as being like this. But your brain is always going through that kind of cycle, according to the theory. Your brain actively meets incoming sensory signals on its own terms based on its having a pre-existing model of the outside world. It constantly makes predictions about the sensory signals it will receive, and works to minimize the difference between predicted and actual sensory information. To minimize prediction error your brain revises its internal model and generates motor actions that change sensory stimuli to bring them closer to what it predicts. Sensory signals function as error signals in relation to your brain’s own self-generated predictions.

\_\_\_

Thinking that perception happens inside the brain is like thinking that flying happens inside the bird’s wings or that dancing happens inside the dancer’s nervous and musculoskeletal systems. This is a category error.

\_\_\_

If we stopped here, we’d have a general framework in which to develop testable dynamical-statistical models of the brain’s role in perception. The problems arrive when our perceptual experience or consciousness is said to be nothing other than the content of our brain’s predictive model. This is to identify perceptual content, which resides at the level of the whole animal or person standing in relation to the world, with neurophysiological transactions, which take place inside the brain.

The identification is fallacious. The brain is not the perceiver; the brain is the central organ of a whole perceptual system that includes the body, as perceptual psychologist [James J. Gibson](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781315740218/ecological-approach-visual-perception-james-gibson) taught us fifty years ago. Thinking that perception happens inside the brain is like thinking that flying happens inside the bird’s wings or that dancing happens inside the dancer’s nervous and musculoskeletal systems. This is a category error. It attributes something that properly belongs only to the whole animal or person to one of its parts.

The mistake is conceptual, not empirical, and it misdescribes perception as a kind of groping in the dark. The mistake has disastrous, self-undermining implications: we don’t perceive the world; we perceive only the content of our internal model of the world. This entails that when we do neuroscience we don’t perceive the brain; we perceive only our internal model of something that we call “the brain” from inside the model. The theory swallows its own postulated basis and hence fails to provide a coherent scientific or philosophical explanation of perception.

Let’s go back over this argument in more detail.

“Reality is a controlled hallucination” is a provocative way of saying that [what we perceive as reality is the content of an internally generated model](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-a-creation-of-consciousness-auid-3365) reined in by correcting for error signals. Perception happens when the brain’s “best guesses” are tightly constrained by sensory signals; hallucination happens when the guesses are unconstrained or loosely constrained by sensory signals. In either case, the nature of the internal content (the guesses) is the same; what differs is the strength of the constraints. It’s supposed to follow that there is no difference in kind between the content of perception and the content of hallucination: perception is controlled hallucination, and hallucination is uncontrolled perception.

What the predictive processing theory gets right is that the brain plays an active, creative role in enabling us to engage with the world. This role cannot be captured by a feedforward network with a one-directional flow of activity from outside to inside. Furthermore, when we model the brain as a recurrent network that loops back onto itself like a snake biting its tail, then we cannot distinguish between perception and hallucination from the vantage point of its internal operation.

\_\_\_

If reality is a controlled hallucination, so too is the brain, and a hallucinated brain cannot explain the hallucination of a brain.

\_\_\_

It doesn’t follow, however, that there is no real difference in kind between perception and hallucination at the level of the whole animal or person geared into the world. On the contrary, perception, like flying or dancing, is inherently relational and world-involving, whereas hallucination is not: hallucination is a kind of sensorimotor misfire that subjectively resembles but does not have the same kind of content as perception. As a general rule, it is illegitimate to read off facts about the whole concrete animal or person from an abstract, simplified model of one of its parts. This not only confuses the map with the territory but also conflates the perceiving agent with its brain. The brain enables perception but the perceiving agent is the whole body. Perception makes the world available to the agent, and the world disclosed to perception provides the standard for determining which subset of experiences may be hallucinatory.

You may be wondering, how could perception disclose the real world if it presents things as having qualities they do not really have? For example, Thomas Metzinger in [*The Ego Tunnel*](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/thomas-metzinger/the-ego-tunnel/9780465020690/?lens=basic-books) (p. 20) writes: “The apricot-pink of the setting sun is not a property of the evening sky; it is a property of the internal model of the evening sky, a model created by your brain. The evening sky is colorless.” Similarly, Seth writes in [*Being You*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/566315/being-you-by-anil-seth/) (p. 120): “Our perceptual world alive with colours, shapes, and sounds is nothing more and nothing less than our brain’s best guess of the hidden causes of its colourless, shapeless, and soundless sensory inputs.”

But this is not the right way to look at things. Although sensory stimuli such as photons or light waves are not coloured in the way macroscopic objects are, colours are not illusions or properties of internal models. Colours are relational properties of the world: they are properties of surfaces and volumes relative to perceivers and environmental circumstances. As I argued in [*Colour Vision*](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9780203417676/colour-vision-evan-thompson?context=ubx&refId=84e93aad-40c7-497e-8cf2-80691b8049cb), colours are ecological properties, and perception makes them available to us and other colour perceivers. The world is truly coloured when described at the proper ecological level of explanation. Although the sky consists of colourless electromagnetic radiation at the microphysical level, this is entirely consistent with the sky being really coloured at the ecological level.

The fatal problem with the statement that reality is a controlled hallucination is that it undercuts itself. To be more precise, it undercuts itself as a physicalist proposition, one that is advanced to explain how consciousness, in the form of perceptual experience, is constituted by nothing but physical brain processes. If reality is a controlled hallucination, so too is the brain, and a hallucinated brain cannot explain the hallucination of a brain.

But wait a minute, you may say, what about the physical brain? Doesn’t it exist outside the hallucination?

If [reality is a controlled hallucination](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston), the physical brain is inaccessible to us in principle. All we have access to is the empirical brain—the brain we can see, scan, and measure—but this brain is part of the controlled hallucination. What we thought was the physical brain, which we supposed to be objective and outside the hallucination, and the source of consciousness, now turns out to be a content inside the hallucination. It follows that we aren’t really studying the physical brain when we do neuroscience, at least not directly. We’re studying our “best guesses” about a hidden, unobservable something that we call “the brain” from inside the hallucination.

This means that appealing to the brain as what generates and constitutes consciousness will not work. Again, anything that’s part of the hallucination cannot explain the hallucination. The hallucination cannot generate its own source. That would be like a reflection generating its object or a film producing the projector.

The argument generalizes beyond the brain. Anything we perceive is just a “best guess” made from inside the hallucination. The hallucination subsumes everything. The model devours its own physical basis. That outside basis becomes just another hypothesis inside the hallucination. Indeed, the meanings of the terms in which the hypothesis is stated (“reality,” “brain,” etc.) are part of the hallucination, and a hallucinated hypothesis cannot explain the hallucination. (Philosophers will recognize the parallel to [Hilary Putnam’s famous discussion of the brain in a vat](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/reason-truth-and-history/17C4C420E3BFE409FD6673C262BF1446).)

But, you may object, if the hallucination cannot generate its own source, doesn’t it follow that it must have an outside physical source?

An outside source, yes, but not necessarily a physical one. Remember that “physical” describes what we perceive and infer to exist from inside the hallucination. We have no hallucination-independent way to access outside reality and establish what its nature is, according to the reality-as-controlled-hallucination viewpoint. For all we know the world outside the hallucination could be mental, as absolute idealists think, or mathematical, as Platonists maintain. The idea of reality as a controlled hallucination leads to an extreme skepticism about our knowledge of the world.

[Helmholtz tried to deal with this problem in 1878](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/ge/helmholt.htm) by saying that what best explains that sensory signals have a causal structure independent of our will—that the hallucination is not haphazard but controlled—is that there exists an outside world with a corresponding causal structure. Sensations are “signs” of things existing or happening, and given such signs we can form “an image of the law” by which things happen and cause our sensations.

This response avoids the incoherence of saying either that something within the hallucination can explain the hallucination or that the hallucination can generate itself, but it has other irredeemable problems.

The term “sensation” is ambiguous. It could mean the sense experiences that the whole animal or person has in their perceptual experience of the world. This is the psychological meaning. But sense experiences and perceptions are not signs. The way a sense experience or perception is about an object is very different from the way a sign refers to an object, as [Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/) (1859-1938) demonstrated in his *Logical Investigations* (1900/1901), written about a decade after Helmholtz’s proposal. To comprehend something as a sign that participates with other signs in signifying a lawful regularity, you have to perceive the physical entity that is the sign (the tracks in the snow, the tree rings). So, sign perception presupposes perception and hence cannot explain it. In addition, a sign indicates something that is absent (the no-longer present animal, the tree’s past), whereas a perception presents its object as present to you (the tracks or tree rings you see and examine). Signs and perceptions differ fundamentally in the kind of meaning they have.

\_\_\_

The world that appears to us in perception is the real world and the one that science investigates. Science is not a body of inferences about an inaccessible reality hidden behind the veil of a controlled hallucination.

\_\_\_

Another meaning of “sensation” is the stimulation of nerve endings. This is the neurophysiological meaning. But now we have the same category error as before. The brain is not the perceiver. Our brains enable us to perceive and our perceptions are correlated with brain states, but perceiving is not the same thing as a brain state, just as a bird’s wings enable it to fly and flying is correlated with wing states, but flight is not the same thing as a wing state. Although we can model nerve stimulations as if they were signs, perceptual experience is not a matter of making inferences from signs (except when the perceptual object itself is a sign).

Helmholtz’s strategy will not save physicalism (as he would have admitted). All we are entitled to infer about the outside world from sensations interpreted as signs is that it has a lawful causal structure. But determining that structure is an ill-posed problem: the problem lacks a unique solution because many different outside structures could generate the structure we experience. Moreover, we remain completely in the dark about the nature or being of the individuals that instantiate that structure. We certainly are not entitled to infer that they are physical in the sense of being fundamentally non-mental while also being sufficient to generate and constitute consciousness.

The problem of consciousness for the physicalist is to explain how a purely physical system (where “physical” means fundamentally non-mental) could possibly generate and constitute consciousness. Predictive processing theory and the reality-as-controlled-hallucination metaphysics bring nothing new to the table to help with this problem. Why should a predictive-processing system embody an experiential point of view? Why should it be consciously aware? Not only does the theory have no answer to these questions but it also postulates a physical source of consciousness that transgresses the theory and for which the theory cannot account. The failure to see these fatal flaws is an instance of what Adam Frank, Marcelo Gleiser, and I call the “[blind spot](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262377751/the-blind-spot/)” in the physicalist worldview.

None of what I’ve argued casts doubt on the potential usefulness of predictive-processing models for getting a working handle on the incredible complexity of the brain. But, [like all computational models of the brain](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262548045/the-brain-abstracted/), they are abstract, idealized simplifications, and hence strictly speaking are false when taken to be literally true of the brain. They also cannot be used to specify the content of perception at the level of the whole animal or person. As for reality being a controlled hallucination, that’s not new science but an old, bad metaphysical hangover.

The world that appears to us in perception is the real world and the one that science investigates. Science is not a body of inferences about an inaccessible reality hidden behind the veil of a controlled hallucination. Science is an enlargement and enrichment of perception as the touchstone of what is real.
