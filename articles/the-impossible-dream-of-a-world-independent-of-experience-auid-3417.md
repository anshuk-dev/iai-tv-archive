# The impossible dream of a world independent of experience

*A review of Graham Harman's Waves and Stones*

**By Michael Kuznets** | *13th November 2025* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,456 words*

![The impossible dream of a world independent of experience](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/25-11-14.world-without-experience2.ata.webp)

---

*We once thought philosophy could step beyond the limits of human perspective, describing the world as it exists in itself. Waves and Stones takes up that challenge, arguing that all things, from people to planets, exist on equal metaphysical footing. Philosopher Michael Kuznets argues that this attempt still ends up reaffirming the very human standpoint it seeks to escape. Even the boldest metaphysical systems remain trapped by our ways of seeing and valuing the world. This follows on from last week’s article [“On the Ultimate Nature of Reality,”](http://iai.tv/articles/on-the-ultimate-nature-of-reality-auid-3408) where Harman laid out his vision of reality as composed of objects rather than processes. Here Kuznets shows how that vision still bears the imprint of human experience.*

When I was at university, a lecturer told the class that a philosopher should be interested in everything, since philosophy touches on everything. Graham Harman would surely agree, writing that “where certain topics arise again and again in every place we look, we seem to be dealing with a basic feature of reality. These are the moments when philosophy…is called to the scene.” His latest work [*Waves and Stones: On the Ultimate Nature of Reality*](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/313185/waves-and-stones-by-harman-graham/9780241392867) (this is his thirteenth published book in a decade), examines the clash between the continuous and the discrete via a sight-seeing tour of all the places Harman finds this basic feature of reality. We see the rise of the Mongolian Empire through the historical theory of fourteenth-century scholar Ibn Khaldun, a dispute over simultaneity between philosopher Henri Bergson and Albert Einstein, Aristotle’s inadvertent innovation of metaphysics, and a bitter fight in the world of evolutionary science. Is this a scattergun tactic, presenting a bewildering array of examples in an unnecessary amount of depth, in order to bamboozle the reader into giving the rather thin philosophical claims of the author a rather kinder hearing than they deserve? Or is Harman carefully weaving his case that the interface of the continuous and the discrete is a fundamental feature of reality that any ontological schema must reckon with?

\_\_\_

*Waves and Stones*expands on 2018’s *Object-Oriented Ontology: A New Theory of Everything.*Object-Oriented Ontology (OOO) arose out of Harman’s frustration with what he viewed as the anthropocentrism in [Heidegger’s ontology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)(and in the ontological projects of fellow philosophers in the continental tradition). By anthropocentrism, Harman is not merely concerned by the privileging of human perspectives over those of other animals. He argues that continental philosophy has neglected object-object interactions. By this, Harman means the ways in which nonhuman things affect, respond to, and encounter one another independently of human perception. OOO is aimed at, amongst other things, redressing that balance. Critics of OOO have often questioned whether the very notion of “objects” can bear the metaphysical weight Harman gives them. In Waves and Stones, Harman responds by grounding the reality of objects in their discrete and continuous features; but in doing so, he exposes the tension at the heart of his project: any ontology that rejects the human viewpoint must still rely on the way things appear to us to make its case.

On the ultimate nature of reality
By Graham Harman](https://iai.tv/articles/on-the-ultimate-nature-of-reality-auid-3408)

Harman explains that the problem of ‘waves’ and ‘stones’ is how the discrete can causally affect the continuous and vice versa, naming this interaction *thixis.* Harman is motivated by a concern that in both his own continental field and within the analytic, there has been a growing disdain for objects, in favor of fields, waves and “everything-is-everything-else Deleuzean Hinduism”. He has, [in interviews](http://iai.tv/articles/on-the-ultimate-nature-of-reality-auid-3408), included amongst the “flux-lovers” everyone from [Slavoj Žižek](http://iai.tv/articles/zizek-quantum-physics-shows-reality-is-incomplete-auid-2940) to [James Ladyman](http://iai.tv/iai-academy/courses/info?course=every-thing-must-go).He gives this kind of reductionism as short shrift as he does its opposite. Yet it is this denial of objects that takes up most of his time, for he sees it as a far more pervasive and fashionable tendency. “Reductionism is an extreme way of reducing things downward to their tiniest pieces, I would add that holism is an equally extreme way of reducing things upward to a widest context that governs all,” he warns. In setting aside objects in favor of a “Blend-O-Rama” ontology, Harman argues the nature of individual things is ignored or waved away. He believes that there are only two kinds of knowledge, the overmining and the undermining. Overmining knowledge reduces a thing to its relations and actions within the greater system of reality and the disproportionate focus on the continuous is, consequently, a disproportionate focus on overmining knowledge. Undermining knowledge, on the other hand, is to reduce something down to its features. For example, to reduce a substance to its chemical structure or the history of its discovery are both forms of undermining knowledge. This undermining is misdirection, Harman coolly informs us, since it turns your attention away from the object itself towards “its physical and autobiographical underpinnings.”

This is a particularly telling critique, locating Harman firmly in his continental and Heideggerian background, despite his longstanding criticisms of both. The claim that describing a substance’s composition turns us away from the ‘real’ object is to privilege inherent preferences, phenomenological characteristics as ‘real', which exposes an issue the book never satisfactorily resolves: is OOO, and Harman’s waves-and-stones ontology, a phenomenological project or is it not? Harman’s complaint with phenomenological ontologies, such as Heidegger’s, is that they are anthropocentric and disregard object-object interactions. Yet he then argues that the true nature of gold is the properties we observe and value in everyday life, whilst the atomic structure of gold is merely a biographical feature.  He decries scientism as reducing a substance to some mathematical or mechanical understanding that undermines the substance. The contradiction is clear: the yellow luster is self-evidently available only to human senses. If his ontology is interested in giving object-object relations and interactions as much weight as their interactions and relations to us, then how can it center features of objects that are only available to humans? The contradiction is fatal: by grounding the reality of things in qualities accessible only to human senses, Harman’s supposedly non-anthropocentric ontology collapses back into the very human perspective it claims to transcend.

Harman takes us back to the pre-Socratic philosophers in order to try and find how our ontological notions around objects and the continuous got to their current state. Linking the tendency of early philosophers to proclaim that the multiplicity of types were really composed of one substance ([Thales](http://iai.tv/articles/11-philosophers-you-dont-know-about-but-should-auid-2669) believed everything was made of water, [Anaxemines](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/presocratics/) preferred air, [Maurice White](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gs069dndIYk) took a more ternary view) to a continuous view of reality. Harman then introduces Aristotle as returning the primacy of objects, though without a total rejection of continuums. Aristotle serves as an inspiration for Harman’s view that the world has objects and that objects have properties. If a coffee cup is an object, its color and the material it is made of are its properties. This has a purpose-oriented view of essence that chimes with Aristotle’s own teleological philosophy but seems to tell against Harman’s desire to go beyond the anthropocentric. How can the essence of cup-ness vital to this conception of the object be taken to be anything but anthropocentric, since cups are a human invention for human purpose?

Harman acknowledges that his ontological framework must account for the causal interactions between the discrete and the continuous, so *Waves and Stones* goes off hunting the problem of thixis high and low across the assorted disciplines of academia. He sees the continuous and the discrete in historical views of gradual change and great events but also Ibn Khaldun’s theory of the conflict between nomadic tribes and settled peoples. There is some fascinating material here; I freely admit to knowing nothing of Ibn Khaldun’s writings. The analogy between the metaphysical problem of thixis and Khaldun’s view of the inevitability of strife between the ‘sedentary people’, whom Harman calls “discrete pockets of culture”, and the ‘continuous steppe’, is an inspired move. The problem, however, is not just that the analogy breaks down, but that it reveals how Harman’s metaphysical categories depend on social and historical metaphors drawn from human experience. If the cities of the sedentary peoples are discrete ‘urban islands’, then what is within those cities would seem to be continuous: a constant flow of cultural and material exchange. Exchange between what, though? Between the individual elements within the city; the families, households, businesses and institutions, themselves made up of people, themselves made up of cells and so on and so forth. The same is true when we zoom out to look at the object-like nations, made up of the both the objects of cities and the continuum of the rural (itself pockmarked with object villages). Harman would nevertheless see this as further proof of his central claim here; that reality is made of both wave *and* stones. The conclusion absorbs the contradiction rather than resolving it, leaving the boundary between the human and the metaphysical as blurred as ever.

There is an overweening joy that emerges when empirical discoveries, such as wave/particle duality, appear to tally with the conclusion of the continental philosopher, as one who has disarmed an attacker and turned their cruel weapon back upon them.

\_\_\_

Harman aims to show to us that objects really do exist, but he never makes it clear what existence amounts to. If *thixis* is the great metaphysical problem, intended to resolve how the continuous and the discrete can interact, then this would seem to require existence to be a metaphysical claim. It cannot be enough to merely shrug and say “of course, objects *seem* to exist and can be usefully spoken of”, even if they are ultimately convenient packaging of parts of the continuum, packaged by human culture, convention or cognition. That view of objects would not present the problem of thixis, and in any event Harman explicitly rejects it. Again and again he stresses that if everything is a continuum, it would inevitably have degenerated into an undifferentiated soup. Yet he fails to show why these distinctions should count as metaphysical rather than conceptual. His opponents would grant such examples as cultural or descriptive truths, not as evidence of a world existing independently of human cognition.

The question of whether these discrete examples are metaphysically ‘real’ or merely culturally convenient pervades Harman’s discussions of architectural history, [punctuated evolution](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2025/03/13/dawkins-and-pinker-discuss-evolution/) and Kuhn’s famous [paradigm shift theory of scientific discovery](http://iai.tv/articles/why-thomas-kuhn-was-no-revolutionary-auid-1597). Of course, this is meant to prove to us that the conflict between the continuous and gradual is a problem that recurs again and again across disciplines, thereby supporting the book’s subtitular claim to be ‘On the Ultimate Nature of Reality’. But these academic tussles were actually between proponents of a continuous and a discrete view, not about thixis between the two. It is not at all clear that the question of whether scientific progress primarily is a primarily gradual progress or rather in conceptual leaps that recontextualizes everything we thought we knew has any bearing on the ultimate nature of reality. Many of the believers in the “blend-o-rama” would simultaneously grant Kuhn his paradigm shifts, while seeing no contradiction in holding a broadly continuous view of the world.

These chapters raise another problem. Harman has a very engaging way of writing, and this is not a book that requires any prior philosophical knowledge or training. He is a gracious and considerate tour guide, never leaving his reader behind, whether we are wading through the battlegrounds of evolutionary biology or exploring the higher mysteries with nineteenth century French spiritualists. Given his prolific output, the care and craft in the writing here is deeply admirable. Yet the affability of the writing and its kindly paternalism allows him to settle a number of scores along the way, however. In documenting the fight between [Dawkins and Gould over punctuated equilibrium](https://necsi.edu/gradualism-and-punctuated-equilibrium), Gould comes out not merely victorious but triumphant, while Dawkins, in this telling, merely humiliated himself. Gould is again victorious over the New Atheists, in proposing his pompously named “[Nonoverlapping Magesteria](https://caspar.bgsu.edu/~courses/4510/Classes/48A078B0-8402-4995-9161-A2C418612C75_files/Gould_97.pdf)”, over which the likes of Harris and Dennett are reduced to impotently huffing about it on stage. (Harris and Dawkins may not make a sympathetic victim but the battering Dennett comes in for later in the book, as a curmudgeonly and narrow-minded man who wished to take the sweet sensations of life away because they wouldn’t fit into his mind of metal and wheels seems unnecessary.)

Inevitably, inexorably, *Waves and Stones* is hopelessly drawn towards the wave-particle duality of quantum physics, where Schrödinger’s cat is dragged out of its box in all its superpositioned glory. Here, again, Harman tells us more about his attitudes than perhaps he intends when he spends more time on the [quirky philosophical beliefs of Louis de Broglie](http://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-makes-no-sense-without-the-mind-auid-2313), contrasting them with the joyless and foolish beliefs of Dennett. That de Broglie, a Nobel prize winning physicist, understood the importance of philosophy and the irreducibility of conscious experience, is of vital importance for Harman. That Hawking, a Nobel prize winning physicist, felt science to be superior to philosophy, is worth only a passing derogatory mention. It is not only wave/particle duality that serves Harman’s argument, but the incompatibility between quantum physics and relativity, a problem that has famously haunted theoretical physics for a century. Here, in the penultimate chapter, comes the fullest embodiment of the problem of thixis! This in itself presents the perplexing attitude to the sciences that takes root in the continental mind. There is an overweening joy that emerges when empirical discoveries, such as wave/particle duality, appear to tally with the conclusion of the continental philosopher, as one who has disarmed an attacker and turned their cruel weapon back upon them.  Yet on the (numerous) occasions that scientific discoveries militate against the conclusions of the philosopher, there is talk of separate magisteria. Indeed, Harman mocks the analytic tradition for its tendency to defer to science, implying their craven lack of faith in their own discipline. If tomorrow science finds a new paradigm to resolve the tension between the quantum and relativity, wouldn’t Harman point to that great shift itself as further evidence that things are not only continuous? If it proves to be elude physicists for another century, that too will show that the riddle of thixis cannot be solved by mere calculators. The house always wins.

By Alexander Kaiserman](https://iai.tv/articles/metaphysics-matters-auid-1750)

Ought the philosopher to be interested in everything? I think it’s a reasonable expectation, yes. But it is an expectation that relies on the distinct overlapping of magisteria, domains of knowledge. If knowledge is a vast continuum, within which we have drawn little circles, for pragmatic and historical reasons, then the philosopher is justified to ignore those crude boundaries and wander whither she will. If subjects are stones, separate and distinct objects, then the philosopher is not a brave adventurer in distant lands of knowledge but a mere dilettante meddling in other people’s subjects.
