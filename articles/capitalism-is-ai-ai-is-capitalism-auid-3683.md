# Capitalism is AI, AI is capitalism

*Nick Land's prescient theory and its consequences*

**By Vincent Lê** | *17th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *3,032 words*

![Capitalism is AI, AI is capitalism](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/ai-is-capitalism-capitalism-is-ai2.webp)

---

*With AI now dominating headlines, those who have been discussing artificial intelligence for decades must be blown away by the strangeness of the narrative. However, that fragmentation and bewilderment is perhaps what many indeed expected to see. Nick Land appears one of the most prescient in his argument that capitalism already is a self-improving, automated machine. Here, philosopher **Vincent Lê** explores Land's apparently wild claims, exploring their truth and consequence.*

The philosopher Nick Land is notorious for the many rumors, legends, and controversies that surround him: from his mysterious occult practices as part of the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) that culminated in a drug-fuelled mental breakdown in the late ’90s, to his reemergence in the new millennium as one of the leading voices in the neoreactionary movement’s Dark Enlightenment. Philosophically speaking, however, perhaps Land’s greatest claim to fame is his peculiar proposition that *capitalism is AI*. To be sure, he never puts it quite so straightforwardly. Rather, this is a simplification of a passage from his 1993 essay, “[Machinic Desire](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09502369308582177)”:

*What appears to humanity as the history of capitalism is an invasion from the future by an artificial intelligent space that must assemble itself entirely from its enemy’s resources. Digitocommodification is the index of a cyberpositively escalating technovirus, of the planetary technocapital singularity: a self-organizing insidious traumatism, virtually guiding the entire biological desiring-complex towards post-carbon replicator usurpation*.

There is much here that is beyond the scope of this short essay: from a Deleuzo-Guattarian account of machines as possessing desires and drives of their own, to a retrocausal conception of time according to which the future influences and determines the present paradoxically in advance of its own becoming. At the Incredible Machines conference held in Vancouver, Canada in 2014, Land himself fortunately condensed—microchip miniaturization style—the key claim I want to focus on: “there’s only really been one question, to be honest, that has guided everything I’ve been interested in for the last twenty years, which is: the teleological identity of capitalism and artificial intelligence.”

Until very recently, such statements tended to be the stuff of bargain-bin science fiction rather than shelved in the philosophy section. Now that the United States’ GDP growth is largely propped up by a feverish bubble of AI investment and speculation, Land’s seemingly far-fetched speculation has shown itself to be strikingly prescient. But he was actually getting at something stranger than the now vindicated, if banal prediction that some capitalist companies are inevitably going to bet their bottom dollars on AI. As he put it in a 2016 lecture, capitalism itself and AI are “basically the same thing being seen from different aspects.”

As oddly prophetic as this proposition might seem, there are a couple of notable historical precedents. From the title of Land’s “Machinic Desire” essay, we can see that one of the most significant influences on his thought are the French philosophers—or what he calls “cyberneticists”—[Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari](https://iai.tv/articles/the-quantum-world-reveals-reality-is-made-of-relations-not-objects-auid-3501). In their 1972 book—or, in Land’s terms, “engineering manual”—[*Anti-Oedipus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/305132/anti-oedipus-by-gilles-deleuze-and-felix-guattari-preface-by-michel-foucault-introduction-by-mark-seem-translated-by-robert-hurley-mark-seem-and-helen-r-lane/), Deleuze and Guattari explicitly tell us that not only capitalism but more generally “the social machine” is “literally a machine, irrespective of any metaphor.”

Another precedent is the Nobel Prize-winning economist F. A. Hayek, whose book [*The Constitution of Liberty*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo9253956.html) (1960) Margaret Thatcher once slammed down on a table at a UK Conservative Party meeting, declaring “this is what we believe.” Drawing on the then cutting-edge field of cybernetics, Hayek reconceived the market’s invisible hand as a self-organizing cybernetic intelligent system, which processes information more efficiently than any government planners or indeed any humans can through the feedback mechanism of competitive price signals. In a seminal 1945 essay, “[The Use of Knowledge in Society](https://home.uchicago.edu/~vlima/courses/econ200/spring01/hayek.pdf),” he writes that “it is more than a metaphor to describe the price system as a kind of machinery for registering change.” Though Land mentions Hayek nowhere near as much as Deleuze and Guattari, when I brought up the Iron Lady and Ronald Reagan’s favorite economist [in a 2026 interview on my Substack *Architechtonics*](https://vincentl3.substack.com/p/a-conversation-with-nick-land-part-a4f), he acknowledged: “I really think Hayek is the guy for all the reasons that we’ve already said. His instincts are just in the tradition of spontaneous order so perfectly.”

Despite Hayek and Deleuze and Guattari having drawn some connections, it is fair to say that only Land has made the identification between capitalism and cybernetic machines—or what today we call AI—quite so emphatically. Notwithstanding other possible interpretations, I want to propose one way I have made sense of this parallel. To that end, we first need to break down what is meant by AI and what is meant by capitalism.

\_\_\_

Seeing as the recursive improvement of increasingly autonomous machines is at the heart of both capitalism and the technological singularity, it stands to reason that they might be one and the same process.

\_\_\_

The term AI has two common meanings. On the one hand, it refers to the modern research programme of making machines, like computer programs and robots, intelligent. On the other hand, it refers to those intelligent machines themselves. In the latter sense of the term, “artificial” refers to things that are made or designed rather than occurring naturally. A specifically artificial intelligence, then, denotes an intelligence that is neither human, nor biological, nor natural. “Intelligence” is more difficult to define as there are many more competing conceptions. If we nonetheless zoom in, like an astrophysicist looking for signs of alien life through her telescope, on just how the word intelligence is commonly used in the modern AI research programme, it typically refers to an agent or system that can perceive, think, and act. That is to say, a machine can be said to be intelligent if it is able to take in inputs from the external world through sensors of some kind, in some sense “think” or “reason” about them through various information processing operations, and then output a series of responses and actions that are most likely to help it successfully achieve its goals.

This broad sense of AI is often broken down into four further classes or types. “Weak” or “narrow AI” refers to all currently existing AIs that can competently perform the perceive-think-act sequence in specific, narrowly defined domains, such as in solving mathematical equations or playing chess. This is in contrast to “strong AI,” “artificial general intelligence,” or “AGI.” While there is less consensus on what AGI means, it is often defined as an AI that is able to autonomously learn and competently perform a wide range of different tasks in real open-world environments, all without human intervention. Another way of putting this is that an AGI is an AI that is at least as proficient at as many complex tasks as us humans. This is because we humans are the leading example of a general intelligence, or so we think in our moments of greatest hubris.

It is neither weak nor strong AI but a third thing that can really be fused with capitalism. “Artificial superintelligence” or “ASI” refers to the more speculative hypothesis that an AGI with human-level intelligence would become more intelligent than humans, because it would have larger memory storage capacity, greater processing power, and feel no hunger, thirst, or exhaustion to slow it down. Consequently, it would be able to rewrite its own code and improve itself better than any human could. Moreover, the improved AGI would be able to go full galaxy brain meme and improve itself even better again, with the even more improved AGI improving itself once more, and so on seemingly ad infinitum. This positive feedback loop of recursive self-improvement is often referred to as “the technological singularity” or “intelligence explosion,” because it would radically exceed our understanding as per other singularities, such as the centre of a black hole, the advent of the universe at the Big Bang, or the mere sight of a Lovecraftian abomination. In a 1970 essay called “[Some Future Social Repercussions of Computers](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00207237008709398),” mathematician and cryptologist I. J. Good describes the intelligence explosion (a term he coined) with all the excitement of a mad scientist stumbling upon new life:

*Once a general-purpose intelligent machine is produced, then at say twice the expense we shall be able to produce a very intelligent machine with hardly any additional complexity. … It can then be trained in the theory of machine construction and will be able to produce a much better machine. In this manner, or otherwise, we shall arrive at an ultraintelligent machine, which is defined as a machine that is better at every intellectual feat than any man. Then it too can be used for the further design of machines, and this will give rise to the intelligence explosion mentioned earlier. The first intelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make since it will lead, without further human invention, to the ultraintelligent machine and the intelligence explosion.*

The immense capabilities of an artificial superintelligence have led many AI researchers to see the need for a fourth type of AI called “friendly artificial intelligence” or “FAI”; that is, an ASI or AGI specifically programmed to serve humanity’s interest, happiness, and prosperity, however these may be defined. In short, think something along the lines of *Star Wars*’ R2-D2 for weak AI, *Ex Machina*’s Ava for AGI, Lovecraft’s ineffably Great Old Ones (or, as we shall see, capitalism) for ASI, and Pixar’s adorably wide-eyed WALL-E for FAI.

Now, theories of capitalism are even more hotly contested than definitions of intelligence. It is nonetheless the case that theorists across the political spectrum—from Austrian economists like Hayek to Marxists—frequently emphasize one crucial component of capitalism: seeing as no capitalist corporation exists in a vacuum but must compete with others on the market, they have no choice but to invest the vast bulk of their profits, like [sacrificial offerings to a needy god](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-moloch-and-the-race-to-the-bottom-auid-2509), into improving the means of production so that they are more efficient than their rivals at producing better goods at cheaper costs.

\_\_\_

The technological singularity already took place among a few Dutch and English experiments in agricultural and industrial production during the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries.

\_\_\_

More precisely, this relentless revolutionization of the productive forces consists in improving technology and upgrading machinery so that corporations will have to pay workers less. Given this incentive to invest in technology that is capable of performing work at ever-increasing efficiency, there is a general tendency towards the automation of work as human labor power is supplemented and even outright replaced by machines. As Marx explains in the third volume of [*Capital: A Critique of Political Economy*](https://archive.org/details/capitalcritiqueo03marx) (1894):

*As soon as the new production method begins to spread, and thereby to furnish tangible proof that these commodities can actually be produced more cheaply, the capitalists working with the old methods of production must sell their product below its full price of production, because the value of this commodity has fallen, and because the labor time required by them to produce it is greater than the social average. In one word—and this appears as an effect of competition—these capitalists must also introduce the new method of production, in which the proportion of variable [i.e. human labor] to constant capital [machinery and other means of production] has been reduced.*

What’s more, since profits tend to increase through the automation of the productive forces, those profits are reinvested into further automation to generate even greater profits. As more and more of the assembly line is automated, we get another positive feedback circuit whereby machines produce more surplus value that is invested into improving themselves, with the improved machines producing even more value that is reinvested into improving themselves again, and so on without cease. In [*Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy*](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.190072) (1942), the liberal economist Joseph Schumpeter makes a similar observation to Marx from the other side of the barricades:

*The history of the productive apparatus of a typical farm, from the beginnings of the rationalization of crop rotation, plowing and fattening to the mechanized thing of today—linking up with elevators and railroads—is a history of revolutions. So is the history of the productive apparatus of the iron and steel industry from the charcoal furnace to our own type of furnace, or the history of the apparatus of power production from the overshot water wheel to the modern power plant, or the history of transportation from the mailcoach to the airplane. The opening up of new markets, foreign or domestic, and the organizational development from the craft shop and factory to such concerns as U.S. Steel illustrate the same process of industrial mutation—if I may use that biological term—that incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, incessantly destroying the old one, incessantly creating a new one. This process of Creative Destruction is the essential fact about capitalism. It is what capitalism consists in and what every capitalist concern has got to live in.*

In a bolt and nutshell, economic progress does not proceed from monkey to man but from man to monkey wrench. In modern times, we are all Charlie Chaplin, bumbling our way into becoming cyborgs.

We have now arrived at the devil’s crossroads where an unholy pact between capitalism and AI can be forged. We have seen that the technological singularity marks the moment when autonomous machines start improving themselves, with the improved machines improving themselves again, et cetera. We have also seen that capitalist competition compels corporations to improve and automate the technological means of production to generate profits, which are then reinvested into further improving and automating the productive forces, et cetera. Seeing as the recursive improvement of increasingly autonomous machines is at the heart of both capitalism and the technological singularity, it stands to reason that they might be one and the same process. This is what Land is getting at when he writes—in dense yet exhilarating ’90s cyberpunk essays like “Machinic Desire” and “[Meat (or How to Kill Oedipus in Cyberspace)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1357034X95001003011)” (1995)—that, in “reaching an escape velocity of self-reinforcing machinic intelligence propagation, the forces of production are going for the revolution on their own”:

*Industrialization is on one side an autonomization of productive apparatus, and on the other a cyborgian becoming-machine of work-forces, following the logistically accelerating rhythm of pluggings and unpluggings that constitutes the proletariat as a detraditionalized economic resource. Technical machinery invades the body; routinizing, reprogramming and plasticizing it.*

*Far from being an internal property or quality of labor, productivity indexes the dehumanization of cyborg labor-power. As regenerative commoditization deploys technics to substitute for human activity accounted as wage costs, it obsolesces the animal, the organism and every kind of somatic unity, not just in theory, but in reality; by tricking, outflanking and breaking down corporeal defences.*

It is no wonder that computer scientist and science-fiction writer, Vernor Vinge, popularized the term “technological Singularity” in a [1993 essay](https://cmm.cenart.gob.mx/delanda/textos/tech_sing.pdf), precisely to name the time when “we will see automation replacing higher and higher level jobs”: “in the coming of the Singularity, we are seeing the predictions of *true* technological unemployment finally come true.” As this socioeconomic revolution is outsourced, it is neither the proletariat (as it is for Marx) nor the bourgeoisie (as it is for Schumpeter) who, in generating surplus value, is the revolutionary subject of world-history. It is the machines that come to collectively produce, appropriate, and consume the vast bulk of the surplus in order to fulfil their own needs. In this sense, capitalism could be described as communism for machines. In a perverse instantiation of *The Communist Manifesto*’s final rallying cry, it is as if the machines want to say: chains of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but the humans to which you are bound!

One way I have found it helpful to think about this identification of capitalism and AI is as a software update on [Marx’s reading of German idealist philosophers like Hegel](https://iai.tv/articles/hegel-vs-marx-ideas-change-the-world-not-economics-auid-3244). According to Marx, Hegel’s absolute idealism is a more or less accurate description of a real process. The trouble with Hegel is that he thought he was describing the dialectical movement of absolute spirit or reason. Marx turned Hegel on his head by reconceiving this dialectical movement as a theoretical or ideological reflection of real material and historical dynamics in the economic forces and relations of production.

In a similar hermeneutical hacking, I would suggest that AI researchers’ speculations about a hypothetical artificial superintelligence are more or less accurately describing something real. Only, they err in imagining that this is merely one possibility lying up ahead in the far-flung science-fiction future. On the contrary, the technological singularity already took place among a few Dutch and English experiments in agricultural and industrial production during the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, before self-organizing into the dominant mode of production that defines all of modernity. Google’s chief techno-optimist Ray Kurzweil’s infamous 2045 prediction for the advent of the singularity—in [*The Singularity is Near: When Humans Transcend Biology*](https://web.archive.org/web/20140316213301/http:/hfg-resources.googlecode.com/files/SingularityIsNear.pdf)(2005)—has often been derided for being far too optimistic. If capitalism is an already existing AI or even a superintelligence in the making, however, then Kurzweil’s prediction should actually be criticized for being *far too pessimistic*, if not outright anachronistic.

While Land’s great achievement is to have fused AI and capitalism more forcefully than anyone before him, I believe that no one has fully extrapolated all of the consequences implied therein. To give just one example—which is the focus of my current research—reimagining capitalism as a really existing AGI seems to entail that the [history of modern economics](https://iai.tv/video/everything-we-knew-about-economics-is-wrong) can also be reinterpreted as a history of AI research and development. We could therefore look to a free-market liberal tradition like Hayek and Schumpeter’s Austrian School for an account of a runaway superintelligence that has escaped human regulation and control. We could also look to Marxism and Keynesianism for historical attempts at realizing the friendly AI project and solving the value alignment problem by regulating and subordinating the capitalist superintelligence to human planning and oversight. It is no surprise that the likes of Bernie Sanders and the Democratic Socialists have recently formed a united front with [Eliezer Yudkowsky and the AI Safetyists](https://iai.tv/video/ai-and-the-end-of-humanity), with both proposing greater state regulation and international governance of AI. Once science-fiction speculations about a coming artificial superintelligence are turned on their head, the father of cyberpunk, William Gibson, appears right to have said that “the future is already here—it’s just not very evenly distributed yet.”
