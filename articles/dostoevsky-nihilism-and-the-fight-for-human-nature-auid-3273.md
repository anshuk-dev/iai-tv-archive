# Dostoevsky, nihilism, and the fight for human nature

*Russian nihilism on trial*

**By Gary Saul Morson** | *31st July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,158 words*

![Dostoevsky, nihilism, and the fight for human nature](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Dostoevsky-nihilism-and-the-fight-for-human-nature2.webp)

---

*Science will usher in a utopia and should be worshipped, so believed the 19th-century Russian nihilists. Their ideas would later inspire the Bolsheviks—Lenin even used the title of Chernyshevsky's* What Is to Be Done? *for his own revolutionary pamphlet. But in seeking to explain away human complexity with scientific certainty, did the Russian nihilists radically misunderstand human nature? And are we repeating their mistakes today? In this article, Slavist and literary critic Gary Saul Morson contrasts the science-worshipping Russian nihilists with Tolstoy, Turgenev, and Dostoevsky, who, he argues, revealed a world too intricate and mysterious to ever be fully understood.*

“Nihilist” and “nihilism”—terms typically attributed to novelist Ivan Turgenev—originally referred to a group that arose in Russia around 1860. Today we often call people nihilistic if they extend no hope that conditions can improve. Unqualified pessimists, they regard all grounds for optimism as illusory. We also use the term “nihilism” to describe extreme relativism about the bases of human knowledge. Science, in this view, is just another ideology, based, like all ideologies, on the interests of a ruling class. Accepted knowledge is nothing more than power made into a philosophy justifying it. This kind of nihilism often interprets various philosophers—Hume, Nietzsche, Marx, Freud, Feyerabend, and others—as justifying the claim that one can build on no certain “foundations.”

Neither understanding of nihilism applies to the original Russian nihilists. Far from despairing, they believed that they knew just how to build the perfect society, which, they also held, could be realized in a few years. Regarding “science” as a set of infallible (and mostly metaphysical) dogmas, they deemed their favored social theories scientific and therefore utterly beyond doubt. As their critics observed, these science worshippers missed the whole point of science, openness to contrary evidence.

The group’s leader, Nikolai Chernyshevsky (1828-1889), exercised immense influence. His utopian fiction, *What Is to Be Done?* (1863)—the question was anything but rhetorical—became the most widely read book among the intelligentsia before the Revolution. Lenin credited it with making him a revolutionary, and the Soviets hailed Chernyshevsky as a thinker in the same league as Marx and Engels. Tolstoy, on the other hand, referred to him as “that gentleman who stinks of bedbugs,” a loathsome figure who has persuaded his followers that “to be outraged, bilious, and spiteful is a commendable thing.”

A conversation among Chernyshevsky, Turgenev, and Dostoevsky illuminates nihilist theses and their critics’ objections. In response to Chernyshevsky’s article “The Russian at the Rendez-vous” (1858), a critique of Turgenev’s novella “Asya,” Turgenev published *Fathers and Children* (1862), which was in turn answered by *What Is to Be Done?*. Dostoevsky’s *Notes from Underground* (1864) then mercilessly parodied *What Is to Be Done?*.

Chernyshevsky’s article develops a comprehensive, putatively scientific, theory of the human condition, a theory opposed not only to Turgenev’s views but also to the presuppositions of the realist novel as a genre. Such novels, according to Chernyshevsky, reflected ideas from the pre-scientific past.

\_\_\_

“You have practically reached the limits of human wisdom,” Chernyshevsky concluded, “when you become convinced of the simple truth that every person is exactly like every other one.”

\_\_\_

Novels presuppose human individuality, but in Chernyshevsky’s opinion, there is no such thing. If you observe people closely, he explained, you “will soon come to the conclusion that every man is like every other and that each is made up precisely like the others . . . The differences seem important only because they appear on the surface and strike your eye, but beneath the visible, apparent differences is hidden a total identity.”

Anyone familiar with modern science would ask: “Why should a person really prove to be a contradiction of all nature’s laws? In nature cedars and hyssops feed and bloom, elephants and mice move and eat, are angry and glad, according to the same laws.” Not only are all members of a species the same, so are different species: “Beneath an external similarity of form, there is an inner identity of the organism of monkey and whale, eagle and chicken . . . the organism of mammals, birds, and fish is identical . . . [and] even a worm breathes like a mammal.”

Realist fiction notwithstanding, therefore, people differ from each other only in trivial ways. “If we take two healthy people . . . the pulse of one will naturally beat a little faster and a little stronger than the other’s. But is that difference great? It is so insignificant that even science fails to pay any attention to it.” Chernyshevsky was a nihilist in the sense that he denied his culture’s supposedly unenlightened governing assumptions, particularly those that attributed complexity and obscurity to a world that is, as he saw it, fundamentally simple and comprehensible.

“You have practically reached the limits of human wisdom,” Chernyshevsky concluded, “when you become convinced of the simple truth that every person is exactly like every other one.” Placed in the same circumstances, each person would behave in the very same way. We know that, Chernyshevsky argues, because science has proven several truths that should, in any case, be plain to every unprejudiced person. First, the world, including human beings, is governed by iron deterministic laws. Whatever happens had to happen. If so, there is no such thing as free will. To be sure, we have the sensation of choosing, but that sensation is itself the product of the same laws and can have only one outcome. Second, all human behavior follows a single law: people always strive to maximize their own advantage. If you want to change human behavior, all you can do is correct people’s views about how to be selfish more adroitly. Third, the only standard for judging actions is utility. Fourth, people are not responsible for what they do since they could have done nothing else. “One ought not to blame people for anything at any cost.” What is called “crime” is merely “misfortune,” a mistaken idea of how to maximize utility. That mistake reflects bad social circumstances, which, if changed, would eliminate both crime and misfortune. It’s all so simple: just follow these principles, and utopia is on the horizon.

As the nihilists saw things, art as we know it obscures these scientific truths and so is, in its present form, entirely useless. As a common slogan proclaimed, “Boots are more important than Pushkin.” Indeed, most art is not only useless but positively pernicious. Only art that inculcates the right principles can be justified. Unfortunately, Turgenev and those like him focus on how individuals fall in love. “Forget about them, those erotic questions!” Chernyshevsky advises. “They are not for a reader of our time, occupied with problems of administrative and judiciary improvements, of financial reforms.”

Dostoevsky never ceased to skewer the nihilists

\_\_\_

Bazarov, the hero of Turgenev’s *Fathers and Children*, argues all these points eloquently. “I assure you, studying separate individuals is not worth the trouble,” he declares. “All people resemble each other in soul as in body . . . and the so-called moral qualities are the same in all . . . People are like trees in a forest; no botanist would think of studying every individual birch tree.” Romantic love, he declares, is nonsense. “What about all those mysterious relations between a man and a woman? We physiologists know what these relations are . . . That’s all romanticism, nonsense, rot, artiness.”

Bazarov denies the assumptions about people, love, and meaning on which realist novels depend, but he is himself a character in such a novel, which subjects his opinions to unwelcome scrutiny. The plot shows they are too simple to account for reality. To his utter surprise, Bazarov falls in love and, despite his denial of individuality, loves a particular person. He fully realizes that his experience contradicts his beliefs. Something “was taking root in him, something he had never admitted, at which he had always jeered, at which all his pride revolted.” If Turgenev were a lesser writer, he would have demonstrated the foolishness of his characters’ opinions and left it at that. Instead, he depicts Bazarov as genuinely heroic. Unlike most of us, who explain away disconfirmation, Bazarov has the courage to recognize his mistakes. When he dies young, we mourn the loss of an admirable, if mistaken, individual.

Most nihilists regarded Turgenev’s novel as slanderous. Chernyshevsky replied with his utopian story *What Is to Be Done?*. Unlike most utopias, this one explicitly makes fun of realist novels and rebukes readers who enjoy such drivel. Chernyshevsky’s heroes and heroines have no doubt that people always behave selfishly and that this selfishness, if thoroughgoing, must lead to the perfect world pictured in the heroine’s vatic dreams. Chernyshevsky’s characters can be sure of each other’s behavior, since people, as rational agents, are wholly predictable. So influential was this book that for generations, young people modeled their living arrangements, dress, and love life on the novel’s heroes and heroines.

\_\_\_

We are not just “piano keys” or “organ stops” played upon by the laws of nature.

\_\_\_

Dostoevsky never ceased to skewer the nihilists. Their smugness, contempt for art, and denial of individual responsibility appalled him. Followers of Chernyshevsky appear in all his major novels, and *Notes from Underground* parodies *What Is to Be Done?* in detail. Part Two of *Notes* retells Chernyshevsky’s most famous incidents by showing how flesh-and-blood people with real psychology would actually behave. In the famous first part of *Notes*, Dostoevsky’s man from underground offers brilliant arguments against the views Chernyshevsky pronounced irrefutable.

A scientist is supposed to respect empirical data, the man from underground reminds us, but look around you: don’t you often see people act irrationally, deliberately failing to pursue what they know to be advantageous? Theories like Chernyshevsky’s are “mere logical exercises . . . But man is so fond of systems and abstract deductions that he is ready to distort the truth intentionally, he is ready to deny what he can see and hear just to justify his logic.”

A key problem with the “advantage” theory is that it misunderstands advantage itself. Its proponents construct a list of desirable things, but there is also a meta-level, where the whole list is called into question. One’s “most advantageous advantage” not only *can* but sometimes *must* run counter to the whole list. The proponents of the advantage theory do not even recognize the existence of this “most advantageous advantage,” but it defines what makes us human. We all feel that our human dignity and sense of selfhood depend on not being a mere object, like some stone. If people resembled stones, you could construct something resembling “a table of logarithms up to 108,000” specifying what each person would do at every moment. That sort of knowledge, indeed, is what utopians count on. They build their “crystal palace” for interchangeable objects with no individuality because genuine human difference, including contradictory values, would make it impossible to satisfy everyone’s desires.

\_\_\_

What the Russian nihilists denied, above all, was the complexity of things. The great Russian novelists, in response, revealed a world so intricate and obscure that people will never wholly understand it.

\_\_\_

What each person values above all else is his or her humanness, which consists in *not* being entirely predictable. Humanness lies in the capacity to surprise, to render untrue any external definition. We are not just “piano keys” or “organ stops” played upon by the laws of nature. We want our lives to be truly our own; we want to *do* things, not just have things happen to us—do things that would not happen unless we freely chose them.

Put people in the utopian’s world where all is determined for them, and, even if that world actually did provide abundant material advantages, people would soon reject it precisely because they would feel that their lives had been taken away from them. No matter the cost, they would choose choice. Rebellion against utopia: that theme was to shape some of the great twentieth-century dystopian novels, including Eugene Zamyatin’s *We*, which founded the genre and alludes constantly to *Notes from Underground*.

How does one demonstrate that one cannot be wholly accounted for? By acting contrary to one’s own advantage just to show that one can do so! The underground man calls such behavior “spite,” and he governs his life accordingly.

The nihilists believed they could create the perfect society because, like all utopians, they saw the world as fundamentally simple. Complexity, in their view, is an illusion invented by vested interests to discourage people from implementing obvious solutions. Countless utopias tell the story of a person who overcomes his belief that the complexity of the world condemns people to misery when he at last realizes that the simple solution to all problems is always at hand. The realist novel of ideas describes the opposite journey, from simplistic beliefs to an appreciation of complexity.

What the Russian nihilists denied, above all, was the complexity of things. The great Russian novelists, in response, revealed a world so intricate and obscure that people will never wholly understand it. Instead of certainty, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, and Turgenev cultivated wonder.
