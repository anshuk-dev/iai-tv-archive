# Spacetime does not emerge from quantum physics

*Quantum mechanics does not give rise to Einstein's spacetime*

**By Sam Baron** | *21st April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,387 words*

![Spacetime does not emerge from quantum physics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/spacetime-does-not-exist.webp)

---

*Einstein’s picture of spacetime, a four-dimensional fabric uniting space and time, breaks down at the smallest scales, where quantum mechanics takes over. For decades, physicists have searched for an account of how spacetime “emerges” from this deeper quantum reality. But philosopher **Sam Baron** argues that the whole idea of spacetime “emerging” from something else makes no sense. All our accounts of how things “emerge” from something more fundamental presuppose spacetime, so the idea that spacetime itself emerges is circular. His radical conclusion is that we must abandon the project of reconciling spacetime and quantum mechanics, and accept that spacetime, at least as Einstein described it, does not exist.*

I don’t think spacetime exists. I know how that sounds. Pretty silly, right? We are very used to thinking of spacetime as the fabric of the universe. We are also pretty used to thinking of it as [the arena in which we live our day-to-day lives](https://iai.tv/articles/spacetime-is-an-idea-not-a-reality-auid-3281). The loss of spacetime would thus seem to be devastating. Wouldn’t it effectively mean the annihilation of the universe? Or, at least, wouldn’t it be hard to plan for anything, since without spacetime there’d be no future and no sense in which there’s anything to come?

Hear me out.

**A quest in physics**

The early twentieth century was transformational for physics. We developed the general theory of relativity and quantum theory. Despite the incredible success of these two theories, physicists are hell-bent on replacing them with a single theory of quantum gravity.

Why? Well, put simply, [these two theories don’t provide a complete picture of the world](https://iai.tv/articles/the-crisis-of-quantum-gravity-auid-1769). General relativity provides an account of how gravity works in terms of the curvature of spacetime. The incredible success of the theory leads us to believe that the world is as the theory describes and thus that spacetime is real. But [while the theory is extremely successful at large scales, it simply doesn’t work when we try to apply it to extremely small scales where quantum effects dominate](https://philpapers.org/archive/BARESW-3.pdf). Since quantum theory has little to say about gravity, this means we don’t have a story of gravitation that reaches all the way into the quantum domain.

\_\_\_

We need spacetime to explain how spacetime emerges, and that looks circular in a bad way.

\_\_\_

There are many approaches to developing a quantum theory of gravity, and not much agreement on what the “right” approach might be. In a number of such theories, however, it seems that spacetime as we know it disappears at the very fine levels of detail where quantum gravitation is expected to apply. In some sense, this is not all that surprising. General relativity, as a theory, must give way. When theories give way like this, we often discover entirely new physics lurking below and revise the way we think about the world.

**Emergence**

Matters are not quite so simple, however. General relativity is a very good theory: it is extremely well confirmed. So, we can’t just throw it away. There are a couple of options for what to do.

One option is to take general relativity to be a completely true theory of a particular sub-domain of the universe, namely the domain in which quantum effects don’t dominate. Because general relativity seems to require the existence of spacetime to be true, we could then draw the conclusion that spacetime, as described by general relativity, exists but only within that domain. Since general relativity does not apply universally, we could then say that there are domains in which spacetime doesn’t exist.

This is to treat [spacetime as *emergent*](https://iai.tv/articles/can-time-emerge-from-a-timeless-world-auid-3084). An [emergent phenomenon](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-emergence), very roughly, is one that arises from something more basic. Consider the wetness of water. Water is a liquid: it flows and drips and makes things wet. But H2O molecules considered on their own aren’t like this: they aren’t liquid. The properties of liquids emerge at what we call the macro scale.

So, we could treat [spacetime as emergent](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-017-1349-4) in a similar way. It exists at some level, but doesn’t exist at deeper levels of reality.

But I don’t like this option very much. The trouble is that we are then on the hook for explaining the emergence of spacetime. [Emergence can be tricky](https://iai.tv/articles/the-absurdity-of-emergence-auid-2552). We have a pretty good sense of how emergence works for liquids: when H2O molecules are arranged and interact with each other in the right ways, they form a liquid at the macro scale.

This story of emergence won’t obviously work for spacetime itself. That’s because the story presupposes spacetime. What is an “arrangement”? On the face of it, arrangements are spatiotemporal: they involve a specification of the location in spacetime of whatever one is arranging. Similarly, causal interaction is commonly understood as a process in spacetime. Given this, we can’t clearly say that spacetime emerges in virtue of the arrangement and causal interaction of something more basic—so-called “atoms” of spacetime—since that would seem to presuppose spacetime in the explaining.

The problem is quite general. Pretty much every account of emergence we’ve managed to come up with so far [seems to rely on spacetime](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1004563122154). This isn’t that surprising: spacetime is a powerful framework for modelling a range of notions, emergence included. But it does pose a problem if we want to take spacetime as emergent. For then we need spacetime to explain how spacetime emerges, and that looks circular in a bad way.

**Elimination**

Fortunately, there’s another option, and that is to do away with spacetime altogether. Rather than taking general relativity to be a true theory that describes the world in some domain—the domain where spacetime emerges and thus exists—we treat general relativity as an approximation. This means that the theory isn’t ever quite true of the world, but it [captures things closely enough](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/truthlikeness/) that we don’t get into trouble when we use it in certain scenarios, namely those where it performs best. Because the theory is not true, and only ever a decent approximation, there is nothing that exactly answers to general relativity: the world does not really feature spacetime.

In a way, this is not so strange. We take this view already, with Newtonian physics. Newtonian physics is really quite good, so long as you are moving relatively slowly in a weak gravitational field. But we don’t think that the world *is* Newtonian in any deep sense. Indeed, we are more than willing to accept that really the world is as described by the theory that superseded it: general relativity. So Newtonian theory is a mere approximation, and not one that we should take as a guide to the nature of reality. Equally, perhaps general relativity is a mere approximation, and not one that should guide how we think of the world.

**Problems?**

So that’s the sense in which I think we can eliminate spacetime: the world is not really spatiotemporal, but we can treat it as such and get away with it for the most part.

At this point, people typically want to respond by messing around with the definition of spacetime. The idea is to concede that nothing exists *exactly as described by general relativity*. But what exists *is close enough* to spacetime that we can say that spacetime exists. Near enough is good enough.

This move is always available when we make new discoveries. For instance, we used to think that color is a property that objects have intrinsically. But when we went looking for such a property, we failed to find it. What we found, instead, was a range of responses that we have to objects under certain conditions that seem to correlate with the colors we see.

One option is to just [eliminate colors: nothing is colored! It’s just an illusion!](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1746-8361.2006.01058.x) Another option is to just shift our definition of color to be broad enough to include relationships between observers and objects. Redness is not *in objects*, it’s sort of in us, by virtue of the way that we interact with objects. But that’s close enough to what we used to think about color to be able to say that colors are real.

Whether this is the right response typically depends on how wild the truth turns out to be. The history of science is littered with the remains of things that didn’t make the cut: aether, phlogiston, caloric, miasma. Things that we once believed were real, but later eliminated because the world was just too different to justify keeping them around.

Where do we stand with spacetime? If a theory of quantum gravity paints a radically different picture of the world, then a commitment to spacetime may not be sustainable, and then elimination will be the thing to do. If the picture is close enough, though, then perhaps spacetime will remain.

**The future of spacetime**

For my part, I’m betting on a big revolution. Suggestions for what a theory of quantum gravity might look like are pretty wild: [strings](https://iai.tv/articles/in-defence-of-string-theory-auid-2522), [spin-foams](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-19-3079-9_99-1), [causal sets](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41114-019-0023-1) and many more exotic beasts have been proposed as the basic ingredients of reality. Moreover, physics likes to get rid of things. A lean metaphysics is pretty much the ideal. So, I think spacetime’s days are numbered: we need to make room at the zoo for more exotic creatures.

For now, though, it’s business as usual. General relativity remains an extremely useful theory, and so we continue to think of the world as spatiotemporal because that suits our purposes.

**Realism and anti-realism**

At this point, one might wonder whether taking this view on general relativity is compatible with a realist attitude toward science. Roughly speaking, realists take our best theories of the world to correspond to reality, and thus treat the things they talk about—things like spacetime—to be real features of the world. General relativity is an extremely good theory and one might think that if scientific realism were ever warranted, it would be about a theory like this.

There are two potential responses to this line of thought. The first is to just give up on scientific realism and endorse [anti-realism of some kind](https://iai.tv/articles/post-realism-auid-1755). Actually, I’m pretty sympathetic to [scientific anti-realism](https://iai.tv/articles/science-does-not-describe-reality-auid-2724), but I don’t think that it has to follow from what I’ve said here.

This brings us to the second response, which is to maintain a version of realism despite refusing to take on the commitments of a very good theory. For scientific realism to be viable, this kind of view has to be available. That’s because the history of science contains a great many very good scientific theories that turned out to carry commitments that we ultimately reject.

\_\_\_

Something doesn’t need to be real to play an important role in our lives. What matters is that believing in that thing, whatever it is, is useful.

\_\_\_

One example relevant to the current discussion is Newtonian mechanics. This is a very good theory, but it carries with it a commitment to absolute time: roughly, a single global clock that marks the passage of time everywhere across the entire universe. One of the things relativity seems to suggest is that this commitment is not sustainable. But, still, Newtonian mechanics is a very good theory, and we want to be able to use it without taking on any questionable commitments. Thus, well before we get to relativity or quantum gravity, we know that the realist must make room for theories that are very good, but that we don’t take to be perfect guides to reality.

In a sense, this response is programmatic: it says that for realism to be viable, the very attitude I’m recommending toward general relativity should be already available as an option. The question though is *how* can a realist admit this attitude. Answering this question would take us deep into the finer details of how to be a scientific realist. One option, though, is that a realist commitment to everything found in a given theory should be reserved only for our most basic or fundamental theories. Since neither general relativity nor Newtonian mechanics are fundamental theories, the realist attitude can be suspended to some degree, in the manner I’m suggesting, without descending into full-blown anti-realism.

**Is there really no future?**

One last thing: given what I’ve said about spacetime, is there really no future? Does what I say force us to give up on our [common-sense conceptions](https://philpapers.org/rec/SELPAT) of space and time that seem to play such a significant role in our lives? I want to pull these questions apart a bit. Focusing on the second question first, something doesn’t need to be real to play an important role in our lives. What matters is that believing in that thing, whatever it is, is useful.

A case in point is *morality*. Many philosophers maintain that there are [no objective moral truths](https://iai.tv/articles/moving-past-morality-auid-2943) (for reasons I won’t go into here). But no-one thinks we should then go out and start murdering each other. Why? Because it serves our *pragmatic interests* to not do that. It is very useful for all of us if we believe and act as though there are moral truths. Similarly, when it comes to planning, it’s very useful for us if we believe and act as though there’s a future to plan for, since it seems that this practice has served us very well in the past, despite whatever deep metaphysical truths there might be about the world.

So, I think that *even if* there’s no future, there never has been and yet we’ve managed perfectly well. Moreover, it would be quite bad if we all suddenly stopped believing that the future exists.

But I also don’t think that denying the existence of spacetime means that we have to give up our common-sense notions of space and time. That’s because these conceptions could be “functional” conceptions, in this sense: something counts as space or time in this common-sense way if it is capable of supporting certain aspects of our lives in the right way. What way is that? Well, planning looks pretty important! So long as the world has something in it that enables us to plan effectively, then I think we can take our common sense views about space and time to be satisfied. It might just be that *what* satisfies them will surprise us all, because it may not be the spacetime that we know and love.

Actually, my view is that what plays this role is causation, and that causal structure is the most basic kind of structure there is, with space and time as they figure in our experience (but not spacetime, as it figures in Einstein’s theory of relativity) being emergent phenomena. But that’s a tale for another day.
