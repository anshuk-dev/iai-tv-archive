# Reality is a dream not a simulation

*Life as a dream is more than metaphor*

**By Andrew T. Jaffe** | *28th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,346 words*

![Reality is a dream not a simulation](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/reality-is-a-dream-not-a-simulation.webp)

---

*Most physicists are materialists who believe the world consists of physical particles at the fundamental level. Others have argued reality is a simulation or a hallucination of the brain. But **Andrew T. Jaffe** challenges all of these views, proposing an alternative consciousness-first theory where space and time arise as within a dream.*

Most people never stop to consider how bizarre it is to exist at all. We live inside the fact of consciousness so continuously that we rarely examine it. Yet to be alive, aware, and conscious in an apparently material universe is not self-explanatory. It is the deepest mystery we know. We appear here, now, in a world of matter, space, and time, as though such a condition were normal. But perhaps the strange thing is not that [idealism](iai-academy/courses/info?course=the-case-for-idealism) sounds radical. Perhaps the strange thing is that materialism ever felt plausible.

Cognitive Dream Interface Theory, or CDIT, begins from this unease. It asks whether the one thing we know with certainty, awareness itself, should be treated as secondary to the things we only ever know through it. Materialism tells us that consciousness somehow emerges from non-aware matter. Cognitive Dream Interface Theory inverts that order. It proposes that [awareness is primary](https://iai.tv/articles/annaka-harris-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3136), and that what we call the physical world is a rendered, lawful, and shared appearance within it.

This is where the dream analogy becomes unusually powerful. Many of the central ideas of nondual philosophy are difficult to grasp in the abstract. No self, unreal time, apparent multiplicity, world as appearance: these can sound poetic, obscure, or evasive. But in dreams they become obvious. A dream can generate a world, complete with other people, landscapes, narratives, emotions, and a self who seems to inhabit it. That dream self may have no memory of the waking person in bed. It may be a student again, a stranger, or someone with a wholly different history. Yet when we wake, we immediately see the truth. The dream self was not the true subject. The dream world was not made of matter. The time and space of the dream were not ultimate. A single mind created the whole appearance.

Cognitive Dream Interface Theory proposes that waking life is not different in kind, only more stable and more coherent. That is why the analogy matters. It does not merely illustrate an idea. It demonstrates a structure.

The usual objection is that dreams are private and unstable, while waking reality is lawful and shared. But this distinction strengthens rather than weakens Cognitive Dream Interface Theory. A dream with greater coherence would look more like a world governed by physics. A shared dream would require stable constraints across many viewpoints. In that sense, lawfulness is not evidence against dream-like reality. It is exactly what a rendered experiential world would require.

What is striking is how much of modern science already points in that direction. Gravity is no longer treated as a force in the old mechanical sense, [but as geometry](https://iai.tv/articles/in-defence-of-string-theory-auid-2522). Dark matter is [not directly observed as substance](https://iai.tv/articles/a-universe-without-dark-matter-auid-3196), but inferred because, without it, galaxies would not hold together. Dark energy is not seen as an object, but functions as a global condition of expansion. The speed of light is a universal limit on causation and information. The second law of thermodynamics governs how states unfold over apparent time. More and more, the universe looks less like a machine made of pieces and more like a system governed by coherence constraints.

From the standpoint of Cognitive Dream Interface Theory, that is deeply suggestive. Dreams are not built from substances. They are held together by rules. Objects remain where they should be. Scenes persist. Bodies have weight and location. Events unfold intelligibly. Waking reality appears to do the same, only with far greater stability. Gravity stabilizes local relations. Dark matter stabilizes large-scale structure. Dark energy stabilizes the global arena. Entropy gives direction to the unfolding. Reality begins to resemble an interface governed by rules rather than a pile of self-existing things.

Biology may be revealing a similar shift. [Michael Levin’s work](https://iai.tv/articles/patterns-are-alive-and-we-are-living-patterns-auid-2919) suggests that genetic code and local chemistry do not fully explain anatomical form. Global bioelectric fields appear to constrain what kinds of structures are permitted, corrected, and maintained. Cells do not merely build from the bottom up. They seem to organize within a larger field of allowable form. This matters philosophically because it suggests that coherence may precede construction. Parts do not explain the whole as fully as we once thought.

Materialism struggles not only with physics and biology, but with the bare fact of subjectivity. No scientific theory has explained how non-experience gives rise to experience. Correlations are not derivations. Brain scans map contents, not awareness itself. This becomes particularly acute in discussions of general anesthesia. Deep anesthesia clearly disrupts memory, reportability, and large-scale integration. But it does not, by itself, prove that awareness is generated by the brain. It shows that the brain is required for coherent reportable experience in waking form. Unlike most bodily functions, which appear on measurable gradients, awareness presents itself phenomenologically as binary. Experience is either present or [absent from memory](https://iai.tv/articles/memory-is-not-stored-in-the-brain-auid-3420), but never directly encountered as partly dimmed in the way one experiences weakening muscles or failing organs. Despite increasingly sophisticated imaging, no location of awareness itself has been identified, only correlates of particular contents and states. Cognitive Dream Interface Theory interprets anesthesia not as the destruction of awareness, but as a suspension of rendered narrative. From the first-person point of view, one never recalls awareness itself fading, only a break in remembered content.

This is one reason Cognitive Dream Interface Theory is not religion. Religions posit a creator outside the world, which immediately raises the question of who created that creator. Simulation theory does the same in digital form, multiplying ontological layers while solving nothing. Cognitive Dream Interface Theory does not invoke a being outside reality. It begins from the one fact already present: awareness. Awareness is not a god. It is not a hidden programmer. It is a natural phenomenon, as basic as gravity or electromagnetism, except that, unlike those forces, it is directly known from the inside.

This also explains why awakening in the contemplative sense matters so much. In dreams, we usually do not realize that the dream world is arising from mind. Only when we become lucid do we understand that the whole scene was being generated from outside its apparent frame. Waking life may be structurally similar. Through meditation, psychedelics, near-death experiences, or spontaneous insight, many report a dissolution of the ordinary self and a [direct contact with a more basic field of awareness](https://iai.tv/video/the-brain-is-not-responsible-for-consciousness). Cognitive Dream Interface Theory interprets such moments not as supernatural revelations but as glimpses of the dreamer from within the dream.

The hardest question for any universal awareness theory is why only one perspective is experienced at a time. Here, again, the dream analogy clarifies what abstraction obscures. A single dreamer may generate many characters, but the dream is lived through one viewpoint at a time. Experience requires localization. Multiplicity exists within the dream. Singularity belongs to the dreamer. Waking life may be the same. What we call separate selves may be many localizations of one awareness, each necessary for a coherent world of differentiated experience.

If that is true, then the real philosophical task is not to explain how matter gives rise to mind, but to explain why mind gives rise to a world that feels so convincingly material. Cognitive Dream Interface Theory answers by turning to the one domain where world creation is already obvious: dreaming. We know from nightly experience that a mind can generate time, space, identity, multiplicity, and narrative without external matter. What we do not usually consider is that this may be [the closest analogy we have to waking reality itself](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-a-creation-of-consciousness-auid-3365).

Reality may not be a machine producing awareness. It may be awareness producing a world. And if that is so, then the most radical discovery is also the most intimate one: what we have been seeking outside the dream may be the awareness through which the dream is already appearing.
