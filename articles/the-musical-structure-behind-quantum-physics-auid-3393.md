# The musical structure behind quantum physics

*The quantum waves that play in music and nature*

*Long before physicists defined energy as coming in discrete packets, musicians had already been working with quantised waves. The same idea that underpins quantum mechanics — energy existing in fixed intervals — also governs the harmonic relationships between musical notes. Harvard researcher Bridget Queenan argues that the first discoverers of the quantised nature of the universe may have not been scientists at all, but composers.*

As the 19th century drew to a close, a gifted musician ran the only scientific experiments of his career addressing a fundamental concern: Are singers rational or irrational?

To answer this question, the musician analyzed the waves of various frequencies radiating from the physical body known as a “choir” and listened for the underlying mathematical patterns. From studying the spectral distributions of choirs under various conditions, Max Planck [concluded in 1893](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/549e4558e4b07c07777e8a60/t/5bc39096e2c48365e0cda996/1539543194730/Max+Planck%27s+Writings+on+Music.pdf) that singers are irrational (meaning they prefer to do wave math with roots) not rational (meaning they prefer to do wave math with round numbers).

\_\_\_

Musicians were quantifying the precise relationships between wave frequencies long before physicists got involved.

\_\_\_

Having abandoned a promising career in music—the systematic study of waves generated by humans—Planck spent the rest of his life thinking about physics—the systematic study of waves generated by Nature. He turned his attention to a new spectral distribution: the electromagnetic radiation released from a “[perfectly black](https://books.google.com/books?id=RVYEAAAAYAAJ&pg=PA1#v=onepage&q&f=false)” body which absorbs all electromagnetic radiation. The musician analyzed the waves of various frequencies radiating from this theoretical physical body and listened for the underlying mathematical patterns. From studying the spectral distributions of blackbodies at various temperatures, [Planck concluded in 1900](https://archive.org/details/theoryofheatradi00planrich/page/n6/mode/1up) that Nature is both irrational and rational: sometimes She does her wave math with mathematical/physical constants that can’t be described as a neat ratio of integers (π, c, h, kB), but sometimes She does her wave math with round numbers (quanta).

**They Can’t Take that Away from Me: Musicians invented quantal wave analysis**

Musicians were quantifying the precise relationships between wave frequencies long before physicists got involved. Musicians experiment with waves in the frequency bands humans perceive as “vibration” and “sound” which provide the foundation for the rest of the electromagnetic wave spectrum. Importantly, musicians learn to recognize and implement generalizable algebraic relationships between wave frequencies, on the fly. They learn to take any variable wave frequency (f) in the range of human hearing and scale it by a proportionality constant called a “musical interval.”

\_\_\_

Planck launched his choir experiment for precisely this reason: he wanted to figure out which mathematical protocol choir singers were using to calculate each musical interval.

\_\_\_

Musicians don’t have one proportionality constant to explain the relationships between waves—they have many (**Table 1** shows a few). In Western European music theory, the formulas are hidden behind pseudo-mathematical codes involving ordinal numbers (like minor 3rd and Major 3rd) and/or Roman numerals (like iii and III). Each code represents its own proportionality constant—each musical interval defines a specific frequency relationship to the Key wave (f, in Hertz). Musicians find waves with these algebraic relationships by hand, by ear, and, in the case of singers, by internal organs.

The pseudo-mathematical musical codes *do not* reveal the underlying formulas musicians use to analyze wave relationships. In fact, each pseudo-mathematical code represents multiple possible protocols for identifying real mathematical relationships between waves. Planck launched his choir experiment for precisely this reason: he wanted to figure out which mathematical protocol choir singers were using to calculate each musical interval.

Broadly speaking, musicians have two general approaches to wave analysis within the auditory spectrum. They are expected to learn both systems upside-down and backwards:

● Rational approach: Musicians calculate relationships between wave frequencies using fractions which are rational. They find pairs of waves related by integer ratios like 2/1, 3/2 and 4/3. This approach may be called “Pythagorean tuning;” Planck called it “natural tuning.” You can learn this strategy from analog string instruments (e.g., guitars, violas). If you have heard a guitarist tune their instrument, they are finding algebraic relationships of x4/3, “a fourth” (IV), between strings.

● Irrational approach: Musicians calculate relationships between wave frequencies using roots which are irrational, meaning they cannot be described as integer ratios. They find pairs of waves related by various roots of 2 (square root, cubed root, fourth root, etc). This approach may be called “equal temperament;” Planck called it “tempered tuning.” You can learn this strategy from digital string instruments (e.g., modern grand pianos).

For centuries, musicians trained in the Western European tradition have been expected to learn two versions of the rational approach—they must be equally comfortable with 3n/2n calculations which are better for finding “sharps” (#) and with 2n/3n calculations which are better for finding “flats” (b). String instruments teach us to hear these formulas – string musicians press their strings to release wave frequencies related by the rational intervals above (**Table 1**, right). More recently, musicians have also been expected to learn the irrational approach to finding each musical interval. Grand pianos teach us to hear these formulas – modern pianos are tuned so the strings release wave frequencies related by the irrational intervals above (**Table 1**, left). All we have to do is hit the buttons.

If we decode the various proportionality constants used in music, we can see what musicians are really up to (**Table 1**). Musicians algorithmically found members of specific wave families within the continuous spectrum of sound using (4/3)n and (3/2)n. Then they rank-ordered waves by frequency into a spectral distribution called the Chromatic Scale: they established 12 intervals which move you from a wave frequency of x1 to a wave frequency of x2. Then they built a digital instrument to teach that formula for irrational wave analysis – a modern grand piano. Cartoons now teach children to do these calculations by ear. Running from lowest frequency to highest, the waves of the Chromatic Scale sound like “elevator going up;” running from highest frequency to lowest, they sound like “elevator going down” or “coyote falling off cliff.” Mathematically, the Chromatic Scale is the exponential growth/decay of 2 *between* x1 and x2 via defined intervals: we learn to take 12 equal jumps across the frequency spectrum and reliably skip the waves in between. Musicians figured out an algorithmic protocol for dividing a continuous wave spectrum into discrete intervals or “quanta.”

**Lady Sings the Blues: Nature’s quantal interval is “flat” (b)**

The reduced Planck constant provides the first hint that musicians might have heard something. In the Western European tradition, the “quantal unit” or basic building block of musical sound is “the minor 2nd” (ii). All other musical intervals are that quantal unit to a higher power: (ii)0 = I; (ii)1 = ii; (ii)2 = II; (ii)3 = iii; (ii)4 = III, etc. Interestingly, the secret irrational formula for the minor 2nd is *f* x21/12 = *f* x1.059.

Non-musical folks might recognize this relationship from the “Jaws” theme song (“duuuh-duh” = x2(1/12)). John Williams is conducting an algebra lesson on the minor 2nd, the smallest quantal interval between adjacent wave frequencies in the Western European musical-mathematical tradition. If you hit adjacent buttons on a modern piano, you’ll release waves related by the minor 2nd = x1.059. If you go back and forth between adjacent buttons x(2(1/12))+/-1, you’ll generate the “Jaws” theme. If you can hum the “Jaws” theme, you’ve learned to generate wave frequencies related by *f* x(1.059)+/-1.

**Strange Fruit: Why is the modern [A] note “sharp” (#)?**

Musicians use both an “absolute” and a “relative” measurement system to analyze wave frequencies. In their relative system, they identify algebraic relationships between pairs of waves. The irrational and rational formulas (“musical intervals”) are hidden behind Roman numerals: ii, II, ii, III, etc (**Table 1**). In their absolute system, they identify algebraic relationships between 1 Hertz and specific families of waves. The wave families (“musical notes”) are hidden behind letters: A, Bb, C#.

In essence, musicians figured out a way of grouping all waves in the universe into wave families. In the absolute measurement system—called “scientific tuning”—musical note families are formally defined based on their mathematical relationship to 1 Hertz.

Within this system, the [A] wave family is defined by its relationship to 1 Hertz of x27. Officially, the [A] notes leaving a grand piano are supposed to relate to 1 Hertz by x27, x54, x108, x216, x432, x864, x1728, and x3456 (each wave is double the frequency of the family member before). However, modern grand pianos are tuned slightly and suspiciously “sharp”: the [A] waves that leave a modern grand piano are related to 1 Hertz by x27.5, x55, x110, x220, x440, x880, x1760, and x3520.

Musicians obsessively analyzed and catalogued invisible waves using multiple algorithmic protocols involving both rational and irrational numbers, yet somehow the fruit of their labors is off. Instead of following their own mathematical rules, modern musicians tune their instruments x55/54 higher than we’d expect. Musicians themselves defined [A] waves as 1 Hertz x33 x2n. Yet, the modern [A] wave family includes 440 Hz and 880 Hz instead.

In fact, modern musicians have shifted *all* their waves up in frequency by x55/54. When musicians want to play together, they standardize their measurements to a wave in the range of human hearing. By convention, the Western reference wave is a member of the [A] family. Someone plays [A]4 and everybody else tunes their instrument to this reference frequency. Officially, [A]4 should be related to 1 Hertz by 33 x 24 = 432 Hz, as it has been historically. However, these days, folks have agreed [A]4 will be a wave related to 1 Hertz by x440 = 440 Hz. All other musical notes are tuned to this reference frequency, meaning all modern musical notes are tuned slightly and suspiciously “sharp” compared to their official definitions in the absolute wave measurement system.

Modern musicians have tuned all their wave frequencies “sharp” for reasons that aren’t entirely clear. Once again, the Planck constant offers a tantalizing hint as to what musicians might be tuning into.

**I’ll Be Seeing You In All the Old Familiar Places: Musicians found lots of useful physics numbers**

Physicists refer to “physical constants” or “proportionality constants,” meaning fixed numerical relationships that help them mathematically explain the behavior of the natural world. Musicians prefer the term “musical intervals.” Musical intervals are formulas the human brain uses to reliably relate wave frequencies to one another on the fly.

It's fun to speculate that musicians have tuned their wave measurements to the numbers Nature uses. After all, musicians spent tens of thousands of years crowd-sourcing their wave measurements, finding ‘absolute’ and relative wave frequencies that make people dance and sing and clap their hands, that help people grieve and celebrate, that make people want to die and make people choose to live. Every culture that ever existed on Earth experimented with waves traveling through various kinds of wood, metal, sinew, plastic, and bone, under various conditions of temperature and pressure and gravitational force, at every possible time of day and month and year, in every location on Earth we could access. Folks then pooled their findings into the wave analysis system we call “music.” Humans speak to each other in a language of wave math that transcends regional human language and makes us feel connected to each other and to the universe. It stands to reason that our global wave language adheres to the basic laws of wave physics.

If we look closely, we can appreciate that, at the very least, musicians figured out a ‘hack’ for finding important physical constants and/or units. The math of the [A] note family alone helps navigate to certain key touchstones of our physical world. If you take the formal definitions of: the official [A] note family (x33), the modern [A] note family (x880), and a musical note family in general (x2n), you can get pretty solid estimates of certain physical units including: atomic-scale energy (the electronVolt), atomic-scale mass (the electron), and human-scale mass (the kilogram). For back-of-the-envelope calculations of physical quantities, an [A] note sounds pretty great.

When we want to change physical scales quickly, bundling groups of (x219)n seems like the way to go. x219 might seem like an awkward choice for a quantal unit across physical scales, but it’s actually the basis of harmonic music: musicians are counting on the fact that x219 ~ x312. Why?

If there was no difference between x219 and x312, the human brain wouldn’t be able to tell the difference. Fortunately, there is a small and well-known difference: x312/219 is called the ‘Pythagorean comma.’ It is the exact frequency relationship between ‘enharmonics,’ meaning the two rational formulas for each musical interval.

Musicians learn to find musical notes rationally with both the (3/2)n system and the (4/3)n system. The relationship between the “sharp” and “flat” version of each musical interval is always x(312/219): it defines the relationship between [C#] and [Db], between [D#] and [Eb], between [Gb] and [F#], etc. You can go back and check the equations yourself: divide the ii (sharp) formula by the ii (flat) formula and you should find x(312/219).

Musicians are absolutely obsessed with hearing the relationship between x312 and x219 – they built their entire system of sound wave analysis around it.  When it comes to defining physical units across scales, that level of mathematical precision is absolutely necessary. Musicians rely on the fact that x312 is almost but not exactly x219, because they are building confidence intervals around numbers that are hard to calculate. In the case of the Chromatic Scale, the “sharp” wave is too much and the “flat” wave is too little, but the irrational root of 2 is guaranteed to lie between them.

**Do You Know What It Means: music is wave physics**

Depending on your existential leanings, you can decide whether these are neat coincidences, useful hacks, or meaningful insights into how human brains make sense of our physical world. Perhaps modern math and physics are simply extensions of what we originally learned from music—musicians taught us wave formulas that worked, and we ran with them over thousands of years. Perhaps these are simply fun (and rational!) ways we can remember scary numbers when we need to make back-of-the-envelope calculations relating physical quantities. Perhaps Nature herself is a musician, playing variations on certain themes within her own musical-mathematical constraints (‘laws of Nature’). Either way, musicians seem to have joined in that number. When Billie Holiday sang, “I’ll be seeing you in all the old familiar places,” she wasn’t just talking about a lost love—she was talking about the principles of wave physics. When Max Planck “left” music to become a theoretical physicist, he did no such thing—he ended up playing the same wave game on a different physical scale. The math Planck learned from musical scales was always waiting to help.
