# AI taking your job isn't the only problem, it's dividing workers too

*AI-savvy workers have more power than they think*

**By Carlos Felipe Balcazar** | *24th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,010 words*

![AI taking your job isn't the only problem, it's dividing workers too](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/ai-taking-your-job-isnt-the-only-problem-its-dividing-workers-too.webp)

---

*Some suspect that AI could lead to mass social upheaval, akin to the Luddite riots in response to nineteenth-century automation. However, given that AI supports some jobs whilst undermining others, it’s unlikely that a united front will form. Institute for the Future of Work Fellow **Carlos Felipe Balcazar** argues this will further concentrate economic and political power in the hands of the top income earners. Redistributive policies would be needed to address this increased inequality that may emerge from automation, but will be made harder to achieve given higher levels of polarisation and the ability of elites to prevent opposition.*

Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, stated earlier this year that artificial intelligence will “probably replace most of the jobs people do today,” and that entire job categories will be “totally, totally gone.” He was predicting a “job apocalypse.”  Mr Altman and other CEOs of AI companies have also claimed that AI will bring a big bounty for all, through lower prices for goods and the ability of societies to provide a universal income. These predictions have not yet materialized, and it’s unlikely they ever will.  AI’s capacity to replace labor is fundamentally constrained by the inherent limitations of algorithms exposed by the [Turing Halting problem](https://stackshala.medium.com/the-halting-problem-e985e19aa614), which states that there are problems that no computer program can ever solve, no matter how clever they get. Society’s capacity for redistribution is also limited by CEOs’ mandate to increase stakeholders’ profits and the need for companies to remain economically competitive to do so; the redistribution of these gains doesn’t happen “just because.” A future with no work and universal well-being is therefore a far cry.

AI, however, does create opportunities for companies to automate tasks that use a computer, reducing the need for those workers performing these tasks. The threat of increased unemployment as a result of AI adoption therefore looms large, and, at the time of writing, it seems to materialize only for early-career workers. The unemployment rate for workers aged 22-25 has declined markedly in comparison to [November 2022](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/what-really-happening-jobs-separating-ai-hype-reality), prior to the advent of commercial AI technologies with ChatGPT. For other age groups, the impact of AI has been more modest.

Increased unemployment that can be attributed to AI has evoked the imagery of the violent riots by the Luddites in early nineteenth-century England, when automated looms and spinning machines took over the tasks performed by thousands of skilled weavers and knitters, plummeting their wages and their ability to remain employed or find new work in the industry. The prospect of unemployment and lower wages resulted in massive solidarity and [mobilization from workers,](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20190385) striking, protesting, and even destroying the machines threatening their livelihoods. Factory owners reacted by shooting protesters.  The situation escalated to necessitate both legal and military suppression by the British government. The Luddite riots created a five-year period of social turmoil between 1811 and 1816.  Today, similar prospects for the health of the labor market fuel concerns for a wave of [social discontent leading to political instability](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691172798/the-technology-trap?srsltid=AfmBOoqULUHUNFKOduh6fWMfw_509AqmD8xAwXlJVzdtEJnrS3-LE0xd), where the distribution of the gains from AI could “take place by the guillotine.”

Although CEOs have invested millions of dollars to [prepare for this type of social turmoil](https://wwnorton.com/books/survival-of-the-richest), their concerns might be overstated. Machines like AI can replace the tasks performed by some workers, but they can also make some workers more productive. Many of us can process information faster with computers and AI than without them; we can manufacture goods and process foods faster with machines and industrial robots than without them; we can provide services of many kinds to a wider audience thanks to technological change; workers who can use these automations to perform their tasks more efficiently can demand higher wages and employment because they become more productive.

For instance, for the US, [according to data from job aggregator Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/career/salaries?from=gnav-jobsearch--indeedmobile), a production line assembler makes [about 70%](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Unions_and_robots.pdf) of a machine specialist’s wage. As such, the machine specialist on an assembly line is better off using her time and effort producing rather than striking and protesting; the assembler, on the other hand, could be rightfully discontent at the prospect of lower wages and employment resulting from automation, and would be more likely to strike and protest. This provides a strong contrast with the Luddite movement of nineteenth-century England, wherein vast swathes of manufacturing workers were quickly deskilled by machines, without options to retrain and further their education to use machines as workers can do today with [widely available educational programs](https://www.fanucamerica.com/training); this lack of job polarization engendered worker solidarity.

Therein lies the problem: the incentives to mobilize for those who benefit from automation differ from those that are harmed because, for instance, the machine specialist is letting go of time and effort he can spend producing more and using it instead for mobilization, which is unproductive. It is unproductive because time and effort used to produce goods or services is instead used for worker organization and mobilization, [reducing the level of economic output](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Automation_and_redistribution.pdf). Further, this type of mobilization compresses economic outcomes between workers, as companies may often redistribute from higher- to lower-earning workers to reduce labor costs. As a result, job polarization [manifests in polarization in the level of social mobilization](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Automation_and_redistribution.pdf) between the workers who win and those who lose from AI adoption. The quintessential social trade-off is one between the level of productivity, which benefits those workers who use machines, and the well-being of workers whose jobs are likely to be replaced by them.

The polarization that emerges from automation has consequences that undermine the possibility that AI will benefit all workers, as some AI-company CEOs claim. While technology has indeed increased productivity, workers are now obtaining a smaller slice of the growing economic pie, while increased profits are [going to top-income earners](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674430006). The share of labor in the economy has decreased by [eight percentage](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/129/1/61/1899422?redirectedFrom=fulltext) points in the past two decades, which means that [higher productivity may not necessarily lift all boats as intended, but will instead lead to a higher level of wealth concentration among the top income earners](https://iai.tv/video/what-the-left-misses-about-trump-with-maurice-glasman). Redistributive policies are thus needed to address the increased inequality that may emerge from automation; if workers cannot make effective demands for redistribution thanks to polarization in social mobilization, then the future may be one of [high social inequality](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2022-10/Tasks%20Automation%20and%20the%20Rise%20in%20US%20Wage%20Inequality.pdf) that favors the top income earners.

One of the direct ways in which this polarization undermines the ability of workers to organize is by reducing the incentives of workers to participate in union activities. Union activities have created venues for workers for more than [one hundred years](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/136/3/1325/6219103?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false) to obtain better job conditions in the face of rapid economic change, including the automation of work, via [collective bargaining agreements](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/handbook/pii/S1573446325000069?casa_token=cCrpMkzYf04AAAAA:QPzJOEmxjHig8HV4M-GqeIk6rVo5FQhsi-Z_BhRabbjc9fp-MgOHz-srmo_kbIte-pw3WVZUGu4E). Union workers negotiate with their employers to navigate the need to automate, while at the same time reducing the degree of wage and employment disruption caused by machine adoption in production. Further, unions have historically [assisted workers in participating politically](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12122-004-1020-y) to push for redistributive policies that benefit them, and to hold legislators accountable when they do not do so. The evidence has shown that when unions are weaker, [redistribution decreases and inequality increases](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/136/3/1325/6219103?login=false).  For instance, historical evidence for the US shows that households with union members have a [10%-20%](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/136/3/1325/6219103?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false) higher income than other households; in particular, union households were of special importance for the low levels of income inequality observed in the US during the [post-World War II era](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/136/3/1325/6219103?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false). Other studies have also found a similar role for unions in inequality in the [UK](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2022-08/Labour-market-inequality-IFS-Deaton-Review-of-Inequalities-2.pdf), [Germany, France, Italy, Latin America and Nordic Countries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/handbook/pii/S1573446325000069?casa_token=cCrpMkzYf04AAAAA:QPzJOEmxjHig8HV4M-GqeIk6rVo5FQhsi-Z_BhRabbjc9fp-MgOHz-srmo_kbIte-pw3WVZUGu4E), where union households could receive up to a 25% union-wage premium.

[Current research](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Unions_and_robots.pdf) also shows that recent waves of automation, such as the one triggered by robot adoption since the 1980s, have indeed created polarization in political participation that has manifested in [less redistributive policy](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Unions_and_robots.pdf); it shows that where workers’ incentives to mobilize are more polarized, legislators are much less likely to support policies that are good for them. These policies include but are not limited to occupational safety, personnel management, unemployment compensation; child and family welfare, services, and relationships; measures concerning public assistance programs or aging; health services, health care coverage and insurance; regional planning, rural and urban development; affordable housing and homelessness; and all aspects of income, excise, property, inheritance, and employment taxes.

Recent evidence on AI also shows that mobilization from workers in response to the advent and implementation of AI has been low and temporary in nature; long gone, for instance, are the headlines of the Hollywood strike, and yet the work of [many performers continues to be automated](https://www.latimes.com/business/story/2026-08-27/hollywood-actors-clash-over-ai-voice-clones). While AI has triggered the negotiation of new collective bargaining agreements in various sectors, the workers most affected by it, both positively and negatively, like those in the tech sector, have [low levels of unionization](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/recoding-power-9780197612873). These workers are often unaware of the threat that AI poses to their jobs and livelihoods—believing to benefit from AI—and the power that their collective demands can have to shape outcomes. Worker mobilization in the face of new waves of automation continues to dwindle by creating winners and losers from AI.

I invite the reader to reassess the political implications that AI may entail. The current trend seems to reflect a lower capability of workers to demand policies that benefit them, like higher wages, better employment conditions, or redistributing the economic gains from AI. If the trend continues, the future will be one of higher inequality. Trying to replace all labor is a [self-defeating social endeavor](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6448898) because without workers there is little to no demand for products and services. The likelihood that redistribution will take place without mobilization from workers is a naïve take that ignores companies’ mandate to maximize profits and the already high concentration of income in a few hands across the world. Further automation also encourages suppression in order to prevent political instability and preserve capital accumulation even at the expense of workers’ wages and employment, [fuelled by the economic elites](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35336); Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson—both 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics—indicate that this was indeed [characteristic of the Luddite riots in the nineteenth century](https://shapingwork.mit.edu/power-and-progress/). Today, [China is showing evidence](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/138/3/1349/7076890) of increasingly using AI in conjunction with Chinese AI companies to suppress dissent. In other words, in today’s world, AI elites can collude with government elites and other economic elites to concentrate economic and political power.

The future may not be one marked by political turmoil led by angry laid-off workers, but instead it may be one where the ability of workers to mobilize is weak enough that it allows economic elites to establish a system that would prevent any further mobilization, thanks to AI’s abilities to polarize and also suppress workers.  The future could well be one whereby we live in a [digital panopticon](https://www.cato.org/blog/digital-panopticon-why-automated-surveillance-threatens-free-society), and not one where workers usher in a renewed social contract.

There are options, however, to reduce the likelihood of such a [dystopian future](https://iai.tv/articles/a-coming-age-of-leisure-is-a-dangerous-myth-auid-3629). Formally, [cooperatives have provided alternative ways of production](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764208327673) that give ownership of companies to workers, reducing the contentious distribution of resources from economic activity. Informally, [advocacy coalition networks](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/104191) provide decentralized forms of worker and social organization that, thanks to the internet and social media, facilitate the creation of networks that share core interests with non-trivial degrees of activity.

While the science of [alternative ways of effective worker and social organization](https://iai.tv/articles/why-reason-must-destroy-society-auid-3438) advances, I believe that the key lies in workers understanding the ability they have to shape working conditions and welfare-enhancing policy when presenting a united front: workers who benefit from using machines have more bargaining power because the impact of a strike is larger because they are the most productive members of the workforce, yet they are largely unaware of the benefits of [organizing and mobilizing alongside their peers](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/recoding-power-9780197612873) to demand better job conditions. Strong labor movements have helped maintain the [social contract](https://iai.tv/articles/hobbes-is-wrong-about-society-auid-2181) for the past [one hundred years](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-economics-051520-025251). The evidence is clear: Economists have found that belonging to a union could increase a household’s income by up to [20%](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/136/3/1325/6219103?login=false)-[25%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/handbook/pii/S1573446325000069?casa_token=cCrpMkzYf04AAAAA:QPzJOEmxjHig8HV4M-GqeIk6rVo5FQhsi-Z_BhRabbjc9fp-MgOHz-srmo_kbIte-pw3WVZUGu4E); furthermore, strong unions can [increase the likelihood that legislators vote for welfare-enhancing policy](https://cfbalcazar.github.io/files/pdf/research/Unions_and_robots.pdf).
