# A post-work world would be a solipsistic nightmare

*Heidegger and work as the expression of being*

**By B. Scot Rousse** | *25th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,996 words*

![A post-work world would be a solipsistic nightmare](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/post-work-world2.webp)

---

*Work is not just a means of survival, but our main way of connecting with the world beyond our own minds, argues philosopher **B. Scot Rousse**, drawing on Heidegger’s metaphysics. Western thought has long prized detached contemplation, but genuine understanding comes from trying, failing, caring, and being answerable to others. In work, we sharpen our skills, test our limits, and engage with reality through shared responsibility. Without that crucible of effort, obligation and accountability, Rousse warns, we risk shrinking into isolation—spectators of life rather than participants in it.*

Many worry that [AI will lead to job loss](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/04/opinion/ai-jobs-employment-industry.html). That framing is understandable, but it misses a deeper issue. The stakes of AI and work reach beyond productivity and employment into the very conditions of the human world. Berkeley computer scientist Stuart Russell warns of the problem of “[human enfeeblement](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/307948/human-compatible-by-russell-stuart/9780141987507)”: as machines take on more cognitive and productive labor, people may lose the gumption and opportunities to pursue meaningful projects, sustain skilled practices, and find orientation in shared forms of life. Today, with [job openings for young people reportedly contracting](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/05/30/technology/ai-jobs-college-graduates.html?searchResultPosition=8) and AI threatening to make more and more skills obsolete, Russell’s worry is becoming ever more urgent.

The phenomenological tradition in philosophy helps us see what’s really at stake by illuminating work’s *ontological* significance. At its best and in its basic nature, work isn’t just a series of discrete tasks, nor is it merely how we are economically productive, pay our rent, or receive social validation. In work, at its best, we express our very mode of being. Work is where we focus and take care of concerns, develop expertise, and take our place within shared forms of life.

**Heidegger: Work as coordinated being-in-the-world**

Martin Heidegger’s *Being and Time* [challenged Western philosophy’s](https://iai.tv/articles/hegel-vs-heidegger-can-we-uncover-reality-auid-3001) fixation on detached consciousness. It focuses on everyday skilled work. This move has roots in Aristotle’s attention to practical wisdom, and Hegel’s and Marx’s emphasis on labor’s formative role, but Heidegger gave these insights a distinctively phenomenological development.

For the carpenter at work, the hammer recedes from explicit awareness, becoming “ready-to-hand,” transparent in the familiar flow of building. But this equipment always refers beyond itself to others. The hammer refers to nails, the wood to the shelf being built, the shelf to the person who will use it. Work happens in and brings forth a shared world, what Heidegger calls our “being-with” each other (*Mitsein*). The hammer also refers to the concern guiding its employment, for example, to create a place to sit.

Heidegger’s analysis shows that we exist primarily not as interiorized minds but as a mode of absorbed involvement in meaningful projects that coordinate our action with others. Crucially, this involvement is always situated: we find ourselves already thrown into situations that present concerns demanding our care and attention. Work thus provides privileged phenomenological access to fundamental structures of human existence: we exist as interdependent, context-bound beings who dwell together in a world of shared equipment, purposes, practices, and concerns.

**Dreyfus brings Heidegger to California**

In the 1960s–70s, Hubert Dreyfus wielded these Heideggerian insights against early artificial intelligence research. In his 1972 book, [*What Computers Can’t* *Do*](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/What_Computers_Can_t_Do.html?id=TsraAAAAMAAJ&redir_esc=y), and in the theory of skill acquisition developed with his brother Stuart (see [*Mind Over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Age of the Computer*](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Mind_Over_Machine.html?id=e9W9m_4q4pYC&redir_esc=y), 1986), Dreyfus argues that [intelligence isn’t reducible to the abstract, disembodied formal symbol-manipulation](https://iai.tv/video/mind-machines) the AI researchers of the time were focused on, but intuitive “coping” with what is ready-to-hand.

One of Dreyfus’s key contributions in these debates lies in his insistence that expertise requires extended experience and emotional investment. You cannot become an expert through detached study alone. Reading a recipe does not give you the skill to cook a meal. Watching a YouTube tutorial does not give you the ability to change a tire. The developing practitioner must try and fail, care about outcomes, and feel both the sting of failure and the satisfaction of success. Skill ripens to expertise only where something is genuinely at stake.

Dreyfus’s critique proved prophetic and was ultimately vindicated as symbolic AI was eclipsed by today’s neural network approach. Along the way, Dreyfus influenced Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores, researchers at Stanford and Berkeley, who undertook a radical rethinking of the nature of human work. In their book [*Understanding Computers and Cognition: A New Foundation for Design*](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Understanding_Computers_and_Cognition.html?id=6TwbGGSz6NYC&redir_esc=y) (1986), Winograd and Flores brought Heidegger’s solitary carpenter into the modern, computer-centered, organizational world.

**Winograd & Flores: Human work happens through conversation and commitment**

Building on Heidegger’s account of skilled involvement and social being-with, Winograd and Flores shift attention from tasks to the conversational practices of cooperative work. On their view, work, fundamentally, is the way people take care of each other’s concerns by coordinating action in conversations, especially those involving requests and offers.

When Heidegger’s carpenter makes a bookshelf, he does so either to fulfill a request or to make an offer. When a request or offer is accepted, it generates a promise with a commitment to deliver what has been requested or offered. The conversations through which this happens involve more than information exchange; they coordinate commitments.

Delivering on these commitments requires ongoing coordination with suppliers, colleagues, apprentices, and customers, a web of interactions that gives concrete form to Heidegger’s notion of being-with. This conversational infrastructure depends on our skills for acting in language and coordinating commitments. Such skills can be cultivated, refined, or allowed to deteriorate. People can be better or worse at attuning to each other’s concerns and taking care of these concerns in conversation (e.g., making and delivering on an offer or request in a timely way). By extension, computer systems integrated into work can be better or worse at facilitating these conversations. The systems themselves, however, do not make commitments or take responsibility. They function as extensions of human conversational competence.

\_\_\_

Work has a pedagogical function: it’s where we learn to coordinate with strangers, negotiate commitments, build trust networks, and take responsibility for our promises. The workplace becomes a school for the skills of living together.

\_\_\_

To provide a more adequate foundation for designing computer systems meant to participate in human work, Winograd and Flores articulate the structure of what they call the “basic conversation for action.” Someone with a concern makes a request or receives an offer, opening a conversation. This initiates a negotiation toward shared understanding of the concern and its conditions of satisfaction, leading eventually to a promise (when the offer or request is accepted). The promise occasions the work of delivery, which culminates either in a declaration of satisfaction or in renegotiation when circumstances change.

The communicative competence and attention to human concerns that Winograd and Flores locate at the heart of work is not a separate layer added onto technical expertise or the execution of discrete tasks. To participate meaningfully in the networks of commitments constitutive of cooperative work is to listen for what is at stake for others, to make offers and requests to deal with these concerns, to be answerable when things go wrong, and to care enough to repair breakdowns and rearticulate shared understanding.

Work, properly understood and enacted, becomes a site for cultivating communicative competence: learning to listen for what others need, negotiating shared understanding, and taking responsibility for unmet commitments. The Winograd-Flores framework thus illuminates what Julian Jonker calls “[the cooperative interface](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10892-023-09449-2.pdf)” through which human social life takes place. The cooperative interface is the complex of technologies, architectures, norms, and organizational practices shaping our opportunities and styles of interaction (e.g., an open-office layout vs. private offices). Jonker argues that workplace technologies aren’t simply instruments for achieving productivity goals but ways of mediating and shaping interpersonal relations.

Winograd and Flores provide both the anatomy and the normative standard for Jonker’s cooperative interface. The anatomy: organizations as networks of conversational commitments. The normative standard: a well-functioning cooperative interface is one where conversational competence is cultivated, sustained, and enacted. As Jonker points out, work thus has a pedagogical function: it’s where we learn to coordinate with strangers, negotiate commitments, build trust networks, and take responsibility for our promises. The workplace becomes a school for the skills of living together in a well-functioning, pluralistic society.

As Jonker warns, though, automation can “thin out” the cooperative interface. When AI mediates conversations, it may handle the transactional surface of requests and responses while bypassing the work of mutual understanding. The conversational loop closes (request made, response delivered), but the practices through which communicative competence develops grow attenuated. The skills that we develop through engaging with concrete others who hold us accountable begin to atrophy.

**Work as taking care of a world**

The foregoing is not a defense of work as it currently exists. Many people today find themselves thrust into meaningless or exploitative forms of work. The technological and economic developments of the last 25 years have created more opportunities for various forms of “gig work,” where interpersonal depth has been stripped away, where coordination happens through algorithms rather than human conversation, and where the pedagogical function of work has been systematically diluted. [Work’s fundamental role in human life](https://iai.tv/articles/my-job-my-self-how-work-defines-us-auid-1078) has in these cases been systematically degraded.

According to the phenomenological tradition I’ve been tracing, human existence is constituted by our involvement in the world with others, and by our engagement with concerns. There is no standpoint outside concern from which human life could be lived. This exposes the folly of one of the visions haunting contemporary discussions of AI and work: [the utopian dream that work will become obsolete](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-349-59072-8_25), that technology will finally liberate us from having to address our concerns at all. For this would separate us from the human condition itself. Of course, [some transhumanists aspire exactly to this](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Economic_Singularity.html?id=8WVd0AEACAAJ&redir_esc=y).

Work is not merely the execution of tasks. It involves the conversational coordination of commitments through which we take care of concerns together. It is a way of being answerable to others, of negotiating standards, of repairing breakdowns, and of sustaining shared forms of life. These capacities cut across every domain in which human beings must coordinate action around what matters.

**The real threat of human enfeeblement**

Human beings will never stop developing new concerns. This is the biggest problem with the fantasy that AI will make work obsolete. New concerns continually arise from our involvement with others, from technological change, from shifting social norms, and from the unpredictable contingencies of life and history. Taking over all of the currently valuable tasks in an economy does not eliminate human concerns. But it does intensify the question of who will take responsibility for addressing them, and on what terms.

The darker alternative to a world without work is a world in which our ability to take care of our own concerns is gradually abandoned and outsourced to autonomous systems. In that world, we would not simply be unemployed; we would become a different kind of being. These are the true stakes of Russell’s problem of human enfeeblement.

Human beings create themselves through their technologies and practices. We are not fixed essences. So far, intuitive, involved, embodied expertise has been central to what we are. The capacities to attune to what matters, coordinate action through conversation, care about the quality of our performance, take responsibility for commitments, and contest the standards that govern our action have constituted the human way of being in the world, having been expressed in a dazzling variety of cultural variations and self-interpretations. To give this over entirely to automation would be to realize the deepest version of Hubert Dreyfus’s worry: not a future dominated by superintelligent machines, but one populated by sub-intelligent, existentially enfeebled human beings, no longer practiced in taking care of a world that still demands their care.
