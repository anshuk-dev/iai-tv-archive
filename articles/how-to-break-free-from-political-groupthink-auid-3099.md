# How to break free from political groupthink

*The freedom of thought seldom used*

**By Robert B. Talisse** | *10th March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,869 words*

![How to break free from political groupthink](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/how-to-overcome-groupthink.webp)

---

*We pride ourselves on being free thinkers. Yet, all too often, our opinions are shaped by our political tribes. Robert B. Talisse argues that true intellectual freedom needs more than hearing opposing views – we should look for solitude, step outside familiar political categories entirely and engage with ideas beyond our intellectual paradigm.*

In the run-up to the recent US presidential election, I adopted what I expected would be a reliable strategy for avoiding unwanted political discussion. Whenever someone said, “Can you believe what Kamala said?” or “Did you see Trump at yesterday’s rally?” I responded “Yes, but I’m not sure what to think about it yet.” This was meant to signal that I understood what had been referenced but didn’t want to talk about it. It would be a polite conversation-stopper, so I thought.

I was wrong. The tactic backfired – it prompted more commentary. Still, its failure revealed something important about how difficult it is to think for oneself.

Here’s what happened. My claim to not know what to think was almost always heard as a confession that I was uninformed. So, most people responded by repeating Kamala’s statement or describing what Trump had done. I would then restate my original claim, “Yes, I know – I’m just not sure what to think about it.” This usually provoked hostility. Many took me to be *approving* of what had been said or done. In one case, a person replied, “Well, what Kamala said is terrible, and if you don’t see that, I can’t help you.” That kind of escalation was uncommon, but the overall pattern was typical.

Weeks later, I witnessed this dynamic play out on national television. On December 1st, President Biden pardoned his son Hunter, who was convicted of [three felony charges](https://www.cnn.com/2024/06/11/politics/hunter-biden-gun-trial-verdict/index.html). The pardon was [startling](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/biden-says-he-is-pardoning-his-son-hunter-2024-12-02/) because Biden [repeatedly pledged](https://youtu.be/4EpUpC9fCSg?si=czqc_LZYV3QWGuzo) before the election that he would respect the conviction. [As the news broke](https://youtu.be/u5kWPaKLgK0?si=Yj3LC7rUxHVNEccM) of Biden’s reversal, liberal commentator [Molly Jong-Fast](https://www.msnbc.com/author/molly-jong-fast-ncpn1031186) was asked on a live program for a “fast and furious” reaction. She paused for a moment, then said, “I just heard it. I have to process it. I don’t have a take. I’m sorry.”

Her reaction became its own story. Several [news](https://nypost.com/2024/12/02/media/msnbc-contributor-molly-jong-fast-left-speechless-by-hunter-biden-pardon/) [outlets](https://www.breitbart.com/clips/2024/12/02/msnbc-contributor-jong-fast-speechless-over-president-biden-pardoning-hunter/) adopted the [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/media/liberal-msnbc-contributor-speechless-over-hunter-biden-pardon-have-process-sorry) headline that a prominent liberal was rendered “speechless,” “[gobsmacked](https://www.mediaite.com/news/i-dont-have-a-take-im-sorry-msnbc-contributor-totally-gobsmacked-after-learning-about-hunter-pardon-live-on-air/)” by the pardon. The next day, conservative commentator Megyn Kelly [featured the clip](https://www.tiktok.com/@megynkellyshow/video/7443930808915610923) on her Sirius XM program as a “very fun example” of liberal hypocrisy. Jong-Fast’s unwillingness to declare an instant verdict was taken as an endorsement of Biden’s act, and her claim to need time to process the news was ridiculed.

Like the exchange with Jong-Fast, my little conversations were driven by the assumption that knowing what to think is simply a matter of hearing and looking – that forming an opinion is immediate, like sense perception. This assumption means that opinions should be automatic. It thus associates clarity of thought with the immediacy of judgment, thus leading us to disregard anyone who claims to need time to think.

Despite its popularity, this is a dubious stance. Forming an opinion about the behavior of others isn’t like sense perception. Statements and actions typically don’t wear their significance on their sleeves. Especially when they’re unusual, we need to consider *what prompted* the statement or act, the circumstances under which it was said or performed, and what was intended by the speaker or agent. In short, if we want to evaluate something responsibly, we must understand it. And to understand it, we need to think.

\_\_\_

We sometimes need to remove ourselves from the spaces we typically inhabit and make room for thinking that’s quiet and detached.

\_\_\_

There’s the rub. Thinking takes time. Clarity is not automatic. As Jong-Fast noted, to evaluate some unexpected event, we need to process it. Yet politics is increasingly dominated by the “hot take,” the immediate verdict, and the snap judgment.

The demand for instant verdicts may be a byproduct of the frantic nature of politics. The world comes at you fast, so to speak, and our thinking needs to keep up. Circumstances don’t always permit reflection. Sometimes we must shoot from the hip.

Fair enough. But there’s a downside to this pace. Hot takes tend to be reheated versions of stale thoughts. Snap judgements are unthinking reflexes. Our “fast and furious” opinions are typically someone else’s opinions.

This holds especially in politics, where our instincts arise from our partisan allegiances. When pressured to form an opinion, [we typically look to who we are](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.2044-8309.1990.tb00892.x). That is, we employ the shortcut of thinking from the perspective of our group membership. Wanting to fit in with our peers, we revert to the views we expect them to embrace. For this reason, our “takes” are groupthink. Ultimately they’re not judgements at all, but more like kneejerk declarations of our loyalties. They express clear assessments, but that’s because they follow conventional partisan scripts.

Upon realizing this, I changed my strategy for dealing with political chit-chat. When someone said, “Did you hear Kamala?” or “Did you see Trump?,” I responded “Yes – that was horrible.” This triggered entirely different reactions. Typically, my reply prompted a lengthy complaint about the mentioned candidate, and an expression of contempt for their supporters.

I often didn’t need to say anything beyond my initial remark. I could simply allow the other person to vent. It turns out that those initiating these encounters were not interested in discussion after all. Instead, they sought a sympathetic audience for the recital of their partisan outlook.

Political *kvetching* in a supermarket queue is harmless. However, as the Jong-Fast episode suggests, the tendency to demand “fast and furious” political evaluations is widespread. Again, these “takes” are unreflective rehashes of established opinions. As those opinions are well-known, this kind of commentary adds little to political discourse.

Yet a larger problem looms. In the US and Great Britain, [political affiliation](https://ejpr.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-6765.12228) has become our [central way of locating ourselves socially](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pops.12485). It’s no exaggeration to say that opposing partisans today occupy [distinct social worlds](https://theconversation.com/partisan-divide-creates-different-americas-separate-lives-122925), where they interact increasingly only with people who are [politically like themselves](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01066-z). As a result, partisanship functions [more like a lifestyle](https://publicsquaremag.org/dialogue/is-ideology-dividing-us-or-something-else/) than a [political ideology](https://iop.pitt.edu/sites/default/files/Elected_Officials_Retreat/2018/Mason_et_al-2018-Political_Psychology.pdf). We are submerged in social networks populated almost exclusively by political allies who demand assurances of our partisan loyalty. This gives us reason to think that a lot of what passes for independent thinking is in fact partisan cheerleading, a mode of codifying our alliances and distancing our foes.

Some degree of cheerleading is to be expected. After all, democratic politics is a team sport. It’s important to know who’s on your side. Alliances matter and rivalries naturally result. Certain forms of hostility and contestation among political opponents are central to democracy. They are arguably a sign of democracy’s health.

But, unlike team sport, democratic politics is not only about coordinated collective action. Democracy also calls for reflection. Citizens need to be deliberate and discerning. They have to weigh options and priorities. They also must take steps to make sure they’re not too deferential. Their civic job is to exercise their own political judgement.

Here's the trouble. When our social environments are politically segregated, divergence from the party line becomes unduly costly. Increasingly, we find that political disagreements [cause broader forms](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/11/13/america-is-exceptional-in-the-nature-of-its-political-divide/) of [social banishment](https://www.npr.org/2020/10/27/928209548/dude-i-m-done-when-politics-tears-families-and-friendships-apart). Meanwhile our partisan alliances have become more [homogeneous](https://quillette.com/2019/05/17/conformity-and-the-dangers-of-group-polarization/) and [less tolerant](https://theconversation.com/what-todays-gop-demonstrates-about-the-dangers-of-partisan-conformity-161401) of deviation from the dominant perspectives. This has led to a sharp increase of [issue alignment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0049089X2030096X?via%3Dihub) among co-partisans. That is, allies today [share more views in common](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1470594X20901346) than ever, even though the views do not logically entail each other.

In short, as our networks have sorted along partisan lines, the social risks of thinking for ourselves have intensified.  Our thinking thus has become homogenized, conformist.

\_\_\_

What can we do? Perhaps we could counteract groupthink by engaging with our rivals. However, in many cases we have already written them off. Even when such interactions are generally civil, they can [fortify our negative attitudes](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1804840115). Perhaps, then, we should turn to our alliances? Yes, it would be nice if our coalitions would embrace robust norms of devil’s advocacy, practices that incentivize self-critique. Yet there is reason to think that our partisan groups are already too homogeneous to adopt such habits. And note, too, that appointing oneself the heterodox freethinker of one’s group can become its own constraint, forcing one into a stance of reliably oppositional orthodoxies.

Here's where we find ourselves. Our physical and social environments are segregated according to our political identity. They prompt from us reflexive expressions of our group loyalties and cross-partisan animosities. Although these reflexes can help to mobilize our coalitions, they also undercut the kind of reflection that democracy needs. So, where does that leave us? I argue in my book [*Civic Solitude*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/civic-solitude-9780197752166?cc=us&lang=en&) that because we are already surrounded with triggers of our partisan identities, we sometimes need to remove ourselves from the spaces we typically inhabit and make room for thinking that’s quiet and detached. It’s important to stress that my proposal is not that we withdraw from democracy, but rather that we expand our idea of our civic duties to include occasions for political reflection that’s at a distance from the fray. Crucially, the required distance isn’t only physical. If we are to reclaim our capacities for independent thinking, we need also to reflect on political circumstances that are not our own.

In other words, we need to expose ourselves not to calm expressions of our rivals’ perspectives, but rather to critics who do not fit into our existing partisan idiom, detractors who can’t be countered by our political reflexes. We need to contend with political ideas that stimulate our imagination.

For an example, note that Aristotle offered an argument that [elections are fundamentally anti-democratic](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D4%3Asection%3D1294b). He contends that democracies should fill public offices by lot. What’s important here is that if you disagree with Aristotle, you probably cannot rely on your partisan impulses to explain why. You need to think freshly about the democratic merits of elections and lotteries. In thinking it through, you might come to better appreciate a range of plausible political ideas that lie beyond your partisan perspective.

Of course, there’s nothing special about Aristotle in this regard. Stimulants for independent thinking can be found in the contemporary artworks, political treatises, and social commentary of societies other than yours, places where the political categories with which you are most familiar are simply inapplicable. Engagements of this kind reveal the constraints of our own partisan scripts, thereby opening new avenues of thought, in contexts free from the conformity pressures of our political allies.

I realize that this all seems counterintuitive. Ultimately my view is that we can cultivate capacities for independent thought by occasionally disengaging from the issues, problems, and circumstances most in need of our sharpest thinking. Admittedly, that sounds odd. However, I have tried to suggest that our thinking is dulled when conducted only under conditions that, in the name of clarity of judgement, provoke conformity. The challenge is bigger than breaking away from how “our side” thinks - it’s about breaking out of the framework of partisan ideas entirely. In a nutshell, my proposal is that thinking for yourself requires occasionally thinking beyond yourself.
