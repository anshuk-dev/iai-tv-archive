# Relationships are more important than pleasure

*Stoicism isn't what you think it is*

**By Massimo Pigliucci** | *27th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 17 mins* | *3,480 words*

![Relationships are more important than pleasure](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Relationships-are-more-important-than-pleasure.webp)

*In this exclusive IAI Live Interview, Massimo Pigliucci makes the case for virtue ethics against establishment beliefs coming from modern neuroscience and psychology and argues that human relationships are more important to living a good life than physical pleasure.*

**Charlie Barnett**: **Your new book,** [***Beyond Stoicism***](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Beyond-Stoicism-Skeptics-Epicureans-Philosophers/dp/B0D4P7ZBML)**, illuminates various insights from the Stoics and other Greco-Roman philosophers about how to philosophise and how one lives a good and flourishing life.** **If you were to take one or two of the greatest misconceptions that people have about Stoic and Greco-Roman philosophy more generally, what would you say they'd be?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: Good question. So the title of the book actually in the American version, which is the one that is a little bit misleading because really only a fraction of the book has to do with Stoicism. In the UK, it has been published as [*Live Like a Philosopher*](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/massimo-pigliucci-3/live-like-a-philosopher/9781035411016/), which is much more appropriate.

So you're asking about misconceptions. Let's pick one general and one perhaps specific to Stoicism. I think the general misconception is that the Greco-Romans were a bunch of now-dead white dudes, and so why should we listen to them 2,000 years later? And the answer to that is, well, first of all, they are certainly dead. There's no question about it. The concept of whiteness, of course, did not apply at the time. In fact, the Greco-Roman world was incredibly diverse, ethnically, religiously, and so on and so forth.

\_\_\_

A common misconception about stoicism specifically is that living like a stoic is like living like Mr. Spock from Star Trek.

\_\_\_

But more importantly, these people were keen observers of human psychology, from which they derived certain interesting thoughts about what constitutes a good life, a good human life. Many of these thoughts have been validated and amplified by modern science. So the reason to listen to the Greco-Romans, as well as to other so-called wisdom traditions from different areas of the world, let's say Buddhism, for instance, from India, from ancient India, or Confucianism from China, is because these people got a lot of stuff right, and why throw away things that work?

A common misconception about stoicism specifically is that living like a stoic is like living like Mr. Spock from Star Trek. So stiff upper lip and suppressing emotions, both of which have nothing to do with actual stoicism. It is true that stoicism does value endurance, for instance.

If something happens to you, and there is nothing you can do about it, then the only reasonable option is to just endure it to see if you can get through it. But by and large, the stoics actually were cultivating what they thought as healthy emotions. So they were not trying to suppress emotions in general, but they were in fact trying to stay away from what they thought were unhealthy emotions.

A healthy emotion would be something like love for your partner, for instance, or your children or your friends, things like that. An unhealthy emotion would be anger. Cultivating anger is, according to the stoics, just a bad idea, because then you're going to act on the basis of a violent emotion that overrides reasons, and you're probably going to regret it.

**Charlie Barnett**: **Philosophical systems, and you had mentioned it with respect to stoicism, often uphold central principles. So some philosophical systems are about reducing oppression, some are about following a universal set of laws, others are about how we conduct ourselves if we conclude that there's no ultimate meaning out there. In the opening of your book, you say that there are three central principles that should be focused on. First is how we live well, second is how we strike the right character, and thirdly is how we doubt things or how we approach the world with epistemic humility.**

**Why should these three ideas be the central principles of a philosophical system, and how do you think a philosophical system should find what's central to it?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: That’s a very good question. So when my co-authors and friends, Greg Lopez and Meredith Kunz, when we started working on a book that we thought of as an exploration of practical Greco-Roman philosophy, we thought, okay, how are we going to organise this thing? Because there's a lot of material, it goes from the 6th century BCE to the 5th century of the current era, so it's more than a millennium’s worth of philosophy and philosophical thinking.

So how do you organise it? The typical way is just chronologically, right? You start with the pre-Socratics and you end up with the neo-Platonists. The problem with that approach is that it doesn't make any sense, because it's just, in that way, one gets the impression that philosophy is just one thing after another, that there is no rhyme or reason to it. So what we decided instead is like, well, let's look at what are the emerging themes that are common to all of these philosophies, or how we can differentiate these philosophies.

And we thought, as you mentioned, that there are three major themes. We use a navigational metaphor, so we call them ports. So we navigate to three different ports. One is the port of pleasure.

That is, certainly some philosophers recognise that human beings are pleasure-seeking animals. I would actually argue that all animals are seeking pleasure in their lives. That's the result of natural selection.

But human beings in particular, we have an extraordinary amount of ways, number of ways in which we seek pleasure. So pleasure is important. Those are the hedonistic philosophies.

And there are two big ones. They are Epicureanism, that at least most people have probably heard of, although they probably think of Epicureanism as the sex, drugs, and rock and roll of ancient philosophy. And it certainly was not that at all. And [Cyrenaicism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tcjr9G47A0o#:~:text=An%20explanation%20of%20the%20ancient%20philosophical%20position,was%20a%20precursor%20to%20both%20the%20Epicurean), which is a lesser-known philosophy, which really did, in fact, teach that the only thing that is important is physical pleasure in the here and now.

The second port has to do with character, or to use the ancient term, virtue. Meaning that these are philosophies that seek to improve ourselves as human beings. Why character? Well, because a good character is what leads to good relationships. And it turns out that having good relationships with other people - your partner, your children, your friends, society at large - is actually a major component of a good human life. So character is important.

There are several philosophies that deal with character in that port. Mostly we deal with Aristotle, we deal with the Stoics, with the Cynics, and so forth.

And then there is what we call the port of doubt. Doubt as in scepticism, or if you prefer, as in critical thinking. Because as it turns out, the Greco-Romans thought that not only we are pleasure-seeking animals, we're not only we are animals that thrive on the basis of good relationships with others, but we're capable of thinking intelligently. Our evolutionary weapon, as we would put it today, is really our ability to interact socially with other people and to solve our problems by way of reason, by way of intelligence.

So critical thinking becomes important for a good human life. And there are in fact some philosophies, which we cover in that part of the book, that were devoted to that. Socrates, in fact, surprisingly perhaps for some people, but we put Socrates under the sceptics.

So-called [Pyrrhonian](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k_SAocFtlio) sceptics, academic sceptics, and even the famous or infamous sophists. We think all of them, in different reasons, in different ways, they taught that reason is fundamental and therefore it's a tool, and we need to be able to use it as properly as possible.

**Charlie Barnett**: **One of the tenets of your book, as you've said, is the port of pleasure.****To throw a modern day criticism of that idea, some like Stanford psychiatrist Anna Lembke have argued that seeking pleasure and avoiding pain is bad in a narrow sense, namely because your body responds to pleasure by making your brain less sensitive to dopamine and therefore your brain needs a higher quantity of the stimulus to create the same response. And secondly, your brain then creates a general lower level of dopamine and therefore lower level of well-being. Can ancient philosophy, like what you've just outlined, respond to that?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: The problem is we need to agree what we mean by pleasure. And the ancient view of pleasure wasn't necessarily the one that emerged, that you just described, that emerges from, let's say, modern neuroscience. We use the same word, you know, part of the problem here is that we use the same words, but sometimes we mean different concepts that we need to be careful.

For instance, while the Epicureans were considered a hedonistic philosophy, Epicurus said that the highest pleasure one can have is tranquillity of mind or lack of pain. So most people today wouldn't think of pleasure that way. So there's certainly something to be said about the fact that you need to be careful about how our natural neurological response is to both pleasure and pain.

\_\_\_

But you do need a little bit of spice on that dish, and that spice is provided by pleasure

\_\_\_

But I think it's hard to deny that most people seek a life in which there is some degree of pleasure, and they seek to avoid, to minimise both physical and mental pain. I mean, there are, you know, entire schools of modern psychology that are devoted exclusively to the reduction of mental pain. And there is a whole, you know, essentially all of medicine that is devoted to the reduction of physical pain.

At the end of the book, we actually suggest that we should put together, we shouldn't just listen to the Greco-Romans, obviously, because as much as they had good ideas, these ideas need to be updated with evidence-based treatment of the same subject matter that comes out of modern science. By the same token, modern science, I think, does need guidance from a philosophical perspective, because most modern scientists tend to be very narrowly focused.

I have a background in evolutionary biology. I know that if I want to publish something new in my field of research, that has to be really narrow and really focused, as opposed to a philosophical approach that tends to be more general. If you put the two things together, it turns out that I would say, broadly speaking, a good life, according both to the ancient philosophy and modern science, is something like this.

It has mostly to do with character, because if you are a good person, you tend to have good relationships with other people, and the evidence is very clear. One of the most important components of a life worth living, according to the data, is in fact having good relationships. So that's the main dish, so to speak.

But you do need a little bit of spice on that dish, and that spice is provided by pleasure. So think of a really nice main meal, and then you have your little pepper and salt on it to spice it up. Now, you can still have a nutritious meal without the spices, okay? So even if you happen to be unlucky enough to actually have a life with little pleasure and a lot of pain, you can still have a life that is worth living if you are developing good relationships with other people.

But certainly makes it better if you have the little spice. The spice metaphor also makes it clear that you don't want that to be the main dish, right? If you flip things around and you say, okay, I'm going to have a really good dish of salt and pepper, that A, is not nutritious, and B, it's actually disgusting. So the proper proportions between those things are important.

What about scepticism or doubt or critical thinking? Well, as I said before, it turns out that human beings really are animals capable of rationality, and rational thinking is what gets us out of a lot of trouble. Therefore, a life that is, again, based mostly on relationships and character with the spice offered by pleasure, and an underlying base of thinking critically, thinking carefully about what we're doing and why, that seems to me like a pretty good combination.

**Charlie Barnett**: **Some philosophers and psychologists like** [**Paul Bloom**](http://iai.tv/articles/paul-bloom-the-pleasure-of-suffering-auid-2208) **have gone as far as to say that we actively need to embrace suffering in our lives in order to give it meaning.** **How would this cohere with Stoic and Greco-Roman philosophy?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: I highly respect Paul, but I think that there is enough suffering as it is in life. I don't think we need to actually go out and sort of somehow embrace it, or even seek it, which is even worse. Suffering already comes with life. It's unavoidable. Even the best life you can think of, right? A life in which you have mostly no setbacks, no tragedy. Well, at some point, you will lose your parents. Another point, you will lose some of your close friends. And of course, in the end, you will face your own mortality.

That's enough suffering - thank you very much! And yes, you do need to embrace those things for sure. And philosophy and modern psychology provide us with tools for doing that. But why would you want to make it a major aspect of your life, right? It still seems to me that minimising that component makes a lot of sense. Again, particularly because it is in fact inevitable. It's not that you can possibly have a life without suffering, even if you wanted to.

**Charlie Barnett**: **I'd like to move on to this sort of second part of the book that you've touched on, which is the port of character. When it comes to being taught ethics, most people are taught three broad ethical systems. The first is virtue ethics, or focus on character. The second is a utilitarianism, a focus on the consequences of an action. And the third is a sort of deontological view of ethics, namely that the inherent rightness or wrongness of an action is really what matters. Why do you think we should prioritise a character-based approach against the other two systems? Is that based on sort of wisdom? Is that based on one being more rational than the other?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: Yeah, that's a very good question. There a number of philosophers throughout the 20th century, perhaps most importantly [Philippa Foot](http://iai.tv/articles/how-four-women-revolutionised-ethics-auid-2013) in the UK, who thought that the Kantian turn, the enlightenment turn toward deontology, and then later on, the utilitarian turn, those were two big mistakes in ethics. Unfortunately, these ideas have become dominant. Of the three you just mentioned, the Kantian approach and especially utilitarianism tend to be the most popular, certainly among philosophers. Although virtue ethics has actually regained a significant amount of popularity over the last several decades.

I think those are mistakes for one simple reason. They essentially amount to redefining ethics in a far narrower and less useful way than it is in virtue ethics.

\_\_\_

So I think of the virtue ethical approach as more natural to human beings and more conducive to a whole life worth living, as opposed to what I see as a narrow and constricting focus on just the rightness or wrongness of specific actions.

\_\_\_

For the ancients, not just the Greco-Romans, but also arguably some of the wisdom traditions from the East, especially Confucianism: ethics is not simply the study of right or wrong or is the answer to is this particular action right or wrong, which tends to be a lot of what's going on in both utilitarianism and Kantian deontology. It is really the study more broadly of what makes for a good human life in general.

And a good human life certainly does include, answering questions like: is this action a good idea or a bad idea? But it includes a lot of other things like what kind of values should we hold? Or what kind of values should we reject? What kind of priorities should we have in life? And what kind of things can we do without? So I think of the virtue ethical approach as more natural to human beings and more conducive to a whole life worth living, as opposed to what I see as a narrow and constricting focus on just the rightness or wrongness of specific actions. That said, there are also specific issues that both Kantian deontology and utilitarianism have faced pretty much from the beginning.

Kantian deontology is extremely rationalistic. It's this notion that you can just think about stuff and derive general answers to questions. I think that's just a bad idea in general. If your approach does not include actual experience and empirical evidence, then you're not going very far. And utilitarianism has a number of problems, one of which is that it's often impossible to actually predict the long term consequences of our actions. That's why in virtue ethics, the emphasis tends to be on your intentions.

It's not that virtue ethicists don't care about consequences; they just admit that there is only so much you can actually plan about consequences. You can do certain things you think they're going to go in a certain direction, and they might not because there's lots of factors that influence the outcomes.

**Charlie Barnett**: **It sounds like you're appealing to a character based view of morality where there is more of an emphasis on wisdom as opposed to sort of a narrow view of rationality.****Some might argue that wisdom itself is intangible. And it's hard to judge who is wiser than another, even though wisdom does exist to an extent. Do you think that is a problem for stoicism?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: So, again, that's a very good question.

Certainly people have used and can use the word wisdom to mean different things. And yeah, you can you can define it in a way that it's big enough that lots of stuff fits into it. But that's I don't I don't think that's that it is true that the word wisdom is not operationalisable and in fact, cannot be quantified. That's what modern psychologists do. There is a whole area of research in modern psychology about wisdom, how you acquire wisdom, how do you quantify it, etc, etc.

So people have thought about this sort of stuff. Generally speaking, people say that wisdom is just a matter of experience and getting old, basically, but it kind of comes automatically with the having the grey hair. That's definitely not the case.

Both common sense and certainly the empirical evidence show that that is not the case. Yes, you do require experience, for sure. You cannot be wise at 12 years old. If you don't have enough experience in life, enough things that have happened to you in life, it's hard to imagine somebody who might become wise. But you also have to pay attention to those experiences, you have to be mindful about those experiences, you have to think about them and try to learn from them and apply those lessons that you learn. If you don't do that, then you don't get wiser, you just get older and crankier.

**Charlie Barnett**: **And one final question: in an exchange you had with Tommy Curry three years ago at the** [**HowTheLightGetsIn Festival**](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/the-big-ideas/speakers-2/massimo-pigliucci)**, you argued that Putin couldn't reason legitimately to invade Ukraine. Curry countered by saying that the boundaries of rationality are determined by the sort of thinking structures of a society.** **Why do you take issue with this line of argument?**

**Massimo Pigliucci**: I take issue because that argumentation leads to the notion which I think is not only incorrect, but seriously misguided: the idea that your reason is just as good as my reason. In other words, it's one step removed from epistemic and possibly moral relativism.

A lot of that discussion hinges on what exactly do we mean by reason. Does reason mean just strictly formal logic? Probably not. At least that's not my definition of reason. But it is true certainly that it does include, it has a component of logical thinking. And logical thinking, just like mathematics, depends on your actions.

So if you pick certain actions, a psychopath can use reasons starting by certain actions. But the choice of actions itself is subject to reason. So what I was arguing in that discussion is that Putin's choices of actions can be challenged on the basis of reason and evidence.

We don't have any other way to challenge them. What am I going to say? Are my intuitions different? If I go in that direction, then somebody else will say, yeah, so are mine and therefore that's it. Or are we going to go to where might makes right? Certainly Putin would like that. But I hope sincerely that most of us don’t.
