# Psychopaths show us that grief relies on self-love

*What Camus’ The Stranger teaches us about mental time travel*

**By Michael Cholbi** | *8th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,398 words*

![Psychopaths show us that grief relies on self-love](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/psychopaths-show-us-that-grief-is-really-about-self-love.webp)

---

*We know psychopaths don’t feel empathy for others, but it turns out they also can’t feel empathy for themselves. Philosopher **Michael Cholbi** argues that psychopaths’ inability to grieve teaches important lessons about empathy and grief for all of us. It shows us that it’s possible to empathise with past and future versions of ourselves (a form of mental time travel) and that a key part of grief is this self-empathy. Albert Camus’* The Stranger *demonstrates this well, with the protagonist’s inability to grieve the death of his own mother.*

Psychopaths have a number of attributes that explain their reputation for antisocial behavior. Psychopaths are deceitful and grandiose, with inflated senses of self-worth. They do not experience normal levels of emotional response, and are prone to impulsive or reckless behavior.

There is one specific characteristic of psychopaths that appears difficult to explain, though: psychopaths experience little if any grief in response to the deaths of others.

Consider Meursault, the seemingly psychopathic protagonist of [Albert Camus](https://iai.tv/video/kafka-vs-camus)’ novella *The Stranger*. Meursault lies routinely, indulges in casual cruelty, single-mindedly pursues the pleasures of the moment—and feels no guilt or remorse about any of this. Upon hearing about the death of his *maman*, the event barely registers with him. He goes through the motions of grieving—attending his mother’s funeral, etc.—but her death is of no emotional consequence to him.

\_\_\_

For psychologically typical subjects, the ability to grieve reflects and is entangled with our ability to care for ourselves and for others.

\_\_\_

It is tempting to explain psychopaths’ diminished capacity for grief in terms of their [lacking *empathy*](https://iai.tv/articles/the-campaign-against-empathy-must-be-resisted-auid-3583). Psychopaths are systematically deficient in caring about others and in being able to take on their points of view. But that fact won’t explain their lack of grief. No doubt one common aspect of grief is that we are sorrowful on behalf of the deceased person. We feel a sense of loss because death was in some way bad for them. The process of dying may have been painful or undignified, or they may have died with a lot of good life still to live. Presumably, Meursault could not have felt such sorrow for *maman*, since sorrow at how her death may have been harmful to her would require him to possess the ability to empathize with her.

Yet there is another dimension of grief, one that psychopaths *should* be able to experience. We grieve the deaths of those who matter to us in some way—those who have become central to our lives. And an inability to empathize with others does not preclude their being central to our lives. Perhaps for a psychopath, that another person’s death could be a loss to that person won’t provide the psychopath a reason to grieve. After all, to grieve what others have lost to death seems to require the very empathy psychopaths don’t have. Meursault’s lack of empathy means that whatever may have been bad about his mother’s death for her does not resonate with him. But that same lack of empathy allows that her death could have been bad for him, an event worthy of grief. On its face, even a psychopath should be able to experience the death of someone who cared about them as a loss. Psychopaths’ empathic deficiencies therefore should not stand in the way of their being able to grieve.

To conclude that psychopaths’ lack of empathy cannot explain their diminished capacity to grieve would be premature, though. To see why, we need to expand our understanding of empathy. We typically think of empathy in terms of how to relate to other *people*. An empathetic individual can take on others’ points of view and cares about their well-being. But suppose we think of empathy in terms of how we relate to other *selves*. Other selves include other people, but they also include earlier and later versions of our selves. To lack empathy in this latter sense is to be indifferent not only to what happens to others, but to be largely indifferent to our own past and future selves. If Meursault lacked *interpersonal* empathy, he could not take on his mother’s point of view and did not care about her well-being aside from how it intersects with his own. But if he also lacked the second sort of empathy—call it “inter-self” empathy—he would be indifferent to *maman* but also indifferent to his predecessor and successor selves. Current Meursault cared little about Meursault-one-year ago or about Meursault-one-year-hence.

Psychopaths’ inability to grieve, I propose, rests on their lacking this ‘inter-self’ empathy. This becomes clear when we take note of other anomalies in the psychopathic mind, anomalies related to [the experience of time and of the self](https://iai.tv/articles/the-plurality-of-the-self-auid-3055). In general, psychopathic consciousness has a very narrow temporal focus. With respect to the future, psychopaths are often reckless and disinterested in planning for their futures. They are instead focused on immediate goals and struggle to apply lessons learned from the past to their future plans. With respect to the past, psychopaths are typically deficient in emotional memory and generally do not assign events from their pasts much significance in explaining their current goals or attitudes. More broadly, psychopaths struggle to engage in *mental time travel*, the imaginative capacity to project oneself into the future or the past. Psychopaths thus seem to exemplify an extreme version of “living in the moment”: Unable to integrate their present self into their pasts or to project that present self into the future, they are intensely present-focused, with their attention or concern extending not far beyond the current moment. In this regard, a psychopath’s past or future self is as foreign or remote to them as are the lives of other people.

How does psychopaths’ intense focus on the temporal present shed light on their inability to grieve normally? Recall that grief involves seeing others as central to our identities over time. When those who are central to our identities in this way die, we experience a loss rooted in our past relationships with them. Furthermore, one dimension of grief is figuring out “how to go on,” that is, who we are to be in relation to the deceased given that they cannot play the same role in our lives and in our identities in the future. To have a parent die, for example, is to experience the loss of the past relationship we have had with them but also to face the challenge of how they can continue to matter in our lives despite being dead. To grieve, then, is to integrate the past, present, and future together by unifying our present relationship with another person with our past and future relationships with them. But this integration or unification of our relationship with another person in turn requires integration or [unification of our relationship with our selves](https://iai.tv/articles/you-are-both-one-self-and-many-selves-auid-3081) across time—to have our past and future selves be objects of our attention and concern. But psychopaths, with their intense focus on the temporal present, do not relate to their past and future selves in this way. Put in terms of empathy, grief requires a kind of empathy or identification with our selves that eludes psychopaths.

Expanding our concept of empathy to include “inter-self” empathy thus allows us to account for psychopaths’ inability to grieve in terms of a lack of empathy, one of the defining features of psychopathy. But it also allows us to see how, for psychologically typical subjects, the ability to grieve reflects and is entangled with our ability to care for ourselves and for others. To be [able to grieve](https://iai.tv/articles/emotion-and-memory-cant-be-safely-separated-auid-3429) is to see our past and (possible) future relationships with others as part of our larger biography, an accounting of ourselves that extends into the past and into the future. In grieving, we manifest a form of “self-empathy.” Grief thus speaks to our ability to love ourselves. But it also speaks to our ability to empathize with others. Seeing our past and future selves as chapters within our own story correlates with an ability to see other people as chapters within our own story too. We thus have reasons to be glad for grief. For not only can grief in response to the deaths of others enable us to achieve a more temporally coherent perspective on our lives, but it also demonstrates that we can empathize with others and see them as no less real than ourselves.
