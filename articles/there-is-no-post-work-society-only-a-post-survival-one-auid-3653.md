# There is no post-work society, only a post-survival one

*The wage economy is not a law of nature*

**By Manuel Delaflor** | *12th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,639 words*

![There is no post-work society, only a post-survival one](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/there-is-no-post-work-society-only-a-post-survival-one2.webp)

---

*The wage economy once appeared natural. It linked labour to production, income, and survival. But AI is making these links unstable: societies can produce more with less human work. Philosopher **Manuel Delaflor** here argues for a new economic framework, one in which we should draw a line between survival and competition. Survival should be guaranteed to everyone, whether they work or not. Essentials such as food, shelter, healthcare, and energy should be given to all and kept outside the competitive economy. Once survival is taken care of, work will then be left to satisfy ambition, innovation, meaning, purpose, and status.*

I bet that you are not interested in another fight between “capitalism” vs “communism,” “left” vs “right,” “individualism” vs “collectivism,” as if these old ideologies were the only ones available for thought. They never were, of course. That old boxing ring is becoming useless, and it is time to put them to rest. These categories are too small for what is coming. They were built for a world in which human labor remained the central route through which society produced, distributed, disciplined, and rewarded itself. But AI is beginning to pull that world apart, and things will get ugly if we keep pretending that our inherited models, the ones we didn’t choose no matter how hard some people have bought into them, can simply stretch a little more and cope.

And I want to talk about this with hope. Actual hope, not the sterilized palliative of marketing campaigns. Think about it: for the first time in history, we are approaching the possibility of producing and coordinating immense portions of social life without needing every adult body to pass through the wage machine as the primary condition of survival. That alone is astonishing. We should be honoring the hard labor of every generation before us, and opening our imagination to the amazing possibilities within reach… and yet most public conversation is still responding with fear shaped by obsolete categories: how do we save jobs, retrain workers, keep consumers consuming?

\_\_\_

A society that must tax its own productivity to prevent its distribution system from collapsing has revealed something deeper than a policy problem: that the wage route to life is becoming incoherent.

\_\_\_

I take those as the wrong questions. Let us change the angle. The best way to do that is to turn ourselves into temporal zoologists. Desmond Morris became famous by looking at humans zoologically, as animals wrapped in symbols, rituals, mating displays, dominance games, and social habits. His 1967 book *The Naked Ape* approached humans as a species, not as the elevated protagonists of their own self-flattering story. That perspective is exactly what we need now. We need to stop treating “wages,” “debt,” “wealth,” “property,” “jobs,” as if they were tangible and given, like trees or tigers. Such abstractions are convenient labels that have overextended their utility.

The epistemic framework called [Model Dependent Ontology](https://iai.tv/articles/we-do-not-discover-reality-we-create-it-auid-3475) comes in handy at this point. Not as a political banner or economic theory, but as an instrument: MDO does not ask what the world “really is”; it asks what a model makes possible, and what it hides. From MDO, ontologies are not discovered; they are instantiated: a species builds a model, the model works, and its outputs start being treated as furniture of the world. That is the trick: when a model works long enough, these primates start calling it “reality,” confusing their own construct with an unchangeable feature of nature. The wage economy is one of those constructs.

Let’s look at the animal. These primates need relatively few things for survival: food, shelter, safety, meaning, and some way of keeping violence from devouring the group. Sure, it is way more complex than that, but many of the complexities emerge from the models they use to deal with what is possible. Once you look from there, the grandeur of economic language begins to wobble. We see billions of primates using invented chains to decide who may access the conditions of life. That sounds normal to them only because they were born inside it. A child arrives, helpless and luminous, and the entire machinery begins to hover around it: “income bracket,” “parental employment,” “childcare cost.” What?

From here, the phrase “earn a living” reveals its inherited nonsense. It sounds ordinary because it has been repeated to them since birth. Now, it is of course correct that every living form must secure the conditions of its own continuation. That is not the issue here. The fox must hunt or scavenge. The tree must draw water, light and minerals. The bacteria must metabolize. Nothing out of the ordinary, until we return to our strange apes, the only species that takes that basic biological problem and passes it through the arbitrary constructs of economics.

You should “earn a living” as if the body first had to satisfy an accounting system before being allowed to survive to the next day. As if they came into the world already owing something to an abstraction. And before the stubborn and irrational reflex arrives... No, I am not advocating “communism” or any other myth from the exhausted museum of the twentieth century. What I care about is our models’ efficiency towards the goal of life: survival. A routing system that made structural sense inside one set of conditions is now being asked to govern a different world. This primate society needs mechanisms to organize itself; the problem begins when one historical routing system presents itself as unavoidable, when the conditions that made it plausible are dissolving in front of our very eyes.

To organize themselves, these apes built a highly revered model called “money,” a strange symbolic machinery. The Bank of England explains that most of the money circulating in the modern economy is created by commercial banks when they make loans. The European Central Bank says it is created when banks expand their balance sheets. These apes treat this architecture of promises, liabilities and enforceable claims as if it were as natural and important as the oxygen they breathe.

And it gets stranger. The Bank of England says it is wholly owned by the UK government while operating independently from day-to-day political influence. The US Federal Reserve System is described by the St Louis Fed as combining public and private characteristics, and the Federal Reserve itself says the system is not owned by anyone. This is not a conspiracy but something far more interesting: a sophisticated arrangement for managing the upper layers of a symbolic permission system that these apes then embrace as “normal.”

Once you see it this way, the textbook innocence fades. Their “money” is not merely a medium of exchange; it’s permission, memory, and control. It decides who can act, who must wait, who can build, who must beg, who is trusted. And because it almost works, as numbers move across screens with hypnotic authority, for these primates the model feels like “reality.”

This is the point of Model Dependent Ontology: models do not need to be objectively real to rule a species. They only need to work long enough to become invisible. Wage society did exactly that. For a long period, it had an internal rhythm. It was brutal, unequal, often humiliating for the majority, but it made structural sense within its own frame. The primates had a “job,” then “wages” gave them “purchasing power,” which became “demand,” which sustained firms. Firms paid “taxes.” States survived. Households survived, many barely, but survived. The same population that powered production returned as consumers to buy the output and keep the machinery working. The body entered the factory, the warehouse, the call center, and came back with tokens that reopened access to life. That was the old cantata of “jobs, jobs, jobs,” sung by politicians as if employment were the voice of reason.

This fantasy was already cracking before AI arrived. OECD research says that over recent decades, productivity growth in most OECD countries has decoupled from real median compensation growth, meaning higher productivity no longer reliably raises real wages for the typical worker. Notice the cruelty hidden there. The machine becomes more capable, yet [the ordinary worker no longer receives the gain](https://iai.tv/video/yanis-varoufakis-on-the-end-of-capitalism). These primates are told to be more skilled, more flexible, more everything, while the link between what society can produce and what the ordinary individual can access becomes increasingly fragile. And then they are told that all of that is for their own good.

Then AI enters as a new operational capacity inserted into an already strained model. The numbers are everywhere if you want them. The IMF talks about 40% of global employment exposed. The ILO talks about one in four workers worldwide. The exact figure matters less than the direction, and the direction is unmistakable.

A recent paper makes the contradiction almost comically explicit. Falk and Tsoukalas argue, in the language of formal economics, that [firms racing to automate erode the very consumer demand they depend on](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.20617), and that none of the proposed “fixes” (universal basic income, retraining, equity participation) remove the trap. That matters because it comes from inside the machinery. A company that automates gains advantage. Many companies automating together weaken the wage channel through which the population buys what companies sell. Then the same civilization that celebrates efficiency panics because the efficient machine has damaged the social route by which efficiency becomes livable. This is a point where a civilization changes.

Let’s talk about the proposed “solutions.” Tax automation. Patch incomes. Keep the old machine alive no matter what. Some of these bandages might be necessary, but a bandage is not a solution; it’s a patch for something broken. A society that must tax its own productivity to prevent its distribution system from collapsing has revealed something deeper than a policy problem: that the wage route to life is becoming incoherent.

This is not a moral argument, although moralists will inevitably try to drag it there because that is where serious structural problems go to die. The point is coherence. A primate society that can feed its members but lets them starve because its permission system does not authorize access is not mature. It is obedient. The United Nations states that today’s global food production is enough to feed everyone on the planet, yet hunger persists. Children are born into that abundance, yet access still depends on whether an inherited permission model has found a profitable use for the adults around them. At some point, this stubborn ape has to wake up from the [extreme inefficiency](https://iai.tv/articles/our-economic-system-is-designed-to-make-us-unhappy-auid-3543) of their beloved system.

It will become obvious soon, I hope, that the question is not how to “save jobs.” Jobs were never the essence of human life. They were the bridge between production and access. The question that matters is how to organize access when that bridge is no longer strong enough to carry a civilization. [This is the post-labor problem](https://iai.tv/articles/a-post-work-world-would-be-a-solipsistic-nightmare-auid-3505). It is not the fantasy that nobody does anything. It is not post-effort, post-care, post-risk, post-responsibility. There are countless important things to be doing. The post-labor economy means the end of wage labor as the route through which life becomes authorized.

That opens a different design space. Survival can and must become the substrate, not the prize. Food, shelter, water, healthcare, baseline energy, basic connectivity, child protection and social continuity belong beneath the competitive layer. The labels that will tempt you are universal basic income, or its cousin of universal services, but these proposals still route survival through the old permission system; they patch the wage model while leaving its function untouched. Substrate is a different kind of claim, about what sits beneath the competitive layer, not about which transfer mechanism funds it. It means treating housing as a provisioning system instead of a speculative asset. Health as a condition of life instead of a business. Child development as continuity infrastructure instead of a private cost abandoned to wage pressure. And the substrate has a boundary, drawn by biology: whatever has a satiation point belongs below the floor. Nobody eats ten breakfasts or sleeps in four beds. What is unbounded is positional wanting, the hunger for rank, and that stays above the floor, where markets and prices keep doing the one job they are actually good at.

Fire protection was once a subscription business; houses without the insurance plaque were left to burn. Then it migrated into the substrate, and today nobody “earns” the fire brigade. Roads, sewage, clean water, primary schooling made the same journey, and the markets above did not collapse; they kept playing above the floor. The alternative is a boundary that has been moving for two centuries, and the productive capacity now arriving lets it keep moving.

I already hear some crying: “There is not enough money, the system cannot absorb the cost.” But if the skeptic insists on the arithmetic, let them do all of it. A society that refuses to fund the floor is not saving that money; it is paying it anyway, at the emergency rate, the least intelligent price available, and then congratulating itself for its “prudence.” Finland housed its homeless and found it saves at least fifteen thousand euros per person per year, because the emergency room, the police cell and the courtroom are the most expensive providers of housing ever devised. Every society that built the floor and left the competitive game running above it sits at the top of every index, and several of them—the Netherlands, the Nordics—out-employ the United States.

So whoever shouts “communism” at this point is aiming at the wrong century and the wrong layer: the regimes that failed abolished the ceiling, and what is proposed here touches only the floor. Substrates are often built in crisis, not in comfort: Social Security was born in the pit of the Depression, and the NHS was founded by a bankrupt country still under rationing. The floor appears exactly when the old routing visibly fails, and that is precisely where these apes are standing now.

A serious post-labor civilization begins from understanding and embracing the nature of the apes. It stops measuring civilization as if “GDP” were an x-ray of the primates’ flourishing. The OECD itself says that understanding whether policies are improving lives requires looking beyond GDP to a broader range of economic, social and environmental outcomes.

This sounds radical only because the old model has colonized the human mind. But once the spell weakens, the proposition becomes obvious. What stands in front of us is a threshold. For the first time since our species began stitching itself into symbolic systems, we have the productive capacity to uncouple survival from permission. Every generation before us would have wept at the possibility. Mothers who watched their children starve inside abundance. Workers broken by labor that produced wealth they would never touch. Business leaders earning billions only to notice that everything they dearly wanted—peace, happiness, connection—was never there. All that suffering was the cost of a routing system that could not yet be otherwise. It can be otherwise now. And the only thing keeping the old machinery in place is the reflex that treats it as nature. The same reflex that once made monarchy feel eternal, that once made slavery feel ordinary.

So, the old ring is in ruins, and you don’t need the gloves anymore. What you need now is focus, stamina and determination, because the window is now. Not in fifty years. Now, while the language of the old system is still cracking in public, I am asking you: do we have the courage to look at the strange ape in the mirror and admit that we built the cage, [that we keep rebuilding it every morning](https://iai.tv/articles/truth-is-the-most-dangerous-fantasy-our-species-ever-invented-auid-3263), and that we hold the key in our own hand? This is an opportunity our ancestors could only have dreamed of. A threshold standing right in front of us, asking whether we will walk through it or keep negotiating with the ghost of a world that is already gone.
