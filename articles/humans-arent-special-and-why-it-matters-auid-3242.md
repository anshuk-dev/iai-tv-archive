# Humans aren't special, and why it matters

*Anthropocentrism is a violent philosophy*

**By Timothy Morton** | *27th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,993 words*

![Humans aren't special, and why it matters](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Humans-arent-special-and-why-it-matters.webp)

---

*Humans think they are special, unique, and the pinnacle of life, but according to Timothy Morton, this is a dangerous delusion. Because evolution is random and not goal-driven, humans are necessarily a part of nature, not superior to it. Anthropocentrism and humanism deny this basic fact and cause much misery and destruction in the process. Letting go of this belief isn't anti-human—it's the only possible way for the human species to survive on earth.*

I’m going to convince you that it’s wrong to think that humans are the pinnacle of life on Earth—that they are special and different. I’ll also convince you that knowing this is a very, very good thing. Ready? You won’t be able to un-read this. I’ve been writing about it since I started in grad school in 1989.

A century before I went to Oxford, William Morris (he of the [wallpaper designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris_wallpaper_designs)) published [*News from Nowhere*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3261/3261-h/3261-h.htm), a novel about a future communist utopia. *News from Nowhere* is a very “talky” novel, containing endless discussions about the way things were and the way things are. One of the characters makes a very good observation about the way human beings in the Victorian period used to see the thing we call “nature”:

Was not their mistake once more bred of the life of slavery that they had been living?—a life which was always looking upon everything, except mankind, animate and inanimate—‘nature,’ as people used to call it—as one thing, and mankind as another, it was natural to people thinking in this way, that they should try to make ‘nature’ their slave, since they thought ‘nature’ was something outside them.

But isn’t this still true? If we are offended by slavery, if our ancestors have suffered from it, if we are inheritors of its legacy in any way—and I would argue that this means all of us here on planet Earth—then we ought to be equally offended by mastery. Slavery and mastery go together like a horse and carriage. How we treat one another is how we treat the biosphere. [Anthropocentrism is the idea that human beings are the center of all life](http://iai.tv/articles/nature-is-not-a-resource-for-humans-1957-auid-1958), and deserve therefore to be the masters of all creation.

Mastery implies some kind of ownership. Thinking of the rest of the biosphere as human property, which can therefore “work” to enrich us no matter what for nothing in return… this is a “teleological” idea, an idea with an aim, a direction. Nature is pretty aimless, however. When you put something with an aim to work on something without an aim, the latter will get munched down to nothing. For this reason alone, human beings should rethink their attitudes to life on earth (and rivers, and mountains, and…).

Yes: nature is aimless. Evolutionary theory teaches us this. Evolution has three drivers: genetic mutation, symbiosis, and sexual selection. Each one is aimless, or, to put it another way, *non-teleological*. Let’s investigate.

*Genetic mutation is random*. This was the great insight of Charles Darwin’s [*The Origin of Species*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm). “Random with respect to current need” is the exact phrase. If Steve Jobs had designed my ears, they would be little pinpricks, because pinpricks are much more efficient than the floppy mess that I call my ears. Those smartphone pinholes can hear perfectly well. The only reason I have ears like these is that similar ears didn’t kill either my mother or father before they had the sex that resulted in me. That’s what “survival of the fittest” really means. It doesn’t mean “survival of the one with the six-pack abs,” no matter what a “social Darwinist” might tell you. (There’s a huge and frustrating confusion here, because capitalist ideology took over Darwin’s theory before he published it!)

*Symbiosis is random*. Imagine you’re a single-celled organism floating through the ocean about three billion years ago. GULP! Something penetrates your cell wall. If you could talk you might go, “OMG. Have I just swallowed poison?!”

Two hundred million years later, some amoebae are having a conference. The keynote speaker is going, “No, by Jove! That wasn’t poison. That was another single-celled organism. It welded itself to the one that absorbed it, and slowly it evolved into mitochondria. And hey presto! Animals! We showed up!” In another ballroom another keynote speaker is going, “No! That was another single-celled organism, and millions of years of symbiosis later, it developed into chloroplasts, and abracadabra! Plants!”

But at the moment of ingestion, when the common ancestor “ate” the other single-celled lifeform, symbiosis is random. It’s like bumping into a stranger by accident only to find yourself falling in love. *Symbiosis is cheap*. It doesn’t arrange dates like a matchmaker.

Sexual selection is really important. Unless you are a bacterium that clones itself to reproduce, you reproduce sexually, and sexual display enters into the equation when there’s enough sentience for it.

*And sexual selection is utterly random*. Ornithologist [Richard Prum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Evolution_of_Beauty) has brilliantly demonstrated beyond doubt that there’s no earthly reason why some females get attracted to some traits. Having gorgeous plumage doesn’t mean “I’m really strong” or “I’m really good at digesting poisons.” Furthermore, there’s no way evolution can contain this attraction in females alone. Do you like peacock feathers? I rest my case. Darwin once said that the very thought of a peacock’s tail made him want to throw up. I reckon this is because he guessed that the way he enjoyed peacock tails wasn’t that different from how female peacocks enjoyed them. Disgust is really more enjoyment than you can handle.

\_\_\_

Any idea that humans are better or at the top or superior in any way is just wrong.

\_\_\_

The fact that sexual selection is random means something totally stunning. It means that *exactly how you enjoy art is exactly how part of evolution works*. You enjoy it for pretty much *no good reason at all*. [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Judgment) argued powerfully that beauty is like pleasure, but not totally—you can’t eat the Mona Lisa. Well you could, I guess, but then you couldn't [find it beautiful](http://iai.tv/video/truth-beauty-and-other-scientific-misconceptions-lisa-randall).

The biosphere: it’s a glorious thing, made up of random accidents all the way down and all the way through: random mutation, random encounters, random beauty, available “cheaply” to all. There isn’t a winner or a top or a best or a central species anywhere to be seen. Saying this is not the same thing as saying there are no “apex predators”—but even this idea needs a rethink.

Now you’ll note that even by point one (random genetic mutation), you ought to have conceded that *humans can’t be the best*, or the top, or the climax of anything. They can’t even be *better*. That’s right: humans can’t even be better than lemurs, worms or pondweed. Any idea that humans are better or at the top or superior in any way is just wrong.

But point two (random symbiosis) *also* means that humans can’t be the apex of anything. Humans are communities of other lifeforms. Consider the less hardwired symbiosis that we *must* *have* in order to live: our stomach bacteria, for example, play a crucial role in digestion. But it’s not as if the stomach bacteria are our employees. It’s perfectly possible to argue the other way around, that human beings are really just vectors for bacteria and for genetic materials such as DNA. (I would rather not do either.) The phrase “highly evolved” and terms such as “higher primate” should really be scrapped. What we mean is “late-evolving primate,” “lately evolved.” And late doesn’t mean better.

\_\_\_

What makes humans unique are just two things: throwing and sweating.

\_\_\_

And point three (random sexual selection) means that whatever we think beauty is also pertains to organisms such as beetles. That’s right. Exactly the same mechanism that Kant was describing: admiring something for no reason at all. [Darwin was adamant](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2300/pg2300-images.html) that whatever we call art is also produced and enjoyed by birds, insects, and flowers. *For the same reasons*.

“Okay,” I hear you saying. “Maybe it’s true in a general way that the biosphere is random and ‘cheap,’ but surely there are some traits such as intelligence or tool use or imagination or opposable thumbs or reasoning powers that make humans special.” Brace yourself. What makes humans unique are just two things: [*throwing*](https://philpapers.org/rec/FERSYT-4) [and](https://philpapers.org/rec/FERSYT-4) [*sweating*](https://philpapers.org/rec/FERSYT-4). Don’t shoot the messenger. This is absolutely, cast-iron, biology-class true.

\_\_\_

Anthropocentrism is violent.

\_\_\_

“All right,” I hear just a few of you saying, “but philosophy has often argued that humans are special because they create their own environment(or ‘history’ or whatever you like). Georg Hegel and [Karl Marx](http://iai.tv/video/why-marx-was-right) were insistent on this.” Come on. This is Humanities run mad. Did these guys ever see a bird’s nest? A spider? For heaven’s sake, did they ever even consider one tiny single-celled organism? *The* *very definition of something with a metabolism* is that it produces its own environment. What do you think breathing and excreting do? And as for “history,” really this just means “the account of what someone or something did, over time.” In the olden days, what we now call biology was called *natural history*.

I think that just about destroys every possible reason for the thing called anthropocentrism.

*Anthropocentrism is violent*. Since it doesn’t fit anything in reality, it has to make its point violently. Destroying something to prove that you’re better than it doesn’t really prove anything: it’s just destroying something. There’s a difference between violence and symbolism. Violence is for when symbolism breaks down. “I hit him to make a point”: no, I didn’t. I just hit him.

*None of this article means that human beings are “bad.”* None of it. To oppose anthropocentrism is not to oppose actual human beings. Far from it. Anthropocentrism is a dangerous myth connected to other myths such as white supremacy. Scratch the surface of the Enlightenment idea of “man” (we still use it when we talk about “humanity”) and all too often you will find a white man (definitely *man*) underneath the seeming universality.

“Humanity” was born and “Rule, Britannia!” was written at the same historical moment. America was born then too, and although it set itself free from Britain, America has still not set itself free from the legacy of enslavement. It just so happens that America is one of the top polluters on Earth. I don’t believe that’s a coincidence.

\_\_\_

The Frankenstein’s monster of mastery over nature is now threatening to master all human beings.

\_\_\_

The “white man” of the eighteenth century is not an actual human being, but a construct. Even “whiteness” isn’t quite right. It’s more like *transparency*, the idea of a universal, one-size-fits all being, as [Denise Ferreira da Silva](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816649204/toward-a-global-idea-of-race/) argues. We could cast aside this idea and humans would love one another *better*. And the difference between me and a lemur would be less if I could reduce the difference between me and another human being. Anti-racism is a key component of the environmentalist toolkit.

“[Anti-humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antihumanism),” practiced by postmodern philosophers such as Jacques Derrida and Michel Foucault, is not the same as being against human beings. There is a very dangerous fascist story going around that has some origins in mishearings and distortions of anti-humanism, “[speculative realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speculative_realism)” ideas that human beings are in a sense evil and should be enslaved or even exterminated. That the enslaved person is a model for some kind of futuristic “[posthuman](https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/display/document/obo-9780190221911/obo-9780190221911-0122.xml)” being.

It’s almost like when a child says, *If I can’t have that toy, no one can!* and proceeds to smash it up. “If we aren’t special, then we should all be exterminated!” Do we really have to go through this Twilight of the (Human) Gods?

Opposing anthropocentrism doesn’t imply any of that. Quite the opposite. Thinking that some kind of AI should enslave everyone and everything for profit is the idea of the transparent, universal human unleashed, *anthropocentrism* *let loose upon the very human beings who thought it up in the first place*. Doesn’t a lot of our contemporary experience seem like the colonial and imperial chickens coming home to roost, as [Pankaj Mishra](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/01/17/opinion/sunday/brexit-ireland-empire.html?action=click&module=Opinion&pgtype=Homepage) and [Ta-Nehisi Coates](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2017/10/the-first-white-president-ta-nehisi-coates/537909/) have pointed out? The Frankenstein’s monster of mastery over nature is now threatening to master all human beings.
