# We can't trust experts, but we can't live without them

*Authority after the knowledge monopoly*

**By Reiner Grundmann** | *28th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,822 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![We can't trust experts, but we can't live without them](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/we-cant-trust-experts-but-we-cant-live-without-them2.webp)

---

*We used to think we could turn to experts and simply follow their lead. But today, with the democratization of knowledge on social media, that confidence has collapsed, and populists on the left and right have urged us to reject their expertise altogether. According to **Reiner Grundmann**, Professor of Science and Technology Studies at the University of Nottingham, this is a radical mistake. The problem isn’t expertise, but the failure to distinguish between crucially different kinds of experts. Once we see the difference, we must use our judgement to weight expert opinion on its merits, without wholesale acceptance or rejection.*

When we encounter a problem that calls for a decision, we often do not know where to start. Should I go with my gut feeling, do some research, ask a chatbot? The more serious the problem, the more likely that I will seek expert advice. The same goes for organizations, especially companies, governments, or public bodies. However, there are many experts, human and non-human, who offer their services, often with different messages.

[Some believe](https://academic.oup.com/book/55947) we should trust those experts with scientific credentials. Because scientists are competent to analyze situations and can provide informed choices for problem solving, they should be listened to. This might work where the problem is technical, and there is an uncontested science base from which to derive guidance. As soon as we allow for scientific debates on an issue, and for a plurality of desirable solutions, we realize that scientists also disagree on matters of concern; and that scientists often do not know what to do, as their facts do not imply specific actions to be taken. Experts have a different role: they are typically asked to answer the question: “what should we do?” Scientists do not have the authority, or competence, to answer this question. Experts should not be confused with scientists.

On the other side there is the view that [scientific expertise has been misused in the public sphere.](https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/5926/The-Weaponization-of-ExpertiseHow-Elites-Fuel) Instead of discussing the political questions underlying the problems, official, science-based expertise is often used to stifle dissent. This expert overreach actuates skepticism about official expertise. It comes in left-wing and right-wing versions, but both invoke democratic ideals or populist visions. Technocracy, epistocracy and illegitimate elite rule are the charges. These have been articulated by left-wing and right-wing voices, with a [shift in visibility and influence](https://doi.org/10.1080/02691728.2018.1546347) from the 1960s, which arguably saw a left-wing dominance (“counter-culture”), to the 2010s when right-wing populism rose.

Democratic institutions should enable citizens to expose and contest the assumptions and omissions embedded in expert knowledge, rather than choosing between unconditional deference to experts and wholesale rejection of expertise. In a way, societies have found a way to deal with this dilemma. It is time to capture the principles of such practices theoretically. Debates about expertise are often confusing, as different meanings of expertise are implied without distinguishing between them. Hence, I present some fundamental distinctions to refine the notion of an expert. Experts have different functions and play different roles in society. [I distinguish three types](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003320227).

The first type of expert has professional training or experience in a field. This is a *specialist.* Specialists apply rules to problems directly. We normally accept this modus operandi, which is the basis of public administration, the education system, healthcare, engineering, and other areas. This is also the basis for the functioning of expert-based organizations and operations, and for legitimate technocracy. Populists will not advocate that members of the lay public should be in command in an airplane cockpit. Interestingly, modern planes fly on autopilot, but in critical moments a human pilot takes over. Automated systems and robotics have been around long before the recent wave of AI applications, and we have delegated tasks to these systems in specific contexts.

Things are not so simple as soon as we consider uncertainties and value conflicts. In such situations, specialists are of little help. Suppose they propose a solution that they believe would work, without getting approval from relevant social groups—chances are that this will be contested. In this constellation we encounter a different type of expert, the *advisor*. Advisors know about the complexities of an issue and the difficulty of getting it right. Unlike the specialist, they cannot draw on a specific rule and apply it to the situation. Their role is to provide an evaluation of the most relevant aspects and advise on a course of action. Advisors are in a relation to a client who either pays for their service or has a close relationship based on trust. The latter type is known throughout history, at court, or at the heart of government.

However, closeness between advisors and advisees can cloud judgement. External advisors are employed to get an independent view. Common to most advisory settings is the plurality of viewpoints and options for political decision, which ironically leads to the involvement of even more advisors. Governments in democracies tend to institutionalize this process by setting up advisory committees which write reports containing recommendations. The composition of advisory boards allows one to steer towards a specific outcome, for which the expert body provides legitimation. Little surprise that people who disagree often take an anti-expert stance.

Specialists and advisors operate in specific contexts which are circumscribed. The specialist addresses well-known problems and proposes solutions which are drawn from a shared repertoire. The consultant or advisor is brought in to advise on complex and contested issues which escape the logic of applying solutions to problems. The consultant is one step removed from decision-making. The articulation of options, guidance and recommendations depends on how the root problem is defined. It involves the [framing of the problem](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780691218946/html), and this prefigures the range and type of potential solutions. This points to the [social construction of knowledge](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315080512), in which narratives play an important role. Alarming and reassuring narratives are well-known examples in many policy fields.

This is where the third type of expert enters the scene: the *commentator*. Commentators share some of the advisors’ traits but operate in public discourse. They provide interpretation, opinion, and sometimes calls for action. But they are not in a relation to a client—they offer their comments to a wide public. They are even further removed from decision-making than the advisor. This category of expert has become important in a relatively short period of time, driven by two factors: the expansion of the education system, and the rise of social media. AI has widened and accelerated this process further. These drivers have boosted the size of available knowledge and the number of commentators.

This is a mixed blessing. On the one hand, it exemplifies the virtues of democracy, as the hegemonic position of mass media and traditional journalism is challenged. In this new world, everyone with access to the internet can be a journalist. But more knowledge, and more types and sources of knowledge, does not mean that they are reliable or trustworthy. This in turn fuels the demand for experts who can provide us with guidance. Recall the situation described above, where the rulers need to select from a plurality of possible evaluations and options. Now the public at large is confronted with the same task. Some commentators see this as a sign that expertise is in crisis. They are badly mistaken. What it actually signals is [a crisis of legitimate authority](https://iai.tv/video/the-ignorance-of-experts), even as demand for expertise grows stronger than ever.

I have suggested theorizing experts according to their role and function. Three ideal types are prominent: the specialist, the advisor, and the commentator. As with all ideal types, these are in reality often blurred. Hybrid roles have emerged where the roles of specialists and advisors are mixed, as the case of medical doctors shows. A doctor prescribes a course of antibiotics when a patient presents with signs of a bacterial infection. Patients with complicated symptoms or unclear diagnosis are treated differently. Patients are often well informed and expect the doctor to advise them, given the uncertainties. It is up to the patients to accept or reject the advice, or to seek a second opinion.

The roles of advisor and commentator can also mix. Prominent examples are members of an *official* expert panel who pronounce their opinions in public. During the Covid pandemic, some UK scientists belonging to the official advisory structure SAGE joined a competing expert group called Independent SAGE and commented in public about the shortcomings of government policy. The same pattern could be observed in Germany, where the German Ethics Council was publicly split, and some members appeared in the media with more assertive individual views on vaccine mandates or lockdown ethics than the Council's consensus text reflected. Climate change is another high-profile case that provides many examples where members of the IPCC make public statements that go beyond the official reports.

Based on the theory and typology suggested above, some important lessons can be drawn which help us to understand expertise in practice. First, there are tasks that are dealt with by specialists, who are entrusted with those tasks. Their decisions will not normally, or in principle, be challenged. Matters are different where the task environment is different. This situation calls for advisors who navigate a complex and uncertain world and competing values. Their advice is requested by clients who want an assessment of the situation and options for decision-making. Commentators also operate in a complex world with competing values, but also competing opinions. No one requests their assessment or opinion; they contribute to public debate.

My argument suggests that the three different types of experts involve different levels of accountability. When information or advice is flawed, the commentator may lose an audience, the advisor may lose a client, and a specialist may lose a licence. AI chatbots don’t really have anything to lose if their advice is wrong.

Defenders of technocratic rule will usually interpret the situation of an uncertain world with competing value conflicts as it befits the specialist. A well-known variant of this approach is to [proclaim that the issue is science-based](https://iai.tv/articles/scientific-consensus-needs-dissent-auid-2926), and thus it does not matter if the proposed solution is met with resistance. Facts and evidence will be mobilized to avoid discussions about complex value-laden problems. This reaction is understandable as it rests on long experience with state bureaucracies that marshalled evidence from scientific elites.

Today, the democratization of knowledge means we live in a different world. There is no way to turn back the clock and revert to knowledge monopolies that [exclude unwelcome views](https://iai.tv/articles/the-organised-hypocrisy-of-experts-auid-1634). It can be challenging for many to accept this reality. The democratization of knowledge means a shrinking space for the good old technocratic mode of government. To be sure, there is still room for specialists and for science-based policies. But the horizon needs to be broadened when trying to understand the challenges and to find solutions that are appropriate for our times.
