# We don't understand matter any better than mind

*There is no 'mind-body problem'*

**By John Collins** | *31st January 2025* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,314 words*

![We don't understand matter any better than mind](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/there-is-no-such-thing-as-a-body.webp)

---

*It is commonly believed that there is a mind-body problem because we can give an explanation of matter but not of the mind. But according to John Collins, we don’t understand matter either. Materialism was refuted by Newton in the 17th century, and the physicalism which has replaced it is not a substantive doctrine. There are gaps in our understanding of the mental – we still do not have a good theory of what the mind is – but after Newton, there is no ‘mind-body problem’.*

Minds are problematic. We don’t quite know what they are. If one has a mind, let us agree, then one is sentient and sapient, able to be self-aware and to think and reason about things. But both of these qualities are opaque, not least because they have no apparent analogy in the non-mental world. What, exactly, does one need to add to a body to get a mind? How is a thing that is merely subject to physical laws self-aware and able to think about dinosaurs and the afterlife? This conundrum is invariably depicted in terms of there being a mind-body problem. I’ll try in what follows to give you reasons to think that this label is at best misleading. There are lots of mind problems and lots of body problems, but no mind-body problem, because there is no realm of bodies in some general sense from which minds are excluded. Before you call for an intervention, let me explain.

**A potted history**

First, we need some history. The mind-body problem in its contemporary guise originates with Descartes in the 17th century. Here is a simplified version of how he reasoned. We are aware of minds (our own, at least) and bodies about us. If minds just are bodies, then they must share their properties, for there is just the one thing, and nothing can both have and lack a given property. For Descartes, however, bodies are in space and so divisible, but minds are not divisible and are not in space. Thus, minds are not bodies. Note that Descartes is not arguing that bodies cannot support mental properties, but more arguing that minds don’t have material properties such as being in space.

Descartes concluded that there are really two kinds of things (three, if we include God) with categorically distinct properties: minds and bodies. Two interaction problems arise: (i) how can bodies causally influence minds (toothache, say); and (ii) how can minds causally influence bodies (deciding to stand up, say).  Following Descartes, a number of philosophers (the so-called ‘occasionalists’, such as Cordemoy, La Forge, and Malebranche) argued that these interaction problems were genuinely insuperable and concluded that the regular arrangement of the interaction of minds and bodies is not causal at all; indeed, the inertness of bodies means that there are no genuine causal relations at all, not even between bodies. One thing is merely an *occasion* for another, an occasion for God to make one thing follow something else. In effect, if we subtract God, we are left with a kind of scepticism: there is regularity, for sure, but no known cause (Hume made this move in the 18th century influenced by the occasionalists). Alternatively, there is causation, but this is our imposition of necessity upon phenomena rather than an independent necessity our minds are aware of (Kant).

The significant thing to note about this post-Cartesian discussion is that the mind-body interaction problem was generalised; that is, mind-body interaction becomes just a particular instance of the problem of how *anything* interacts. There are two features of the history that are crucial to understanding this generalisation.

First, materialism in the 17th century was something of a revival of the ancient doctrine running from Democritus, through Epicurus, to Lucretius. According to materialism, on this understanding, everything reduces down to indivisible simples or ‘atoms’ whose conjoining and interaction-by-contact account for all phenomena (this was married to a view of empty space – the void – or a plenum – a full space).  In this sense, Descartes, the occasionalists who followed him, and his English critics, such as Hobbes and Boyle, were all materialists. But the mind remained problematic as an apparently non-material outlier. Yet materialism faced (at least) three serious problems.

First, if ‘atoms’ are genuine simples, then they can’t be divided, but if they are in space, they have dimensions, and so can be divided after all (a top and bottom half, say).  Thus, we would appear to be left with infinite divisibility. If we seek to bring a halt to divisibility at some level, it is difficult not to see that cut-off point as mere stipulation, so long as the simples are in space. Leibniz concluded, from such reasoning that simples are not in space at all, which is to give up on materialism as understood.

Secondly, the atoms, in virtue of being simples, would appear to be inert, lacking any properties that would explain causal interaction. There must be some missing ingredient that explains the lawfulness of interaction, or merely the regularity of events.

Thirdly, according to materialism, all causal relations are realised by contact; there are no ‘occult’ properties, understood as sympathies and antipathies between objects with no intermediary mechanism. Yet it would appear that the planetary system is occult, for what holds the planets in place in relation to one another? Serious thinkers hypothesised, among other things, a vortex, invisible cords, and magnetism to explain the planetary system. It remained a profound mystery.

\_\_\_

For Newton, science should content itself with the mathematical framing of laws unburdened by assumptions of materialism; indeed, from a materialist perspective, gravity appeared occult.

\_\_\_

So, although materialism was dominant in the 17th century, it was not free of problems.

The second factor that encouraged a general understanding of the problem of interaction is the development of classical mechanics by Isaac Newton, who had a profound influence on Hume’s scepticism and Kant’s eschewal of the world as it is in itself. Newton effectively showed that materialism was moribund, if not already dead. He explicitly rejected indivisible atoms and his hypothesis of universal gravitation, a long-range instantaneous relation, came with no contact mechanism. For Newton, science should content itself with the mathematical framing of laws unburdened by assumptions of materialism; indeed, from a materialist perspective, gravity appeared occult.

So, the post-Cartesian tradition of finding *all* causal relations, not just mind-body ones, problematic was perfectly rational against the backdrop of the science of the day. Chomsky has remarked that Newton destroyed the machine, while leaving the ghost intact. As a historical remark, this reflects the scholarly understanding of the period.

But this is all history, surely. Why should we care what happened in the 17th and 18th centuries? Well, the question to ask now is whether some notion of body arose post-Newton against which the mind counts as an outlier. It turns out that the answer is not at all straightforward.

**Physicalism**

Materialism did not make a comeback in the 19th century. Lange’s magisterial and seminal *History of Materialism* relegated it to a method as opposed to a serious metaphysics, and early 20th century physics, while deeply concerned with non-locality action-at-a-distance issues classical physics had bequeathed, resolved the issues in a thoroughly non-materialist manner, as Einstein himself made clear. Such problems remain.

\_\_\_

While old-school materialism was essentially a metaphysical doctrine, to which science was expected to conform prior to Newton, physicalism insists on the converse, that our metaphysics must conform to science (well, physics).

\_\_\_

Yet, many people still call themselves materialists. This label is anachronistic at best, for materialism is dead, if understood as a 17th Century doctrine. What people seem to intend is a doctrine often referred to as *physicalism*, which claims that what is real is either what physics establishes or what can be reduced to (/explained by) physics. Note the difference. While old-school materialism was essentially a metaphysical doctrine, to which science was expected to conform prior to Newton, physicalism insists on the converse, that our metaphysics must conform to science (well, physics). This supposed adherence to science can be questioned, for it hardly follows from any *particular* science that there is a unified physical realm. Let this quibble pass, though. As regards the mind, at any rate, the doctrine is that if minds are real, they are *really* something else, the kind of stuff physics could handle, at least in principle.

Physicalism, however, faces a dilemma: what science, exactly, is supposed to tell us what is real? There appear to be three possible choices. *Past* science won’t do, because it sanctioned all kinds of entities and forces we no longer accept. Nor will *current* science do, for it is incomplete, and it is mere hubris to think that it is near completion – the more we know, the less we know. Nor even can it be *future* science, for it is unclear what that is. On the face of it, then, physicalism is not a substantive doctrine, unlike materialism, which at least had the virtue of being false. But is this dilemma escape-proof?

One could respond to the dilemma by construing physicalism not so much as a metaphysical claim, but just as the idea that mental phenomena should be explained in ‘lower-level’ terms, which would pattern the mental along with biology and chemistry vis-à-vis physics. Reduction in this sense, the thought might continue, characterises scientific progress generally.

\_\_\_

Similarly, knowing where something happens in the brain has all the explanatory depth of pointing to a carburettor to explain the internal combustion engine. We know so little about the brain that it is little wonder mentality seems problematic.

\_\_\_

Yet this conception of science is simply not true. The physics of the late 19th century was incompatible with the time-scales of evolutionary biology, and it was the physics that changed. More recently, it was only with the development of quantum theory that chemical bonding could be understood, about which we already had an established mathematically formulated theory. In the case of the mind, a leading cognitive scientist, Randy Gallistel, has remarked that we don’t even know how a brain can add 1 and 1. Similarly, knowing where something happens in the brain has all the explanatory depth of pointing to a carburettor to explain the internal combustion engine. We know so little about the brain that it is little wonder mentality seems problematic.

It might be thought that even if we don’t have a clear idea of what the physical is, we know enough to know that minds have properties that are incompatible with what we do know. But this thought does not really constitute a mind-body problem but the mere acknowledgement that we don’t have a good general theory of mentality, whether in humans or animals. Such gaps, though, are hardly peculiar in the sciences. What gave Descartes’ characterisation of the mind-body problem such impetus was precisely the idea that materialism could explain *everything*, apart from the mind. We now know that his materialism is false and that there are gaps throughout all of the sciences, including, especially, how the brain works, let alone dark matter and the gap between micro and macro physical processes.

It might, though, be further thought that the mind gap is special, but in the absence of a good theory of the mind, there is little to appeal to here save for intuitions. For example, there was no good theory of colour prior to quantum mechanics, but no one spoke of a colour-body problem. Where we do have good theories, then the gaps become more than intuitive.  Thus, we have good theories of micro and macro physical processes, but still can’t bridge the gap. We have good theories of chemistry and biology, but still don’t understand the origin of life. We don’t even understand why humans favour one hand over another, still less why it tends to be the right.

\_\_\_

The moral, rather, is that there is no grand and peculiar mind-body problem, for there is no fixed sense of what the non-mental is. The mind remains opaque in its own terms, at least to the extent that central phenomena, such as free action, linguistic production, self-awareness, and so on, are in want of any plausible theory.

\_\_\_

**What I have not argued**

Rejections of materialism tend to be pitched in anti-scientific terms, or with recommendations to acknowledge different realms or kinds of understanding. I have not argued for either. Materialism was refuted by 17th-century science, and no corresponding general metaphysics has replaced it. If you think that only science offers knowledge, then nothing I have said should perturb you. Science might well be the only source of genuine knowledge, from which it doesn’t follow that we do or even could understand everything. Equally, nothing I have said counts against any first-order work on mental phenomena that seeks to integrate them with biophysical phenomena, for no such work seeks to solve a mind-body problem. Sapience and sentience remain topics of serious inquiry, but explanations here neither presuppose nor entail any general theory of body.

I have not, even, acknowledged the mind to be a ‘hard problem’, something that potentially requires a new science. The moral, rather, is that there is no grand and peculiar mind-body problem, for there is no fixed sense of what the non-mental is. The mind remains opaque in its own terms, at least to the extent that central phenomena, such as free action, linguistic production, self-awareness, and so on, are in want of any plausible theory. Progress has been made and we may hope for future progress, but there is no grand problem to solve, just, in all likelihood, many, many little problems stretching to the horizon of our methods of empirical inquiry – just like in any other area of inquiry, whether it be maths, physics, or biology. Such a conclusion might seem depressingly pedestrian, but if the world is recalcitrant to our theories, so much more so is it indifferent to the demands of publishers’ blurbs for the latest solution to the ‘mind-body problem’.
