# Žižek: Quantum physics shows reality is incomplete

*An interview with Slavoj Žižek*

**By Slavoj Žižek** | *6th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,419 words*

*Despite being our most successful scientific theory, quantum mechanics raises deep ontological questions about the nature of reality—questions that bring philosophy back to the forefront of our quest to understand the universe. Slavoj Žižek argues that we should take it’s philosophical consequences seriously, we should see reality as incomplete.*

In Žižek's most recent book [*Christian Atheism: How to Be a Real Materialist*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/christian-atheism-9781350409323/), he addresses the relationship between quantum mechanics and film, using [Everything Everywhere All at Once](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt6710474/) as a metaphor for quantum superposition and the randomness of reality. However, Žižek sees this cinematic portrayal as a negative example, warning against the misuse of complex scientific topics in popular culture.

***“Two points I’ll try to be as brief as possible. First, I will begin with my usual paradox. First, of course, I haven't seen the movie. I tried to watch for five, ten minutes… it's boring, but I saw some clips and summaries for context. I meant to use this starting metaphor as a negative example, as a warning of how topics developed in an imminently scientific way in quantum mechanics are used and predominantly misused in popular culture.***

***The idea of superpositions is often misrepresented as a freedom of choice, as if human subjects can simply choose which of the multiple realities to actualise. This is a total misreading. The collapse of superpositions is contingent and out of our control."***

Žižek frequently criticises how pop culture misappropriates scientific concepts for metaphors that distort their true meaning. He highlights this negative example of how quantum mechanics, specifically superposition, is turned into a metaphor for [human freedom](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/does-quantum-physics-rule-out-free-will/):

***"The idea that we can choose between realities, like selecting a cake from a menu, completely misses the point. Superposition collapse is not a matter of choice, but of uncontrollable contingency."***

This critique extends to even serious science popularisers, who Žižek believes often oversimplify the [implications of quantum mechanics](http://articles/quantum-mechanics-and-the-return-of-free-will-tim-andersen-auid-2475):

***"The collapse of superpositions is not like choosing a dessert. It's a process beyond human control, contingent on factors we cannot dictate."***

Žižek's interest lies not in critiquing quantum physics itself but in exploring the philosophical implications it presents. He points out the paradox that, despite quantum physics being one of the most rigorously tested theories in science, it leads to wildly different philosophical interpretations.

***"Quantum physics compels us to radically change our notion of objective reality,"*** Žižek says.

He acknowledges the challenges posed by the different interpretations of quantum mechanics, from [Niels Bohr's view of quantum waves](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-copenhagen/) as mere epistemological tools to the [multiverse theory](http://articles/the-multiverse-is-unscientific-nonsense-auid-2668), which posits that all superpositions are realised. The differing theories on the Big Bang and the ontological status of quantum waves are of particular interest to Žižek, who marvels at the philosophical questions about realism and determinism they raise.

Bringing him back to the upcoming debate, I asked him about how this holds up against the views of Sabine Hossenfelder, who advocates for the idea of superdeterminism. This theory suggests that quantum mechanics is part of a larger, deterministic framework, in line with Einstein’s quest for a more complete theory of reality. Žižek, building on his attack of superdeterminism and the old materialism of the past, argues that the indeterminacy we observe is not a flaw in the theory but really is a fundamental aspect of reality itself.

Žižek also offers a striking metaphor in which he imagines God as a ‘lazy creator’ who left the universe unfinished, assuming humans would never understand its deeper structure:

***“When God created the universe, he thought, we humans are too stupid. We will never progress beyond the structure of the atom. So God said, ‘wait a minute, why should I lose time with filling in all the details?’ And my metaphor, which with pleasure, I learned, is also used by some quantum physicists, is that quantum mechanics, with this indeterminacy, as it were, catches God with his pants down.***

***The obscene expression gives some kind of intellectual orgasm. Intellectual to me, is precisely this idea that what we perceive the limit of our knowledge. But what if this is a feature of reality in itself? What if reality is in itself is incomplete?***

***I am a realist. But I reject the spiritualist readings of quantum mechanics—the idea that observers create reality. The greatness of quantum mechanics is its ontological incompleteness – reality itself is incomplete […] Anyone who claims quantum physics proves there's no material reality would get a one-way ticket to lifelong Gulag,"*** he jokes.

This Stalinist metaphor reflects his commitment to a realist interpretation of the universe, one that acknowledges the inherent incompleteness of reality rather than reducing it to human perception.

He goes on to express his admiration for both Hossenfelder and Penrose, emphasizing his desire to engage with their ideas during the festival. Žižek is particularly keen to challenge the deterministic interpretations of quantum mechanics, seeing them as an oversimplification of the profound indeterminacy at the heart of the quantum world.

He recounts a podcast interview with Penrose where he had argued that the very fact of observation collapse of quantum waves through observation couldn’t be properly accounted for in quantum terms. So for him, this very duality is a proof that quantum mechanics is not a complete theory. But Žižek takes the mainstream view seriously, arguing:

***“There are now two main tendencies in how to interpret - we cannot call it reality in the ordinary sense - let's say quantum-oscillations’ domain. I think they are not just epistemological tools, probability calculations. I think that, since experiments prove that, in order to fully account for how this classical one particle photon travels from point A to point B, to explain the results - this is the point of all the double slit experiments and so on. You have to accept that in some sense, although it collapses in one point, the particle took all the paths.”***

Žižek’s approach to quantum mechanics highlights the deep philosophical questions that arise from this complex field of science. While he admires the rigor of quantum physics, he believes that its implications force us to reconsider our basic assumptions about reality. Žižek’s provocative and often humorous style brings a unique perspective to the discussion, which shall make for an excellent addition to the upcoming panel.

He also critiques materialist interpretations within quantum physics, such as those of physicist Carlo Rovelli. Although he admires Rovelli’s work, Žižek questions his idea that any interaction between particles could be considered a form of observation:

***"Rovelli suggests that whenever particles interact, one is 'observing' the other. But this interpretation reduces observation to mere interaction, which I find problematic.”***

For Žižek, such theories fail to fully grasp the deeper philosophical implications of quantum indeterminacy.

Žižek’s admiration for Sabine Hossenfelder is also evident and leads into a more serious reflection on the relationship between philosophy and quantum mechanics. Žižek is firm in his belief that philosophers still have something essential to contribute to the conversation, especially in questioning the limitations of scientific theories. When asked whether philosophers can teach quantum physicists anything about reality, Žižek responds with his Hegelian insight:

***“What if the limits of our knowledge are not just our limits but limits in reality itself?”***

This is where philosophy comes in, not to provide empirical answers but to interrogate the very framework in which science operates. For Žižek, quantum mechanics compels us to radically rethink the nature of reality, and philosophers have a crucial role in that rethinking, offering insights that science, in its pursuit of objectivity, might overlook.
