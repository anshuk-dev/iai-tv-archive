# Recent claims we all see the same colors are mistaken

*Is your red my red?*

**By Keith Allen** | *18th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,564 words*

![Recent claims we all see the same colors are mistaken](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/recent-claims-we-see-all-the-same-colors-are-mistaken.webp)

---

*A new study has been interpreted as the strongest evidence yet that we all see the same colors,* *but philosopher **Keith Allen** argues this assumption is mistaken. The original study claimed that because we have similar structures of color experience - for example, this turquoise is more blue than green - then these similar structures point to us also experiencing the same underlying colors. However, even one instance of difference in a structure changes the relationships throughout it. Meaning if I do see red differently from you, I also perceive every other color differently too - even if our underlying structures of experience remain the same.*

When you look at a tomato, is the color you see the same as the color someone else sees? According to the classic philosophical thought experiment, a [version of which](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualia-inverted/#LocInvSpeSce) is described by the early modern philosopher John Locke, we can imagine two people whose color experiences are systematically inverted relative to each other: when one person looks at a tomato, it looks the same way that fresh grass looks to the other person, and vice versa. But could we ever know they have different experiences, if both people describe tomatoes as “red” and grass as “green”? Indeed, can we imagine someone who looks at the tomato and lacks conscious experience altogether, even though they talk and act exactly like everyone else? It seems easy enough to imagine robots that are able to identify tomatoes as red and grass as green, and in fact we already have LLMs that can accurately estimate similarities between colors (represented by hex color codes). Perhaps we can also imagine people like this, too: “philosophical zombies” who walk among us, behave exactly like us, but have no inner mental lives whatsoever.

Exciting [new research](https://www.cell.com/iscience/fulltext/S2589-0042(25)00289-5) by Naotsugu Tsuchiya, Masafumi Oizumi, and colleagues seeks to answer these longstanding questions. Rather than focusing on individual color experiences, and asking whether two people looking at the same object have the same color experience, their approach is holistic and analyzes relations between color experiences. According to recent coverage in the [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2470759-do-we-all-see-red-as-the-same-colour-we-finally-have-an-answer/), their study provides “the strongest evidence yet” that people share the “same subjective experiences of color.”

Colors stand in distinctive relations of similarity and difference: for example, green is more similar to turquoise than red; red is more similar to pink than yellow. The basic idea behind the research is to ask participants to make judgments about how similar or different pairs of colors are, and then use computational analysis to investigate whether different people’s experiences of color stand in the same overall relations of similarity and difference. The hypothesis is that if two people’s experiences have the same overall structure, then their experiences are identical; if two people’s experiences have different structures, then their experiences are different.

Large language models (LLMs) can generate color similarity structures that can be matched with those of normal perceivers, but it does not follow that LLMs enjoy conscious color experiences.

\_\_\_

The goal of creating systematic representations of similarity relations between colors is not new. There is a long history of attempts to produce color-order systems that seek to represent relationships between the colors. A well-known example is the [Munsell Color System](https://munsell.com/about-munsell-color/how-color-notation-works/). What is novel about this approach is the specific form of computational analysis it uses to compare what the researchers call different “qualia structures.”

This approach is an example of an increasingly popular “structuralist” approach to the problem of consciousness. The general idea is to describe conscious experiences in terms of mathematical structures, to provide a better understanding of “what it is like” to have those conscious experiences. Ultimately, the hope is that this will provide a way of “cracking” the so-called “[hard problem of consciousness](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2519288-the-simple-questions-cracking-the-hard-problem-of-consciousness/%5d.).”

This is an interesting suggestion, but there are limitations to what the study establishes. One limitation of the recent study that the researchers themselves acknowledge, for example, is that it only compares qualia structures for groups of perceivers, not individuals. How this approach applies at an individual level remains to be seen. This is an important question because there is existing evidence of variation in color perception even among otherwise “normal” perceivers (perceivers who pass standard color vision tests).

Colors can be described in terms of their similarity to six psychologically basic referents: green, blue, yellow, red, black, and white. This is the basis of the [Natural Color System](https://ncscolour.com/), a system for describing colors widely used by artists and architects. These colors are psychologically basic in the sense they have instances that are “unique”, and which cannot be described in terms of other colors: for example, while every instance of orange can be described in terms of red and yellow, there are instances of red that are neither yellowish, bluish, greenish. [Previous research](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/col.10237) has found apparently substantial variation in the perception of the unique hues, and “unique green” in particular (i.e., a green that doesn’t look yellowish, bluish, or reddish). Indeed, in one study, some participants saw as unique green a light that others saw as unique blue. Is my green the same as yours? There is a reasonable chance that something that looks unique green to me will not look unique green to you.

At a group level, the researchers were able to show that the qualia structures of “normal” color perceivers are distinct from those of perceivers who are red-green “colorblind”. This demonstrates that their methodology successfully distinguishes between people whose experiences differ. However, it also serves to highlight a wider limitation of this approach, since it appears to leave open substantive questions about what it is like to be red-green colorblind.

A natural way to think of the qualia structure of someone who is colorblind is as a sub-region of the larger space associated with a “normal” perceiver. On this view, someone who is colorblind has the very same conscious experiences of blue, yellow, black, and white as a “normal” perceiver, but just can’t see reds and greens. But this is not the only possibility. It might be that changing the overall structure of the qualia space thereby changes the elements within the structure, so that someone who is colorblind doesn’t see blue, yellow, black, and white at all—or at least not in the same way that “normal” perceivers do.

Consider an analogy: would someone who is an only child be the same person if their parents had had more children, such that they had grown up with siblings? In some sense, yes, although in some sense, no: the family structure in which you are embedded arguably makes a difference to who you are. The natural way of thinking of colorblindness might turn out to be the correct way (see [here](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/mila.12370) for further discussion), but on the face of it, a purely structural description cannot decide between these alternative theories of what it is like to be colorblind.

If asymmetries such as these mean that inverted qualia structures would differ, then these experiences could be shown to be different. However, remember that the methodology is supposed to be an entirely general one for studying all [types of conscious experience](https://iai.tv/video/the-structure-of-consciousness). Even if human color space is actually asymmetrical, it seems conceivable that there could be other conscious creatures whose color spaces are symmetrical. Or perhaps there could be qualia structures that describe other sensory experiences, like taste or smell, emotional experiences, or aesthetic experiences, that are symmetrical. What these possibilities help to illustrate are the limits of describing conscious experiences in structural terms. Describing conscious experiences in terms of their place within a structure in effect describes them only indirectly.

Related concerns arise when considering the claim that two perceivers with the same qualia structures will have the same conscious experience. This is only supposed to be a necessary condition, not a sufficient condition. Large language models (LLMs) can generate color similarity structures that can be matched with those of normal perceivers, but it does not follow that [LLMs enjoy conscious color experiences](https://iai.tv/video/the-future-of-artificial-minds). So, something else is needed for conscious experience, beyond just a qualia structure of a certain kind. But again, this illustrates the limitations of this way of describing conscious experience. I can presumably only know that my conscious experience of red is the same as your conscious experience of red if I can know that you have conscious experiences, full stop. But the fact that our qualia structures match doesn’t show this: it is consistent with your responses that you have [no conscious experiences at all](https://iai.tv/articles/llms-show-language-does-not-describe-reality-auid-3578).

This concern perhaps reflects a wider issue with debates about consciousness. The problem is often framed as a problem about understanding what goes on inside our minds. If we start from the assumption that our conscious experiences are private to us, then it is always going to be difficult to determine whether the thing to which only I have access is the same as the thing to which only you have access. But note that when participants are asked to make similarity judgments about colors, for example, they aren’t being asked to make judgments about their experiences or anything inside their minds; they are asked to make judgments about colors presented on computer screens, which on the face of it are parts of a shared physical world. Perhaps if our [similarity judgments do coincide](https://iai.tv/video/evidence-in-the-dock), this isn’t because the “qualia” inside our heads are the same; perhaps it is just because we are making judgments about the same features of our shared environments: colors.
