# Experiences of the uncanny reveal cracks in our metaphysics

*Doppelgangers, automatons, and the gaps in our beliefs*

**By Mark Windsor** | *7th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,327 words*

![Experiences of the uncanny reveal cracks in our metaphysics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/experiences-of-the-uncanny2.webp)

---

*From waxwork figures to David Lynch films, we’re often both attracted and repelled by the uncanny. Philosopher **Mark Windsor** argues this reveals something deep about our psychology and the relationship between reality and our theories. The uncanny arises, he suggests, when experience contradicts our most deeply held—often unconscious—beliefs about the world. Automata that appear too lifelike, for example, threaten our conviction that life is non-mechanical. The uncanny thus reveals to us our implicit theories while also probing their limits, showing us how they fail to capture reality itself.*

Simulacra of humans, from waxworks to automata, tend to produce in us a certain feeling of the uncanny. There is something both disturbing and fascinating about such figures, especially when they are very lifelike. In E. T. A. Hoffmann’s short story “Automata,” an ingenious mechanical figure called the Talking Turk arrives in town and is soon attracting visitors from morning to night. The Turk is about the size of a human being, “dressed in a rich and tasteful Turkish costume,” and sits on a low stool. Visitors are invited to approach him one by one and whisper a question in his ear, upon which the Turk turns, “first his eyes, and then his whole head,” to give his answer. Visitors feel a “gentle stream of air, like breath coming from his lips” when he speaks.

Such figures “can scarcely be said to counterfeit humanity so much as to travesty it,” says Lewis, a character in the story, who as a child ran away crying from an exhibition of waxworks. Even to this day, he cannot enter such places without a “horrible, eerie, shuddery feeling.”

We all know [this uncanny feeling](https://iai.tv/video/the-secrets-of-the-uncanny). Various kinds of objects and situations can evoke it. For example, it might be a strange coincidence, or a suggestion of the paranormal. Yet simulacra of humans that are too lifelike seem to have a capacity to elicit uncanny feelings with particular consistency. What is it that makes them so disturbing? The answer, I suggest, is that they threaten our very sense of who we are—of what it means to be human.

In "[The Uncanny Valley](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6213238)," the roboticist Masahiro Mori hypothesised a relationship between the degree of a figure’s verisimilitude and the positive or negative feelings it elicits. As a figure becomes more lifelike, it elicits increasingly positive feelings of affinity, but only up to a point. Past a certain degree of lifelikeness, the effect of the figure changes into one of negative affinity or uncanniness: it falls into the uncanny valley. The only way out of the valley is to become virtually indistinguishable from a human being.

It is questionable whether it is always possible to escape the uncanny valley by becoming perceptually indistinguishable from a real human. Having the *thought* that [something that appears just like a real human is *not* a real human](https://iai.tv/articles/who-is-rachael-the-philosophy-of-blade-runner-and-memory-auid-885) seems uncanny enough. This is a possibility that [advances in robotics and AI are bringing uncomfortably close](https://iai.tv/video/the-future-of-artificial-minds) to home. Yet there is a deeper assumption that Mori’s hypothesis rests upon that I want to call into question here: that ordinary, healthy human beings represent the antipode of uncanniness.

To see why we might want to question this, it will be helpful to consider some examples of other, related kinds of uncanny phenomena. It is often observed that there is something uncanny about looking at identical twins, such as we see in Diane Arbus’s famous photograph [*Identical Twins, Roselle, New Jersey, 1967*](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/67958/identical-twins-roselle-n-j). Notice that the effect of looking at identical twins is remarkably similar to that of looking at waxworks and other very lifelike human figures. Yet twins are not uncanny because they threaten the boundary between the human and the non-human, or the animate and the inanimate. Rather, they are uncanny because they appear to be the same person.

We find the uncanny effect of identical twins amplified in that uncanny motif par excellence, the doppelgänger. In Edgar Allan Poe’s classic doppelgänger story "[William Wilson](https://www.eapoe.org/works/tales/wilwilb.htm),” the narrator describes how he is troubled by a fellow pupil at his school who has a remarkably similar appearance to himself, a very similar name (William Wilson; we never learn what the narrator’s name is), and even a similar gait, manners, and voice. One evening, the narrator sneaks into Wilson’s bedroom while Wilson is asleep and peers closely at this face:

*I looked, and a numbness, an iciness of feeling instantly pervaded my frame … Gasping for breath, I lowered the lamp in still nearer proximity to the face. Were these—these the lineaments of William Wilson? I saw, indeed, that they were his, but I shook as with a fit of the ague in fancying they were not. What was there about them to confound me in this manner? I gazed—while my brain reeled with a multitude of incoherent thoughts.*

The terrifying thought, which the narrator grapples with and vainly tries to push away, is that Wilson *is himself*. I expect that the thought of encountering a person *who is yourself* is enough to send a shiver down anyone’s spine.

Twins and doppelgängers are disturbing because they pose a threat to [our sense of personal identity](https://iai.tv/video/life-story) and autonomy. To the extent that another person appears to be oneself, one is not fully in possession either of oneself or one’s own actions. Uncanny disturbances of this kind are not limited to twins and doppelgangers, however. There is something decidedly uncanny about seeing people sleepwalking—an effect brilliantly exploited in Robert Wiene’s 1920 film [*The Cabinet of Dr Caligari*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hTpjmTF1Lfk). Sleepwalkers present images of people who have lost control of themselves; hence, the impression they give of someone possessed.

One last example. The neurologist Oliver Sacks, in his book [*A Leg to Stand On*](https://www.oliversacks.com/oliver-sacks-books/a-leg-to-stand-on/), describes his experience of recovering from an operation on his left leg following a mountaineering accident. For about two weeks after the operation, the leg became “depersonalised”—he could no longer “recognise” it as his own, leaving in its place a lifeless, alien thing. The experience, he said, was “inconceivably shocking and uncanny.” Sacks’s depersonalised leg reveals the material contingency and fragility of one’s embodied self—the one thing we take for granted before all else.

These uncanny phenomena show how deeply ingrained certain ways of thinking about what makes us human are in us. Many people say that they do not believe in [free will](https://iai.tv/articles/free-will-vs-determinism-can-you-escape-your-fate-auid-1861). Many people hold to a [physicalist picture of the mind](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-9991.2008.00163.x), according to which conscious experiences are identical to, or functions of, physical brain states. Yet if someone fully believed—believed all the way down, as it were—in these things, we would not expect them to find anything disturbing about being presented with the brute materiality and contingency of their embodied self (as in the case of Sacks’s depersonalised leg), or to find they have the ability to go about everyday tasks in an apparently non-conscious state (as sleepwalkers appear to do). Yet these phenomena clearly maintain their power to disturb in spite of what theoretical beliefs one might hold [regarding the self](https://iai.tv/video/the-self-and-the-world) or free will: Sacks’s experience is a case in point. When Hoffman’s character Lewis says that mechanical figures “can scarcely be said to counterfeit humanity so much as to travesty it,” maybe the truth is that they travesty not what it is to be human but an idea of it that we cling to—and that [may be impossible to shake off](https://iai.tv/video/galen-strawson-free-will-is-a-necessary-illusion2)—despite what science tells us and many of us profess to believe.

Mori’s idea that a normal, healthy human being offers an escape route from the uncanny valley might be wishful thinking. We find hyper-realistic figures uncanny less because *they* are too much like *us* than because *we* are too much like *them*. The automaton prompts us to ask whether we are not also essentially mechanical; the sleepwalker makes us wonder whether we are not all already sleepwalking. If there is no escape from the uncanny valley, it might just be because we find ourselves at the bottom of it.
