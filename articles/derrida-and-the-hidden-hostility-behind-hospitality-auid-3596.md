# Derrida and the hidden hostility behind hospitality

*All welcomes conceal a rejection*

**By Rachael Newberry** | *12th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,507 words*

![Derrida and the hidden hostility behind hospitality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/RESIZED-2-derrida-and-the-hidden-hostility.webp)

---

*We think of hospitality as a generous, inclusive act. But within every act of hospitality there are concealed acts of exclusion, every guest list excludes those not on it, every open door has a bouncer. **Rachael Newberry** applies Derrida’s theory on the hostility of hospitality to the modern political issue of refugees, examining the UK’s Homes for Ukraine scheme, widely praised as an extraordinary act of generosity, but built on vetting, time limits, and an unspoken assumption that Ukrainians are the “right” kind of refugee. True unconditional hospitality may be impossible, but it reminds us that every system of welcome excludes someone, and without it as a standard, all welcome becomes indistinguishable from administration.*

Liberal democracies hypocritically commit to compassion toward refugees while at the same time constructing ever more elaborate systems of surveillance, categorization, and exclusion. The UK has, within the last five years, introduced a multitude of systems to deter people from seeking refuge from war, economic instability and unstable climatic environments.  Thus, as Jacques Derrida argues, hospitality, in the sense of welcome of the other, is structurally dependent upon the hostility it claims to oppose.

[According to Derrida](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1353464052000321056), all philosophical thought asserts that “there is no culture or social bond without a principle of hospitality.” However, this principle comes with certain conditions. Derrida’s neologism “hostipitality” asserts that hospitality has a “troubled and troubling origin […] a word which allows itself to be parasitized by its opposite, “hostility,” the undesirable guest which it harbors as the self-contradiction in its own body.”

The Homes for Ukraine scheme brings Derrida's paradox out of the realm of philosophy and into real lived experience.

Hospitality becomes conditional the moment we ask the stranger to identify themselves. A passport check may appear administratively neutral, but ethically it transforms welcome into interrogation. The question “what is your name?” is not simply a means of introduction, but a way of cataloguing and labelling those we deem strangers. “What is your name?” carries with it all sorts of additional enquiries—where are you from, why are you here, what right do you have to be here, when are you going back?

Derrida presents us with a difficult challenge, asking if it is ever possible to welcome the other unconditionally. This is an unnerving question, offering an impossible challenge to anyone who believes we owe a duty of care to those seeking refuge. Can a nation, a community, an individual extend hospitality to those in need without any expectation of reward or reciprocity? The conditions we set around welcome come with a set of expectations we may consider to be reasonable. We would not expect a guest to stay for longer than they were invited, for example. But would we, for instance, give up our bed for them? Allow them to help themselves to food from our kitchen?

These questions become more than academic when we examine the UK’s [Homes for Ukraine Sponsorship Scheme](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/689af330a6eb81a3f9b2e34f/Homes+for+Ukraine+Sponsorship+Scheme_.pdf), introduced in March 2022 following the Russian invasion of Ukraine. The scheme allows Ukrainian nationals and their families to reside in the UK for up to eighteen months if they have an approved sponsor. Sponsors provide accommodation with a minimum expectation of a six-month stay. The Homes for Ukraine scheme brings Derrida's paradox out of the realm of philosophy and into real lived experience. For perhaps the first time in recent British history, thousands of households were asked not merely to support refugees in principle but to welcome them into their homes. The scheme appears to embody an extraordinary act of hospitality. Yet it also exposes how conditional that hospitality remains.

Sponsors know who they are welcoming. Refugees arrive through a state-administered process of vetting, documentation and matching. They are granted temporary residence rather than an open-ended right to remain. Hospitality is therefore extended, but only through an elaborate framework of conditions. More revealingly still, the scheme has prompted little of the hostility directed towards other groups of asylum seekers. The figure of the Ukrainian refugee is often represented as culturally familiar, hardworking, European, and therefore worthy of being labelled a deserving guest. The scheme is still open to applicants and has already housed over 100,000 Ukrainian guests, with 37,500 more people with valid visas who may choose to travel to the UK. (Note the government website calls them guests, rather than asylum seekers or refugees, and I’ll come back to this.) The significance of the Ukraine scheme lies not merely in the conditions attached to it, but in the fact that these conditions are widely accepted as reasonable. Hospitality becomes easier when the refugee can be imagined as a future neighbour or friend.

This raises an uncomfortable question. Is the generosity displayed towards Ukrainians evidence of unconditional or pure hospitality, or does hospitality become easier when the stranger appears less strange? Dominant discourses frequently present Ukrainian refugees as culturally and socially familiar in ways that Syrian or Afghan refugees are not. And although there are [safe and legal humanitarian routes](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9630/) to the UK for non-European refugees, the Syrian Vulnerable Persons Resettlement Scheme (VPRS) has ended, and the UK Resettlement Scheme is both less well publicized and omits the word “home” from its title.

[Language matters](https://iai.tv/articles/derrida-and-the-trouble-with-metaphysics-auid-2089). Along with “home,” the word “guest” is used in the government literature about Ukrainian refugees. A guest is someone who is expected, welcomed and temporarily accommodated. Their presence is temporary and therefore non-threatening; they are expected, eventually, to leave. An asylum seeker arrives without invitation—their right to remain must be justified. A refugee raises difficult questions about permanence, belonging and obligation. Derrida calls them “the wrong kind of guests.” To rename a refugee a “guest” is therefore not a neutral linguistic choice. As [Andrew Bennett argues](https://gyansanchay.csjmu.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/An_Introduction_to_Literature__Criticism_and_Theory.pdf), “Language changes, and even creates the social and political world in which we live. Ideology in that sense *is* language.”

The government thus creates a vocabulary, an ideology through which hospitality can be imagined. Conversely, hostile language (swarms, parasites, a drain on society) shapes who we imagine as deserving of welcome. Such language does not merely describe refugees; it constructs them as threats to the social body. The stranger ceases to appear as a potential neighbor and instead becomes an object of management, suspicion or exclusion. They are too much, too many, too needy. Hospitality becomes impossible not because the stranger lacks humanity, but because they are imagined as making [excessive demands upon the host](https://iai.tv/articles/the-faces-of-faceless-people-auid-1795).

Yet hospitality is not simply a matter of language or ethics either. It is embodied. The stranger is never encountered as an abstract human being but as a body that carries visible markers of nationality, race, language, religion, and class. Whether someone is welcomed or feared often depends upon how easily they can be [imagined as belonging](https://iai.tv/articles/the-faces-of-faceless-people-auid-1795). Yet embodiment is not only physical. Refugees are also required to perform a particular social role. Gratitude, humility and deference become part of what makes someone legitimate as an authentic refugee. Acceptance depends not only upon legal status but upon the successful performance of what a refugee is supposed to be. As Marta Niccolai argues, “they have to perform the refugee, produce a speech that enacts a believable refugee, because their status is decided in that moment by those with the power and authority to do so.”

The contradiction revealed by the refugee is not simply a failure of immigration policy. It is a feature of liberal democracy itself. Liberal states ground their legitimacy in universal values of equality, dignity and human rights, yet they govern through borders, categories and exclusions. The refugee stands at the point where these commitments collide. They are simultaneously a human being in need and a stranger whose presence must be managed. Hospitality therefore remains caught between ethics and sovereignty, openness and control.

We might, then, ask whether unconditional hospitality matters at all. As states require borders, and hospitality functions through a set of conditions, why not accept that conditional hospitality is enough? Derrida’s answer is that without the ideal of unconditional hospitality, hospitality risks becoming indistinguishable from administration. A welcome extended only to those who satisfy the correct criteria becomes a process of selection. The ideal of unconditional hospitality therefore functions less as a practical desire than a yardstick through which *all* hospitality is measured. It reminds us that every system of welcome excludes someone, and that those exclusions should remain constantly open to scrutiny. Conditional hospitality may be necessary, but without the ideal of unconditional hospitality there is little reason to question whom we exclude, or why.

Derrida’s insight is not that hospitality fails because we are insufficiently generous. Rather, hospitality fails because it can never be pure. The moment we ask who someone is, where they come from, or how long they intend to stay, welcome becomes conditional. Yet this impossibility does not render hospitality meaningless. Instead, it is what gives hospitality its ethical force. The task is not to achieve unconditional hospitality but to remain attentive to the exclusions concealed within every act of welcome. The refugee reminds us that the stranger can never be fully welcomed without unsettling the boundaries through which we understand home, belonging and ourselves.
