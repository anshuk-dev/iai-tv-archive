# New political parties won't save democracy

*The two-party system is crumbling, but democracy isn't necessarily saved*

**By Marta Lorimer** | *13th August 2025* | *Read time: approx. 20 mins* | *3,973 words*

![New political parties won't save democracy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-two-party-system-isnt-the-problem-copy.webp)

---

*Across the world, we are witnessing the birth of new political parties, on both the left and right. With polarisation and populist governments gaining power across the world, some lay the blame at the feet of the two-party system. Yet political scientist [Marta Lorimer](https://profiles.cardiff.ac.uk/staff/lorimerm) argues this is mistaken thinking. The two-party system has never been the only game in town and the political system is not to blame; our politics is. Parties need to make an ideological argument, not pander to what they think voters want. And parties need to set the agenda, not take their lead from the narratives driven by the populist right.*

**Let’s start with a broad question. Why do you believe the two-party system has been threatened for the past couple of decades, seemingly in many places across the world? What has changed?**

I think there are things that have changed and things that have stayed the same. I think, first of all, that our understanding that the two-party system is a dominant system is wrong. Because it is very much influenced by a certain way of thinking about politics that is linked to places like the US or the UK. The truth is that the two-party system hasn’t necessarily been there in most places for a very long time. So perhaps one way to think about it is: why is it in crisis in the places where it exists, or where it has existed for a very long time?

We might want to think about this more broadly as well: why does it *seem* we are experiencing a crisis of the bipolar – rather than two-party—system? Why is it, for example, that we no longer seem to be seeing politics contested over this left-right axis? Why is it that we’re seeing different axes of competition appearing?

**Why do you believe the left-right axis is increasingly irrelevant for many? And what axis do you think is substituting it, if any? For instance, I think of** [**Jonathan Hopkin’s system/anti-system dichotomy**](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/anti-system-politics-9780190699765?cc=it&lang=en&)**.**

There are a variety of ways that we can cut the political system. Left-right has always been a very interesting one to me. Because I think the way that political scientists think of it, at least, is a very narrow conception of the left-right spectrum. If you ask a political scientist, particularly those who work with spatial modelling, they will tell you that the left-right cleavage is about material questions. So it is essentially about redistribution, where you have the right that is against redistribution, and the left that is in favor of redistribution.

That isn’t the way that the left-right spectrum has always been thought about, though. We’ve seen different ways of thinking about what the left-right spectrum is about. As the Italian philosopher [Norberto Bobbio, who is probably one of the best-known thinkers on the subject, says, Left and Right are essentially about equality and inequality](https://mronline.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Bobbio-Norberto-Left-and-Right-Significance-Political-Distinction.pdf). And you can have different ideas sitting in there that structure your axes of political competition. What’s interesting about thinking of the left and right in this broader sense is that it enables you to bring other lines of conflict under it.

\_\_\_

These parties’ core claim is that they represent the nation; that they represent the people. And this idea of “the people” is a monist understanding. You cannot have divisions within “the people.”

\_\_\_

We might want to think of what we even take left and right to mean, and whether what we are observing is actually a general decline in left and right politics, or if we are seeing new axes of competition appearing next to it: if what we’re observing is a new form of competition that has nothing to do with left and right and has everything to do with, say, national and transnational politics—or liberalism and authoritarianism.

Back to your original question. I think in that way, [left and right was always a simplification of sorts](http://iai.tv/articles/the-age-of-left-right-politics-is-over-auid-2993). It was always a useful spectrum for bringing some order to messy politics. My suspicion is that it still matters, even if we consider it in the very narrow sense of conflict over redistribution. What we’re certainly seeing is some people trying to reject this political cleavage and saying it doesn’t matter any longer. And one of the reasons they try to do that is that they suggest that they appeal beyond political divisions. And that is slightly more suspicious to me. Because anyone claiming they represent absolutely everyone isn’t really being honest about their politics.

**That’s very interesting. In those places where there is a two-party system and the left-right spectrum is being challenged, I wonder why it is being challenged particularly by the so-called right of the spectrum?**

[I have written about this](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315144993-7/ni-droite-ni-gauche-français-marta-lorimer?context=ubx&refId=dceaf5ee-12b1-47dc-92a9-df0d82afa928)! My claim is that one of the reasons that far-right parties in particular object to the left-right cleavage is that it isn’t necessarily compatible with the kind of politics that they push. It doesn’t speak to their priorities. So if we think—let’s say, left and right is about material distribution—this is something that is a very secondary issue to the radical right. So they won’t really want to conceive of politics or speak of politics in that way.

The other reason that they tend to reject it is, again, that the concept of left and right speaks of a division within society. And specifically to a legitimate division within the nation. Now, these specific parties’ core claim is that they represent the nation, or nationals; that they represent the people. And this idea of “the people” is a monist understanding. You cannot have divisions within “the people.” Which is why they reject a concept that speaks to the fact that not only does division exist, but it’s also a good thing, that it’s very reasonable to have different ideas of what politics should be doing. The radical right doesn’t really go with that idea. They’ll use narratives of common sense to make claims that, basically, we all know what is good, and everyone agrees on it. If you don’t, it’s because you’re a traitor. You’re not a [real patriot](http://iai.tv/articles/supremacy-liberty-and-the-right-auid-2169). And so that’s why they reject this left-right distinction, I think.

**I was listening to** [**a recent interview with Jean-Luc Mélenchon**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6WFTiA_H67g&t=3076s)**. He said his party was trying to recover the use of the French national flag, because for the people who wielded it, it represented “the people” as opposed to the elite. And he wanted to believe that anyone could fall under this category. So it wasn’t so much nationalistic as elite vs. anti-elite.**

I understand the left’s attempts to reclaim [patriotism and nationalism for their own](http://iai.tv/articles/identity-politics-is-over-heres-what-will-replace-it-auid-3179). I think we’ve seen Labour doing the same thing, putting front and centre the idea of Britain, and the British flag. I suppose that’s what Mélenchon is doing—saying, look, we’re not anti-national. We just have a different understanding of what national is. And to me, that’s not necessarily a wild claim. I think you can make the claim that a flag does not have to represent what the far right says it represents. What I don’t know is the intention of the left in using it. Is it because they are trying in fact to win back right-wing voters? And if that is the case, are we really picking the best way to do it, and is that really the constituency you want to appeal to?

**Why do you think voters have recently been so receptive to the right-wing logic you’ve presented? Has there been a shift? And what has provoked it? Why are people so susceptible to this broad idea of “the people” and other such values being invoked by the right?**

There’s a bunch of possible explanations. I quite like [Daphne Halikiopoulou and Tim Vlandas’ very good report from a couple of years ago](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4122538), where they distinguish various reasons why far-right parties have become more successful with time. One of them has to do with demand, with shifting demands from the electorate. So there are more people who are concerned about migration or, for example, feel that their economic condition has deteriorated, and so on and so forth, and who then turn to far-right parties because they feel they are offering solutions to these problems.

Then you have the supply-side, which is the far-right parties themselves. If you look at far-right parties circa 20-30 years ago, most of them were very disorganized, saying things that you quite simply couldn’t say under any reasonable understanding of politics. That has changed. These parties have become more media-savvy; they have become, in large part, more organized in terms of their politics. They have found new ways of framing their agenda that are more broadly appealing.

And then we really have to think about context as well. Because again, one of the things that has really changed doesn’t have to do with the far right themselves, but with the actors in the mainstream. Really, one of the big [enablers of the far right](http://iai.tv/video/the-rise-of-the-new-right) recently has been mainstream parties essentially copying their ideas, saying their ideas are right, but we can deliver them better. Instead of saying: their ideas are terrible, their analysis is wrong, we can respond to your grievances, far right solutions will not work. So in a way, there’s been a tendency from the mainstream to not just start addressing the issues of the far right, but addressing them [on the terms of the far right](http://iai.tv/articles/the-politics-of-fear-is-in-full-effect-auid-3140).

Say, if you want to talk about migration. There are different ways that you can tackle migration—you can talk about it as something important to discuss in society. You don’t have to speak of it in terms of “we’re going to send back the boats”. That’s really where a lot of mainstream parties are going wrong—they’re not engaging with voters’ issues, they’re just copying radical right solutions.

\_\_\_

Some parties don’t know what they stand for. We’ve convinced ourselves that ideology is dead, that it doesn’t really matter, that as a party, you shouldn’t really stand for anything specific, but you should just stand for what the people care about.

\_\_\_

**So why do you think mainstream parties have lost some of their agility and ability to respond, and ceded this space to the right?**

I think that the problem is that when you’re a party and you’re faced with a new problem, you don’t necessarily know what to do. And you’re going to have different people having different ideas. And this is initially exactly what happened with the radical right. So initially, not just parties, but also the media more broadly, had to decide what to do with the far right. Do you engage with their ideas and take them as serious ideas you need to talk about? Do you just not talk about it and pretend they never existed? Do you engage with the ideas, and do you engage to critique them?

And I think there’s no good answer to this. Because you can’t keep ignoring them forever. But presumably copying them is also not a very good strategy. Because then the point again becomes: why would you be better than them? So there’s a broader point of relevance going on here.

So I don’t really know why it is that political parties made some pretty terrible decisions here. I think some of these were probably well-meaning, in terms of saying, look, if public opinion tells us this is important, then we have to respond to public opinion. Others were probably more strategic and less well-meaning, saying, well I think that public opinion is completely wrong, but if I want to win an election, then I actually have to engage with these claims, because otherwise the far right is going to win, so I’m going to steal their ideas and pass them as my own.

And I think there’s perhaps a broader issue here, which seems to be that some parties don’t seem to know any longer what they stand for. That’s perhaps a broader issue where we’ve convinced ourselves that ideology is dead, that it doesn’t really matter, that as a party, you shouldn’t really stand for anything specific, but you should just stand for what the people care about. When in fact we know it’s not that they don’t know what they care about, it’s that they do respond to different stimuli of what it is that matters. And the objective of a political party is, perhaps, to try to make sense of that and say we have this overall goal that we think is actually going to help you achieve that thing you want to achieve.

**Would it be correct to say that many mainstream parties have become quite technocratic and that this has weakened their position?**

I think technocratic is one way of putting it. I don’t even know if technocratic is the right answer. I think there was a moment in politics where everyone convinced themselves that voters all want the same things. And political competition is about who is perceived as best placed to achieve those things that we all agree are wonderful and desirable. The problem with that way of thinking is that, of course, we don’t all want the same things. Voters actually want different things, and even when we do agree, even assuming we could agree on the things we want, we might have wildly different ideas about how it is that we get to them. [And the responsibility, to some extent, of a political party, of a political leader, is to work that bit out](http://iai.tv/articles/why-economics-needs-philosophy-auid-2757).

**Why do you think the left, as traditionally conceived, has been slower in picking up on this need or lack in the mainstream parties?**

I don’t know if the left has been slower; I just think this is a bigger problem for the left, because left-wing politics has always been about presenting an alternative to the status quo. Conservatives have it easy in a way, because conservatives will usually tell you: we want to keep things steady, we value tradition. We also have conservatives who went pretty revolutionary, like Margaret Thatcher, in terms of wanting some radical change. But, in a way, conservatives don’t really have this necessity to put forward an alternative vision of society or some kind of utopia, an idea of what you want society to look like. And that’s why I think left-wing parties struggle a little bit more with achieving this once they’ve lost the sense of an alternative vision of politics. That might be why they’ve struggled with who it is that they are.

**Turning this into a more concrete discussion, I wanted to compare different political systems in the West. Europe, which you’ve studied a lot; the UK, different from Europe largely because Brexit has been achieved and Europe plays a different role; and the US, which has a much stronger two-party system, but which still feels like it’s being threatened, perhaps in a different way. I was wondering if you could speak across these three contexts: where the party political cleavages are in each, and how they work within that particular system. Or whether you think they’re quite similar.**

I think they’re probably quite similar in the sense that all of these countries have a lot of different cleavages. Those cleavages aren’t necessarily represented within their party systems. And that has probably more to do with the challenges to establishing a new party and various quirks of electoral systems. UK politics isn’t running on a strictly speaking left-right axis. The UK party system isn’t bipartisan any longer—there are several different parties. But the representation is still by a small number of political parties. The main parties have been receiving an increasingly lower share of the vote. And that’s what is interesting—not so much the number of MPs that anyone has, but how many votes are going to just two parties.

France has experienced something very similar. Even in France, it used to be the case that the two main centrist parties received most of the votes. And again, that’s no longer the case. Now you have something between a three-party and four-party system. Then you have places like Italy, that never really had a two-party system. Italy basically had this system of polarized pluralism. So you had one centre party, and then two extremist parties that would never have a right to rule. Then it went through an era of, broadly speaking, two-party politics, not really with two political parties, but two coalitions, one on the left, one on the right. And now we have a very skewed party system where you have a lot of parties on the right, and other parties sort of on the left, and the Five Star Movement, where no one knows what it is.

And then you have places like the Netherlands, where there are just more parties than anyone can keep track of, where a government will usually involve at least four parties, three of which don’t want to talk to each other. So, it’s quite a complex picture—it’s just that in these places you can see it very visibly because of the electoral system not hiding the divisions within it.

It’s worth keeping in mind that divisions of various types exist in many places. In some places, they are more visible than others; in some elections, they are more visible than others. And which divisions we see will partially depend on the electoral institutions, and will partially depend on what the parties decide to prioritize. Because if you have an election where all you end up talking about is migration, then you are very likely to have far right parties do well. If you end up in an election where all that is being discussed is the cost-of-living crisis, then maybe the far right isn’t really going to do quite as well.

**As you mentioned at the beginning, the obsession with the two-party system is probably an Americanization. American politics is so easy to follow in its two-party way. As the US is such a benchmark for politics around the world, do you think it has exported the two-party system? Or do you think it’s under threat there as well, in a comparable way to Europe?**

It seems not to be under threat in a comparable way to Europe, mainly because I don’t think that anyone has been able to—and I’m not an expert in US politics—successfully create a new party, or anything that has been able to actually challenge the two-party system. What we’ve seen instead with radical right politics is that these haven’t been taken up by a new party; these have been taken up by the Republicans. So it’s almost as if they’ve been able to integrate these new tendencies without the need for a new party to emerge.

I also wonder how much this vision of the two-party system is a very Anglo-Saxon way of thinking about it. The two-party adversarial system where the two parties not only go against each other, but also don’t work with each other. It’s not the rule in other places. And I’m not sure it’s coming under attack in the US.

I think in Britain, it’s interesting. I don’t think the two-party system is necessarily there in the minds of the voters; I think it just so happens that because of the electoral system, it seems to be very difficult to move away from the two-party system. We might end up with the two-party system again, but it’s just not the same parties that were there at the beginning.

**Do you think there’s something to salvage about the two-party idea? Do you think it contributes at all to a healthy political system?**

I quite like the linearity of being able to say: look, there are two parties, Party A stands for this, Party B stands for that, fight it out. There’s something quite simple and straightforward about thinking about politics in that way. And it works to the extent that those parties are able to encapsulate a variety of ideas.

Where I think the model faces a bit more of a challenge is that it does mean that these parties really have to represent a lot of ideas, and some of those ideas just don’t stick together. And so that can be very difficult to navigate. And if you feel that your ideas within that party are always the minority, maybe you’ll stop feeling that politics really represents you. So there might be something to be said for having a ton of parties, each one representing a small segment of the electorate and having to work with the others to work out: okay, what is it we can actually agree on?

Ultimately, I don’t necessarily have a strong preference. I think I have more sympathy for a system where there are more parties representing genuinely different ideas, with those different parties having to somehow work together. But I can see there’s something quite beautiful in the simple way of thinking of political competition as us vs. them. But with the general understanding that we all get a chance at power at some point.

\_\_\_

Sometimes the problem is political, and you need to find political solutions for a political problem.

\_\_\_

**I like that**—**it struck me from your discussion that you’re right that our sense of the domination of the two-party system is blown up out of proportion. It’s not actually that frequent across the world. So why do you think there’s this feeling of a crisis in the system? Does it come from something else, from the establishment being threatened**—**what do you think might be the cause of this alarm?**

I think it’s just in places that are used to this kind of system. You start thinking of it as part of what you are, and part of what makes your politics function. So I think inherent to fears around the Westminster model facing a crisis is the idea that the UK is a stable democracy, it’s been a stable democracy for a long time, and one of the reasons is that if you have a government, the government has a majority, and it can push through the measures that it wants.

So that’s probably why you feel a sense of crisis, because it’s so entrenched in the way that you do politics, that you assume it’s the recipe for your success, without necessarily realising that there are countries that have never functioned that way and are still broadly conceived of as functional democracies. Germany is probably the best example. Or even the Netherlands. These are systems that were never really two-party systems, that have never worked in this adversarial form of politics the UK works in—where, instead, you do post-electoral coalitions, where you decide what’s to be done then. And that always worked.

It doesn’t necessarily seem to be working anymore. So, I think—I’m going to go on a limb here—the problem is not really the system itself. The party system is not really the problem. The problem, I think, is something bigger, about what politics even means anymore. And what the expectations around politics can actually do and achieve. So if you have a political system—if you have two parties or twenty parties it doesn’t matter—if no one feels that they can do anything, if no one does seem to be doing anything, then you are going to end up with an electorate that feels disillusioned because it doesn’t seem that anything is working for them, or that anything can be achieved. The problem isn’t really the party system; the problem is what the parties are doing in the first place.

**You hit it on the head. The fact that, for instance, people are voting less in the UK for the main party blocs is because people are voting less in general. So there’s a deeper malaise in democracy.**

Yes—the problem isn’t the party system. Italian politics has this thing it does a lot: you try to find institutional fixes for a political problem. Sometimes the problem is political, and you need to find political solutions for a political problem.
