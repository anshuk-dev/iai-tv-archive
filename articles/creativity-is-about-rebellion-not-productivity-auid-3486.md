# Creativity is about rebellion, not productivity

*Imagination against monetization*

**By Oli Mould** | *4th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,375 words*

![Creativity is about rebellion, not productivity](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/creativity.webp)

---

*Creativity is everywhere: demanded by employers, schools, politicians, and now algorithms. But when it is framed as a skill to be optimized and monetized, it risks losing the radical power that once made it transformative. **Oli Mould**, author of* Against Creativity*, argues that real creativity is not about constant innovation or productivity, but about imagination, refusal, and collective change, and that reclaiming it may be one of the most urgent political tasks of our time.*

Being “creative” is now essential. That is, if you view it through the lens that has been created (and curated) for us by the political and economic orthodoxy of the last half a century. Specifically, I like to draw that line in 1997 with the landslide election of Tony Blair as the UK prime minister, in that it created the now internationally-recognized remit of the “creative industries” as an economic and political entity—but the lens was being crafted well before then by the Thatcherites, let’s be honest.

This lens, that is now all-pervasive, refracts creativity—a deep human trait that has pushed through the arc of history and civilization—through capitalist relations, narrating it as an important “skill” that will advance your career, the economy, the country. Creativity, through this lens, becomes a personal and social necessity. It is a character trait that can be asked for at a job interview, a word that can be dropped into a policy document to secure government funding, a narrative to mask ongoing gentrification, or a stick to beat underperforming public institutions with. Viewed this way, [creativity is an obligation](https://iai.tv/articles/the-crisis-of-creativity-auid-2239): if you aren’t creative, then you’re left behind.

But such a view of creativity is not only reductive, it’s damaging. For much of human history, creativity was imagined as the spark of change; it was [Archimedes in his bathtub](https://iai.tv/articles/the-power-of-the-unreal-in-scientific-discovery-auid-2030), Al-Khwarizmi inventing algebra, [Shakespeare](https://iai.tv/articles/shakespeare-vs-wittgenstein-the-fight-for-meaning-auid-3066) writing Henry VI, or even Rosa Parks refusing to sit at the back of the bus. It was the spark that allowed us to leap forward out of the status quo, to challenge conventional orthodoxies in science, literature, politics, and art. Creativity was what artists, misfits, activists, and rebels used to prise open the cracks of the dominant and oppressive operating system of society (be that religious, economic, or political) and slip through into something freer.

\_\_\_

The more creativity becomes whatever can be captured, measured, or monetized, the more it loses the capacity to unsettle power.

\_\_\_

But today, the lens that has been created by late capitalism that refracts creativity through purely economic orthodoxies means that “creativity” hasn’t just lost its oppositional edge, it’s been cannibalized by the status quo itself. From primary schools to corporate boardrooms, from the gig economy to the rhetoric of political campaigns, creativity has been conscripted as the soft power of contemporary capitalism. It is no longer a mode of refusal. It is a metric of productivity.

Students must “be creative” to stand out in assessment regimes designed to standardize them. Workers are told to innovate, always innovate, whether they are designing user interfaces or drafting emails or producing content for platforms that will absorb their labor and reward them with algorithmic indifference. Even our leisure time is colonized by creative self-improvement: side hustles, personal brands, craft practices that must be shareable, marketable, optimisable. What once felt like liberation now feels like homework.

This is all the more paradoxical given that we live in a moment routinely celebrated as an unprecedented flowering of human creativity. The now common tools of production—digital editing suites, social platforms, and [unethical AI systems](https://iai.tv/articles/dont-blame-ai-for-its-mistakes-auid-3421)—are unimaginably powerful and astonishingly accessible. At a technical level, many of us have, in our pockets, devices that would have looked like dark sorcery to any previous generation of artists.

But the ubiquity of these creative tools has not guaranteed the freedom to use them meaningfully. Instead, they yoke us to the infrastructural logic of late capitalism that demands of us compulsory originality. To compete in hyper-precarious and mediatized labor markets (as well as the hyper-mediated public and urban spaces in the physical and digital worlds), we must present ourselves as constantly inventive and innovative. Creative labor becomes indistinguishable from self-surveillance. And the horizon of what counts as “creative” is increasingly dictated by politicians, platforms, metrics, and markets. The more creativity becomes whatever can be captured, measured, or monetized, the more it loses the capacity to unsettle power.

The rapid (and yes, unethical) mainstreaming of generative AI has intensified this paradox. Early debates focused on whether machines can be creative; a more urgent question is what happens and what is lost when creativity is automated.

When ChatGPT drafts your emails, Sora gives us visual propaganda, or a fine-tuned model composes music in the style of your favorite artist, creativity becomes less a spark of human genius and more about how quickly we can rehash existing ideas to be different enough to have an edge in the increasingly saturated marketplace of more and more generic and insipid “content.”

Humans are being relegated to supervisors of this incredibly bland and economistic version of creativity: responsible for prompting, selecting, refining. This means that companies now exhort workers to “use AI creatively”—which actually just means using it efficiently. Education systems urge students to develop AI literacy so they can be competitive rather than thoughtful. Creativity becomes something like fitness: a regime to be maintained, a discipline that keeps you employable.

\_\_\_

Creativity once helped us imagine otherwise. Now it often helps us tolerate the intolerable.

\_\_\_

And significantly, these systems don’t do what creativity did: challenge existing power structures. AI creativity is built atop training data that encodes the norms and patterns of the world as it exists. It can recombine them, stylize them, exaggerate them, but [it struggles to imagine beyond them](https://iai.tv/articles/ais-growing-threat-to-challenging-ideas-auid-3089). In that sense, it automates “inspiration” as an ingredient of conformity to capitalist dogma.

Even within our polarized politics, creativity has been rebranded as a spectacle of disruption. Populist leaders—often harvested from entertainment industries (from Trump to Zelenskyy)—position themselves as creative rule-breakers: they “think differently,” “break the mold” and “disrupt the elites” (despite often being elites themselves). What was once the language of radical counterculture has become a toolkit for populist demagogues looking to ossify the hierarchies and unequal societies that spawned countercultural revolutions in the first place. Their creativity is performative, reactionary and largely destructive: it is a political theatre of spontaneity that thrives on constant demonization of marginality, the constant manufacture of hate, and a politics of resentment.

\_\_\_

The task is not to be more creative. It is to reclaim the right not to be creative on demand.

\_\_\_

The tragedy is not that creativity has been co-opted by capitalism, however all-conquering that co-option has been. The tragedy is that we rarely notice it anymore. We have so thoroughly internalized the injunction to be creative that it appears natural, obvious, and totally boring. It has totally lost any sense of social and historical progress. [Climate breakdown](https://iai.tv/video/climate-culture-and-creativity-lola-young)? Get creative with green entrepreneurialism. Social inequality? Get creative with personal branding. Institutional decay? Get creative with online communities. At every turn, we are encouraged to innovate around problems rather than transform the systems that produce them. Creativity once helped us imagine otherwise. Now it often helps us tolerate the intolerable.

If creativity is to regain any critical force, we need to detach it from the imperatives of capitalist productivity. That means reimagining creativity not as novelty but as refusal: the refusal to produce when production is demanded; the refusal to innovate when innovation only accelerates the status quo; the refusal to be “original” on someone else’s terms. As I have argued for over a decade now, creativity should not be a performance but a political orientation. Not a marketable skill but a collective practice of resistance. Not an obligation but an opening.

Paradoxically, the most creative acts today may be the least visible ones: the formation of solidarities, [the building of commons](https://iai.tv/articles/reimagining-creativity-auid-2305), the slow reorganizing of everyday life around values that resist extractive logics. These require a [radical imagination](https://iai.tv/articles/the-crisis-of-creativity-auid-2239), but of a kind that cannot be captured by metrics or optimized by algorithms. The task is not to be more creative. It is to reclaim the right not to be creative on demand. Only then can creativity regain its capacity to disrupt, to unsettle, to liberate, and to help us make lives that are not merely innovative, but genuinely different.
