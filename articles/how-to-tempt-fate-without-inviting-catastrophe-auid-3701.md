# How to tempt fate without inviting catastrophe

*What Alex Honnold and Harry Kane teach us about risk*

**By Duncan Pritchard** | *12th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,933 words*

![How to tempt fate without inviting catastrophe](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/how-to-tempt-fate-without-inviting-catastrophe.webp)

*Probability is the wrong way to think about risk. When Alex Honnold free solo climbed El Capitan with no safety equipment, that was incredibly risky, despite Honnold’s extreme skill and experience making it unlikely he’d fall. Philosopher **Duncan Pritchard**, author of the recent book* Tempting Fate*, argues that we should instead measure risk in terms of fragility – how easily a catastrophe could occur. We have to learn to make high risk decisions in our lives, as a life of caution is often one of regret. Understanding risk allows us to do this without tempting fate.*

My mother, who passed away recently, had a lot of sayings, one of which was that “you should never tempt fate.” What she meant by this could be summed up by one of her other sayings: “hubris begets nemesis.” If you are arrogant and overly confident in your plans, then rest assured that this will be noticed by the Gods and you will incur their wrath. That all seems right to me.

There is, however, another meaning of tempting fate that is especially relevant to taking risks. When it comes to risk-taking, you can’t be assured that everything will work out. Things can go pear-shaped. That’s the sense in which taking risks can involve tempting fate, as one is opening oneself up to¾inviting, you might say¾catastrophe. And yet it can’t be the case that we should never take risks, as it is difficult to flourish if one is always taking the cautious path. How then do we tempt fate without hubris, without inviting catastrophe?

If you look to the standard theory of risk for an answer to this question, then I think you will be disappointed. When we assess for the level of risk, we have in mind a specific unwanted risk event that we hope to avoid. According to the probability theory of risk, the level of risk is determined by how likely the risk event is. If this probability is high, then the risk is high, while if the probability is low, then the risk is low.

For lots of cases, this proposal generates the right result, which explains why it’s so widely held. Juggling dynamite is high risk because the probability of the risk event of blowing yourself to pieces is very high. Do this 100 times and in most, if not all, of these scenarios you are no more. In contrast, juggling apples is low risk because the probability that this will get you blown up is negligible. Do it as many times as you like, and you’ll be fine. The problem is that this cannot be the full story about risk.

In 2017, Alex Honnold made the first free solo climb of El Capitan, a 3000-foot slab of rock in Yosemite National Park. This is an incredible achievement. As the name suggests, free solo climbing means no safety equipment whatsoever. It is just you and the mountain. It takes immense levels of skill and years of preparation to undertake a complex free solo climb of this magnitude. But even with all this expertise and planning, the fact remains that it is a monumentally risky undertaking. Honnold could have died at any moment on that mountain. The slightest error, and he would be falling to his death.

Notice what the probability theory of risk would say about this case. If what Honnold is doing is high-risk, then that means that it was likely that he was going to die on that mountain. Sure, he succeeded on this occasion, but if we run this scenario 100 times, then he’ll die on most of these iterations. But that doesn’t sound right. Remember that Honnold is an expert who has meticulously prepared for this climb. It’s not at all likely that he’s going to die on this mountain. Indeed, he makes free solo climbs all the time, and I’m pleased to report that he’s still with us. Besides, if we really thought that it was likely that he was going to die on that mountain, then shouldn’t we have stopped him? The alternative, however, would be to say that what Honnold is doing is actually low risk. But obviously that can’t be right. Free solo climbing is about as risky as things can be. The standard view of risk is in trouble.

The solution is to recognize that risk is not primarily about the probability of the risk event, but about its *fragility*. Fragility is concerned with how easily something could occur. If something could easily occur, then this is because very little needs to change for it to happen. The idea is that if the risk event could easily occur, then we are facing high levels of risk, whereas if it couldn’t easily occur, then we are facing low levels of risk.

It’s easy to overlook the fragility of a risk event because it tends to go hand-in-hand with its high probability. If you juggle dynamite, then the risk event of being blown up is fragile, as sticks of dynamite that are thrown around can easily explode. But of course it’s also true, as we noted above, that if you juggle dynamite, then there’s a high probability of the risk event of being blown up. Crucially, however, although [fragility often coincides with probability](https://iai.tv/video/the-science-of-uncertainty-with-tim-palmer) in this way, there are important cases where they come apart.

Honnold’s free solo ascent of El Capitan is an excellent illustration. What makes this ascent inherently risky is the fragility of the risk event. Since Honnold has no safety equipment on that mountain, he could easily fall to his death at any moment. Just the slightest thing goes wrong, and he’s a goner. Crucially, however, this fragility doesn’t translate into it being likely that he will die on this mountain. On the contrary, his incredible climbing skills and his planning for this feat mean that it isn’t likely at all. The climb is high risk because the risk event of him dying is fragile, not because it is likely.

The idea isn’t that the probabilities don’t matter. For one thing, as we’ve just noted, probability is usually a good guide to fragility. But the more important point is that while there is nothing that Honnold can do to prevent this climb from being high-risk, there is a lot that he can do to lower the likelihood of the risk event. In fact, that’s just what Honnold’s skill and preparation are doing. It isn’t making the climb low-risk, as nothing could do that—free solo climbing is in its nature a high-risk activity—but it can lower what I call the *depth* of the risk, which is the likelihood that this climb will lead to disaster. This is what sets Honnold apart from a rank amateur who attempts the same climb. While the level of risk for both of them is high, the depth of risk is low for Honnold, in contrast to the amateur. Take away Honnold’s superlative expertise, and you have someone who is going to die on this mountain at every attempt.

Once you recognize how fragility can come apart from probability, you will see examples everywhere. Indeed, it’s a characteristic feature of most sports that they exhibit this distinction. It’s the FIFA World Cup final, and it’s gone to penalties. If you can suspend your disbelief for a moment, imagine that England are not only one of the teams in contention but that they are currently winning the penalty shootout. Harry Kane steps up to take his penalty, knowing that if he succeeds, then England have won the World Cup. Knowing what’s at stake, he aims for the top left corner, a tricky shot to pull off. He shoots, he scores, and England is triumphant. Football is finally “coming home.”

Clearly, the risks involved here are enormous. The slightest thing could have led to failure. Hitting the ball a bit too hard so that it goes over the bar, a small slip on the run-up to the ball, a marginal lack of connection on the boot as it strikes the ball that sends it wide, and so on. The risk event of him failing to score is fragile. But this is not reflected in the probabilities. Kane is a consummate professional when it comes to scoring penalties, especially under intense pressure. There’s no one else in the world you would want to be depending on right now to score this goal. In short, the probabilities are clearly in favor of him making this goal. The risk is due to the fragility of the risk event, not its likelihood.

Here’s how this all relates to our issue about tempting fate. What we’ve seen is that there can be certain kinds of activities that are in their nature high-risk. There’s simply no getting around the fact that free solo climbing is high risk. Similarly, there’s massive risk in play in a World Cup final penalty shootout. No amount of skill and preparation could change that. But what skill and preparation can do is lower the depth of the risk. The crux is that when it comes to these inherently risky activities, it’s important that we nonetheless take the risks skillfully to minimize the depth of risk. That’s what Honnold does on the mountain and Kane does on the football pitch.

This is relevant to tempting fate because if we are to flourish in our lives, then there will be all sorts of risks that we will need to take. Let me describe an example that’s close to home. About a decade ago, my wife and I took a momentous risk in deciding to leave a comfortable life in Edinburgh behind and move our family to Southern California. Obviously, this wasn’t a choice made on a whim. In fact, we agonized over it and spent a lot of time researching the pros and cons of our decision. No matter how much preparation one puts into these life-changing decisions, however, the fact remains that they are inherently risky. There are [so many variables in play](https://iai.tv/articles/lifes-secret-weapon-against-the-uncertainty-of-the-world-auid-3558) that there is no way of knowing in advance that things will work out. And if they don’t work out, then there is usually no way of returning things to how they were before—you are effectively buying a one-way ticket. But avoiding these life-changing risks is itself risky—the life of caution is often also a life of regret at the interesting paths not taken.

Our reflections on Honnold’s risk-taking shine a light on how we should think about these life-changing risks. Just as free solo climbing is inherently risky, so decisions of this kind are inherently risky too. While there’s nothing one can do about the [inherent fragility of the risk event](https://iai.tv/video/how-do-you-solve-a-problem-like-uncertainty) in these cases — which means that the level of risk is inevitably high — one can nonetheless lower the depth of risk by making one’s decision in a skillful way. That’s what prevents the risk from being rash and irrational.

That’s how you tempt fate without inviting catastrophe. It is by skillfully making these life decisions that one avoids the hubris that beckons one’s downfall. Of course, catastrophe might await you anyway, just as Honnold could have died on El Capitan or Kane could have missed that penalty. When you take risks of this kind, then [potential disaster is always just around the corner](https://iai.tv/articles/it-is-time-for-the-world-to-embrace-uncertainty-auid-2986). We can’t always eliminate this fragility, but that doesn’t mean that we should throw caution to the wind. Like Honnold on that mountain, even in embracing these risks, we should nonetheless trace our path through life with care.
