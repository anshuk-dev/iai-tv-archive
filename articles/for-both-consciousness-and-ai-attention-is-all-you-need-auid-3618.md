# For both consciousness and AI, attention is all you need

*Subjectivity's secret ingredient*

**By Michael Graziano** | *9th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,459 words*

![For both consciousness and AI, attention is all you need](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/for-both-consciousness-and-AI-attention-is-all-you-need2.webp)

---

*When the bombshell paper “Attention Is All You Need” appeared in 2017, it began today’s race for AI dominance. But what’s so special about attention? Princeton neuroscientist, composer and novelist **Michael Graziano** argues that attention is the most fundamental process in our brains. It builds our internal picture of reality, rations energy in our most energy-hungry organ, and is closely connected to consciousness. Indeed, he claims, subjective experience arises when the brain attends to its own attention in order to control it, like an artist painting a picture of themselves painting pictures.*

The most energy-expensive organ in the body is the brain. The human brain, for example, coming in at about 2% of the body’s mass, uses 20% of the body’s energy. Neural tissue is so costly that evolution does not generally favor large brains. Otherwise, the entire animal kingdom would have evolved giant ones, instead of just a few species that have found a way to exploit that trait.

To pack as much capacity in as small a mass as possible, the brain has evolved a specific kind of efficiency. At any one time, it focuses on a limited set of items and processes them deeply. As you look around the room, moment by moment, your visual system focuses on less than one in a million details. That winnowing is called attention, and without it, you would need a brain the size of a house to achieve the same level of intelligence. In a sense, attention is merely a data-handling trick—but that trick is the most fundamental process in the nervous system. It [makes us what we are](https://iai.tv/articles/the-death-and-rebirth-of-attention-auid-2973). Attention turns out to be [the crucial ingredient](https://iai.tv/articles/simone-weil-the-attention-economy-and-the-annihilation-of-autonomy-auid-3532) in much of psychology and neuroscience, in [consciousness itself](https://doi.org/10.1080/02643294.2020.1761782), and now even in artificial intelligence (AI). Its importance spans science, philosophy, and technology.

\_\_\_

Attention is not just about neurons, or about the brain enhancing internal representations. Attention is a gateway to subjective experience itself.

\_\_\_

William James, one of the founders of psychology, [famously said in 1898](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin11.htm), “Everyone knows what attention is. It is the taking possession of the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seems several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.” But if there’s one thing everyone knows in psychology, it’s that nothing is harder to pin down than the thing that “everyone knows.” Attention has been defined in at least half a dozen ways since James. Overt attention is where you’re looking. [Covert attention](https://doi.org/10.1080/00335558008248231) is when your mind is focused on something off your center gaze. Spatial attention is when you deeply process a particular location, like a spotlight. [Feature attention](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2006.04.001) is when that spotlight is broadened over a larger scene, but attuned to a single type of object, such as when you look for the guy in the red hat amid a sea of people.

Attention is mainly studied in the visual domain, but the same principles apply to hearing, touch, emotion, thought, and memory. Your attention is constantly moving between domains. One moment, your mind is focused on the apple in your hand, then it shifts to the sounds in the yard behind you, then to the memory of what you ate for breakfast, then to a sequence of thoughts. In each case, a representation in the brain, a pattern of activity among connected neurons, has been enhanced, allowing the information it represents to be more deeply processed. At the same time, resources are withdrawn from a million alternatives. The trick for attention is controlling it effectively—which means partly allowing bright or loud or unexpected things to seize attention, while at the same time, partly guiding attention according to internally-driven needs.

Transcending these moment-by-moment details, [attention also has a special relationship to consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/the-structure-of-consciousness). You may have heard of the famous invisible gorilla experiment, [published by Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons in 1999](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059). In that test, you’re shown a video of a basketball game and told to count how many times the ball changes hands. Your attention is so focused on the ball, and so withdrawn from every other aspect of the video, that you fail to notice the man in the gorilla suit weaving in and out of the game. The phenomenon is called [inattentional blindness](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386), and it’s a beautiful illustration that attention is not just about neurons, or about the brain enhancing internal representations. Attention is a gateway to subjective experience itself.

Pinning down that gateway relationship is tricky, but at least one modern theory provides an explanation. The brain builds neural representations of things—a touch on the hand, the sight of an apple, the emotion of joy. Then, applying attention, it selectively enhances some of those representations. In the [Attention Schema Theory](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00500) (AST), the brain also builds a representation of attention itself. Imagine an artist paints pictures of things in his world. Now imagine that he paints a picture of himself painting pictures. That’s AST. Suppose a person feels a touch on the hand. That person says he has a conscious experience of that touch. He says so because his speech networks are informed by his cognitive networks, and his cognitive networks contain a representation, a neural picture if you will, that describes that state of conscious experience. His cognitive networks contain that representation because automatic subsystems, hidden beneath the layer of cognition, construct that information and pipe it up to higher cognition. Those subsystems construct that information because it serves as a useful, though simplified, representation of attention. In effect, we think we have consciousness of the touch, when we actually have attention on the touch, and the consciousness story is the way that the brain understands its own attention. AST provides a pathway from the act of attending to something, through intervening stages of cognitive representation, to the explicit belief and the verbal statement that a conscious experience exists.

In the last few years, attention has leaped beyond psychology, neuroscience, and philosophy. It now plays an outsized role in technology. In 2017, a now-famous paper was published, called “[Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762).” In it, Lukasz Kaiser and his colleagues proposed a new type of artificial neural mechanism called the transformer architecture, which incorporated principles of attention. The result was a sudden leap in the capability of artificial neural networks. Almost overnight, AI went from a science fiction trope to a reality. People could talk to machines—and the machines could talk back, answer questions, solve problems, and understand the imprecise language of real humans. The technological leap was complex, intricate, and yet at the same time, simple. The enormous power of attention had been harnessed.

Scientists are [still debating](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.08211) whether the artificial attention in transformers is the same kind of thing as the attention implemented in the brain. The answer [appears to be both yes and no](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2502.19281). The underlying concepts are similar. Transformer attention depends on selectively weighting some “tokens” or internal representations more than others, just as physiological attention depends on weighting some neural representations more than others. The exact mechanism of weighting differs. Using artificial neurons modeled after biological ones, hooked together into vast networks modeled after real neural networks, and trained on data from the internet that reflects human behavior, AI has come to function in some respects like the human brain.

Regardless of the specific comparison between transformers and physiological attention, AI is now showing an emergent property that more closely matches human attention. It resembles, more than anything else, the kind of attention that William James poetically described over 100 years ago. Just like in the human brain, artificial neural networks represent information in the form of patterns of activity across neurons. These patterns can represent concrete objects or highly abstract concepts, ranging from the color blue, to a cat, to the idea of justice. Researchers have begun to probe these representations in the hidden layers of AI, studying how the representations rise up in activation, shift and combine, and then sink back down again, as the agent solves a task or answers a question. This type of research, spying on the thoughts encoded within the mind of the network, is called [mechanistic interpretability](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2305.01610), or “mech interp.” When a representation rises in activation and dominates the output of the AI agent, that process is, to borrow James’ words, “the taking possession of the mind … of one out of what seems several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.” We are seeing attention in the most human sense emerging naturally in the artificial mind of a neural network agent.

If AI can have human-like attention, then [can it have human-like consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/the-case-for-conscious-ai)? Opinions are of course conflicting, but according to theories like AST, the answer is probably yes. Attention is the key to much of our psychology, and if it is implemented in artificial systems, then a human-like mind may follow in its wake.
