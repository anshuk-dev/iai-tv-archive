# Reality and objective truth diverge

*Are morality and mathematics objective or real?*

**By Justin Clarke-Doane** | *18th June 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,080 words*

![Reality and objective truth diverge](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Objective-truth-and-reality-diverge.webp)

---

*If God is dead, then everything is permitted, worried Dostoevsky. Without a realm of moral facts independent of our minds, objective moral truth seemed impossible. Yet, argues Justin Clarke-Doane, moral anti-realism is perfectly compatible with objective moral truth. Conversely, if we insist on realism about mathematics – the idea that our mathematics tracks a reality outside of our minds – then we must give up on the idea that mathematical claims are objectively true. Realism and objectivity, far from always going together, seem here to be in tension.*

**Realism and objectivity**

To what extent are the subjects of our thought and talk real? This is the [question of *realism*](http://iai.tv/articles/introducing-the-realism-debate-auid-1763). Realism about a subject says that there are facts about it, and these facts hold independently of our beliefs. For example, many of us are realists about mathematics. We believe that there are facts about numbers, sets, and tensors, and these facts do not depend on us. Realism contrasts with constructivism, according to which the facts about a given subject do depend on the minds of enquirers. Many of us are constructivists about aesthetics, for instance.

\_\_\_

Philosophers have widely associated, and even identified, realism and objectivity. But this is a mistake. As we’ll see, realism and objectivity are in tension in domains like mathematics and morality.

\_\_\_

What is the relationship between realism and *objectivity*? Objectivity about a subject opposes relativism, the idea that there are only relative facts about the subject. Philosophers have widely associated, and even identified, realism and objectivity. But this is a mistake. As we’ll see, realism and objectivity are in tension in domains like mathematics and morality.

Consider geometry. Suppose that you pass two straight lines, *A* and *B*, through a third line, *C*, and on one side of line, *C*, the sum of the angles that *A* and *B* make with *C* equals less than *180*° – that is, their sum is less than the sum of two right angles. Must *A* and *B* intersect? This is the famous Parallel Postulate question. We could understand it as a question about lines (“geodesics”) in physical spacetime. But let us ask the Parallel Postulate question as a question of pure mathematics. Is it true then?

The question is misconceived. In a sense that is distinct from mind-independence, the Parallel Postulate question lacks a non-relative, i.e., *objective,* answer. There are simply different geometries, and these give different answers to the Parallel Postulate question. Relative to Euclidean geometry, the answer is “yes.” But relative to, say, hyperbolic geometry the answer is “no.” All we would learn in answering the Parallel Postulate question is something about how we use words. We would only learn which geometrical structures we were talking about, rather than learning which there were.

To be clear: it is not that geometrical reality depends on us. If mathematical realism is true, then how things are with Euclidean, hyperbolic, or variably curved spaces depends entirely on the mind-independent mathematical facts. The point is that there is no once-and-for-all answer to the Parallel Postulate question. Geometrical reality is so rich as to furnish any answer to it that we might have proffered. Even if geometry is real, it is not objective.

**Anti-Objective Realism about Mathematics**

The view that geometry is not objective in my sense is hardly controversial, even among mathematical realists. But controversy arises over so-called *foundational* theories, like arithmetic and set theory (the theory of collections, with ordinary, i.e., “classical”, logic). Foundational theories concern the assumptions that we are allowed to make when reasoning about any mathematical subject, whether Euclidean space, hyperbolic space, or something different. Could it be that those assumptions are *also* like the Parallel Postulate – concerning something arguably real but not objective? Could there be different natural numbers, and different universes of set, as there are different geometries – with no canonical collection of them all?

Not in general, it seems. Some foundational axioms must be objectively true. Kurt Gödel proved that [claims about consistency](https://academic.oup.com/book/55022) – e.g. the claim that standard arithmetic is consistent – are equivalent, by coding, to arithmetic statements that we can consistently deny (or affirm), if standard arithmetic is consistent. His Second Incompleteness Theorem asserts that standard arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency if it is consistent. [1] And logic tells that if a theory, *T*, (like standard arithmetic) does *not* imply a sentence, *S*, (like the claim that standard arithmetic is consistent), then *T* + not-*S* must be consistent. So, if all consistent sets of foundational axioms were legitimate, as we maintain in the geometric case, then there would not even be an objective fact about whether arithmetic is consistent! [2]

There is, however, a lot of breathing room between the view that all areas of mathematics are like geometry, and the view that every sentence of our foundational theories is objectively true or false. For instance, perhaps while every *arithmetic* sentence (including any that codes a claim about consistency) is objectively true or false, every other sentence that is consistent and not a truth of pure logic is like the Parallel Postulate. If we call a theory that fails to imply any arithmetic falsehoods “arithmetically coherent,” then we may define mathematical anti-objectivism as the view that all arithmetically coherent foundational theories are true (albeit of different subjects, as in geometry).

What is there to recommend mathematical anti-objectivism? Mathematical objectivism generates intractable epistemological problems. How can we know what foundational axioms are true?

Consider the standard axioms of set theory, the so-called *ZFC* axioms (which encompass standard arithmetic but much more). There are consistent alternatives to these axioms, each with defenders. How can a disagreement over the *ZFC* axioms be resolved? Not via proof. Proofs assume axioms! Nor are experiments relevant, as they are to typical scientific disagreements. No experiment can tell us whether the Axiom of Choice (a standard axiom of set theory) is true. The view that axioms like this are objectively true or false, despite no way of settling debates over them, is like the view that the Parallel Postulate is. We outgrew the latter view. Perhaps it is time to outgrow the former.

The upshot is that mathematics invites us to tease apart two concepts that are often conflated: realism (the idea that there is an independent reality) and objectivity (the idea that questions admit of non-relative answers). A plausible philosophy of mathematics combines mathematical realism with anti-objectivism. Realism and objectivity are easily confused because they seem to converge in the empirical case. If we follow Einstein in holding that the moon exists when no one is looking, then we are realists about the macroscopic empirical world (notwithstanding debates about the “interpretation” of quantum mechanics). And if we reject radical multiverse scenarios, then we believe that truths about it are objective.

**Objective Anti-Realism about Morality**

What about the other direction? Can an area be objective, but not real? Consider a *prima facie* unrelated domain, morality. We said that in the mathematical case, the Axiom of Choice is like the Parallel Postulate. It is true relative to some universes of set, false relative to others, and that is all there is to it. The setsAC and sets~AC live in harmony, no one more real than the other – as in the geometric case. But now consider a moral question, like whether we ought to kill one person to save five people in some concrete scenario. Is this question *also* like the Parallel Postulate question? If so, then we ought to kill one person relative to utilitarian ethics, but also ought not relative to, say, Kantian ethics, and that is all there is to it.

\_\_\_

And, yet, morality is not real.

\_\_\_

That cannot be right! We would still have to decide *whether to do* what we ought relative to utilitarian ethics or ought not relative to Kantian ethics to do. Morality, unlike mathematics, issues in action, and we can only do one thing in a circumstance. Killing one person in a given scenario precludes not killing them. So, insofar as morality is action-guiding, it is objective. Deliberation about what to do demands a non-relative answer.

And, yet, morality is not real. Perhaps it is coherent to imagine independent facts about what we ought to do, along with such facts about prime numbers – whatever the liabilities of this imagery. But no such facts could be moral in the really important sense. None could *settle deliberation*. Suppose that in wondering whether to kill one person to save five people, we learn that it is a *fact* that we ought not. This fact is as robust as the fact that there are infinitely-many prime numbers. The question remains: *whether to do* what we ought to do. This question cannot be the question of whether we *ought* to do what we ought to do, since this latter question is trivial, while the former is substantive. For any fact, it is *up to us* whether to live our lives by consulting it. And this decision cannot be externalized – treated as a further fact to consult – on pain of regress.

\_\_\_

When we deliberate, we aim to discover what to do. The point is that what we discover cannot be a fact. The question of what to do remains after all the facts are in.

\_\_\_

The point is not that answers to moral questions are arbitrary. We do not just plump for, say, utilitarian ethics and act accordingly. Nor is it the truism that we can be weak in will, failing to act in line with our values. When we deliberate, we aim to *discover* what to do. The point is that what we discover cannot be a fact. The question of what to do remains after all the facts are in.

**A Tension between Realism and Objectivity**

Reflection on mathematics and morality, therefore, reveals a bifurcation, even a tension, between the concepts of realism and objectivity. There is *epistemological* pressure for mathematical realists to be anti-objectivists. If the independent mathematics facts were objective, then it is hard to see how we could know them. But if mathematics is generally like geometry, then our mathematical knowledge is intelligible. Conversely, there is *deliberative* pressure for moral antirealists to be objectivists. Anti-objectivism is not an option at the level of action, because we can only do one thing in a circumstance. We have to take a stand in action in a way that we need not in belief.

I have argued elsewhere ([2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mathematics-and-metaphilosophy/10EC72A19110CEC96F866E477BC25E4E), [2024](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/morality-and-mathematics-9780198898863?lang=en&cc=me)) that anti-objectivism about mathematics engenders anti-objectivism about modality (the theory of how the world could have been) and even logic (the theory of what follows from what). Since any adequate theory of the physical world will be formulated mathematically and closed under logical consequence, with implications for how the world could have been, the physical facts cannot be fully objective either (despite our naive realist objectivism about the empirical world alluded to earlier). But there *is* an objective question of what the right physical theory is. “Right,” like moral “ought,” is evaluative, answering to a decision. So, as with the question of whether to kill one person to save the five people, this question demands a non-relative resolution. Realist anti-objectivism about mathematics, and anti-realist objectivism about morality, therefore lead to realist anti-objectivism about science and anti-realist objectivism about intellectual policy. The world is real. But its objectivity is substantially our projection. [3][[3](#_ftn3)
