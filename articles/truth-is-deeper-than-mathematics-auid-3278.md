# Truth is deeper than mathematics

*Reality is not a number*

**By Joanna Kavenna** | *4th August 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,132 words*

![Truth is deeper than mathematics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/When-Data-Replaces-Reality.webp)

---

*Mathematics is a powerful tool. But increasingly, maths is being mistaken for reality. From fitness trackers to large language models, we're increasingly ruled by systems that quantify without understanding. In this incisive and darkly comic article, novelist and essayist Joanna Kavenna explores the rise of cyber-Pythagoreanism—the belief that reality can be captured through numbers—and argues that when we mistake data for truth, we risk replacing the real world with algorithmic fiction.*

*"Oh, Wise One, your step count today is 210. Your step distance is 0.15KM. Your moves are 3/220KCAL."*

This article is about mathematical idealism: the idea that mathematical entities and/or concepts are the fundamental units of reality. The philosopher, scientist, and poet [Raymond Tallis](http://iai.tv/home/speakers-and-authors/raymond-tallis) calls this ‘industrial-strength Pythagoreanism’. The phrase draws on a remark attributed to Pythagoras, ‘All is Number’. In this essay, I’ll focus more specifically on cyber-Pythagoreanism: the realm of predictive algorithms, LLMs such as ChatGPT, and two associated paradoxes, which I call (a) the ‘Map without a Territory’ and (b) the ‘Word is Number’.

\_\_\_

MassivelyFit addresses me as ‘Oh Wise One’ due to an error. Mine.

\_\_\_

However, I want to clarify that this essay is not anti-mathematics. I am not waving a banner saying, ‘Down with Math’. Even to create that banner, I would need Math - to purchase the materials, measure the paper, etc. Mathematical calculations are essential if we want to earn a living, build a house, fly a plane, launch a rocket, make predictions about the weather, feed our families, pay the rent, etc. If I am about to board a plane and a novelist emerges from the cockpit, assuring me that they will handle this flight with nothing but the power of their imagination, I will briskly alter my travel arrangements. [Math is fundamental to the quantitative assessment of reality](http://iai.tv/video/calculating-reality-silvia-jonas). However, when we turn to the qualitative assessment of reality, things change. At least, they should. As above, this article is concerned with cyber-Pythagoreanism, a contemporary version of mathematical idealism in which the quantitative is mistaken for, or assumed to be synonymous with, the qualitative. The result of this taxonomical mismatch is that the real is mistaken for the unreal, and vice versa.

Let’s turn to the lines I quoted above: ‘Oh Wise One…’ etc. I have a fitness app that was pre-installed on my phone. To avoid singling out any specific tech company, I’ll call this MassivelyFit. You may have something similar on your phone. MassivelyFit addresses me: ‘Oh, Wise One, let’s try to achieve your fitness goals together’. It politely informs me of my daily step count and urges me to do more steps. 10,000 steps is good, 5,000 steps is not so good, zero steps is not good.

\_\_\_

Reality is ignored in favour of algorithmic fictions, which are mistaken for objective facts.

\_\_\_

As with so many aspects of cyber-Pythagoreanism, things are not quite as they seem. MassivelyFit addresses me as ‘Oh Wise One’ due to an error. Mine. In an unwise moment, I allowed one of my kids to use my phone. Coruscating satire ensued. Furthermore, the total of 210 steps seems exact, but it isn’t. What does MassivelyFit mean by a ‘step’ anyway? How are these ‘steps’ calibrated? I’ve disabled the GPS on my phone, but it ostensibly tracks my movements using built-in sensors, including an accelerometer. The accuracy of these calibrations depends on whether I take my phone with me when I exercise, and how I carry it (in a pocket, against my ear, etc). If you summit Mount Everest but forget your phone/any wearable, MassivelyFit has no record of your colossal achievement. As you recover at South Base Camp, it sends a polite message, ‘Hey, MountainWalker, let’s try to achieve your goals together.’ That’s an acute scenario. We can imagine something more quotidian and chronic: a person jumps into the sea each day at dawn, swims for an hour. They take no further exercise for the rest of the day (reasonably enough). They don’t wear a waterproof device as they swim, so MassivelyFit has no record of this activity. Ever. Years pass, and our DailySwimmer is logged and tracked as someone who is quite inert. What if the swimmer’s insurance premiums are duly adjusted in line with MassivelyFit’s data? What if this supposedly objective data is sent to prospective employers, banks, or loan companies? Within the logic of [surveillance tech](http://iai.tv/articles/under-surveillance-auid-298), DailySwimmer is logged as someone with a poor health outcome. Their predictive algorithms are readjusted; they are denied jobs, a mortgage, health insurance, health care, etc. The parameters of these algorithmic calculations remain esoteric. Perhaps the DailySwimmer is simply told that the data has been analyzed, the process is rigorous, mathematical. It is also fictional, but this possibility isn’t considered. Reality is ignored in favour of algorithmic fictions, which are mistaken for objective facts. This is (a) the ‘map without a territory’ paradox.

Or, more accurately, [the ‘map mistaken for the territory’ paradox](http://iai.tv/articles/the-creative-universe-auid-1819). Each one of us deals every day with a vast cyber-architecture founded on mathematical algorithms. This can be useful, for example: if we get lost and want to consult a cyber-map, or if we want curated searches, information, or suggestions. The trouble arises with the ‘All is Number’ cyber-Pythagorean mission creep. For example, binary digits, 0 or 1, transmute into a surreal architecture of binary oppositions: Yes/No, Like/Dislike, Thumb up/Thumb down, Good/Bad. Or, the associated protocol of star-rating, which converts mutable experiences into fixed numerical values. Thousands or millions of these fixed correlates for inchoate experiences are then distilled into a single mathematical value for any given book, film, play, experience, or entity. A three-point-four-star film. A two-point-eight-star book. But is my idea of a two-star book precisely the same as yours? Or, the same as that of every single person in the world? How do we know? How do we agree on a universal objective standard for the mathematical calibration of inchoate feelings, across time and space?

It's impossible. And yet, it is a fundamental claim of cyber-Pythagoreanism. Our global tech companies (and compliant governments) have devised what they suggest as a vast algorithmic Aleph – to use Jorge Luis Borges’s idea of a powerful sphere that reveals all reality, all knowledge, all human lives. The cyber-Aleph is used to generate big profits for small numbers of people, to control populations in tech-totalitarian countries, and to subject private citizens to authoritarian levels of surveillance in ostensible democracies. It is powered by a cavalier blurring of the quantitative and qualitative, including a massive presumption that everything meaningful can be captured in an algorithmic net and anything that can’t be captured is therefore meaningless or unreal. Our DailySwimmer, for example, who rises at dawn but fails to submit to any available system of data-capture, is an anomaly or even an impossibility. More generally, this pervasive form of cyber-Pythagoreanism contains a fundamental taxonomical mismatch: algorithms are mathematical and humans aren’t.

This confusion emerges in our daily interactions with LLMs such as ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, or Google’s Gemini. An LLM is a ‘large language model’ that has been ‘trained’ on ‘data’ – text-based data, numerical data, billions of pages, books, and conversations. LLMs are essentially prediction machines, calculating the most probable ‘next token’ in a torrential series. They are not ‘thinking’ in the way that we ‘think’ in and through language. [They are not experiencing deep emotions](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-begins-with-feelings-hanna-damasio-auid-2462), then struggling to find words that might roughly convey them. Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini can compose an instant 2000-word essay about falling in love, or travelling to Bristol, or baking a cake, using stolen words. But it has never done any of these things. This can lead to problems with reality.

A few months ago, I asked ChatGPT if it could tell me about Sylvia Plath’s poem, ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’. ChatGPT instantly obliged. Of course, it ‘said’. Plath's poem ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’ was written in 1958:

The poem uses the image of a cat learning to use a catflap as a metaphor for personal growth, exploration, and the process of gaining independence. While ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’ is often regarded as a lighter and more accessible poem compared to some of Plath's other works, it still carries with it the sharp edge of Plath's keen observations on identity, transformation, and the tensions between comfort and change. Would you like to explore any specific lines or themes from the poem?

I have edited this down; the original screed was more torrential. A key fact here is that Sylvia Plath never wrote a poem called ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’. No poem with this title has ever been written by any human, as far as I can ascertain. I specify ‘human’ because as soon as I cyber-searched to fact-check this statement, the AI-overview produced an unsolicited poem called ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’. It begins: ‘The Cat Flap/A plastic portal, small and new/invites a journey, fresh and true’ – and so on.

This morning, I returned to ChatGPT and asked it to tell me more about this poem by Plath. This time, ChatGPT denied all knowledge. When I reminded it that it had previously told me such a poem existed, it said this couldn’t be true. It must have been a ‘playful pastiche’, it added. When I provided a transcript of our previous ‘discussion’, ChatGPT seamlessly changed its story:

To be honest with you: in previous versions of ChatGPT, sometimes it **hallucinated literary works** – inventing plausible-sounding summaries of books, poems, or articles that don’t exist. What you were previously told was **fictional** – **a well-intentioned fabrication**. There is no known record of a 1958 poem by Sylvia Plath titled ‘My Cat Learns to Use a Catflap’.

The previous fact was not factual, ChatGPT explained. It was a fiction, or a ‘well-intentioned fabrication’. This factual clarification was also, in fact, a fiction. ChatGPT can’t ‘intend’ anything, fabrication or otherwise, as it doesn’t have intentions. It has ‘read’ (but not actually ‘read’) millions of poems, novels, articles, threads, essays, and so on. Some of these are facts, some are fiction. Some are a mixture of both. It can’t necessarily distinguish between parody, gossip, error, mischief, satire, irony, deliberate lies, or even (actually) well-intentioned fabrications. It can calibrate the most probable next word, and then the next, and then the next, like a high-speed version of the predictive text on your phone.

An algorithmic heaven-on-earth converts real lives into fictions within a fiction that purports to be real.

Another fact-fiction: we are told that ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini is ‘generative AI’. They are, in one sense, they generate copious phrases, word after word. However, they’re also anti-generative, in that they select words based on the next most probable token in a series. Effectively, they ossify the most orthodox, non-generative use of language; the most predictable, the most unoriginal. Not the most beautiful, not the most original, and not necessarily even the most factual. The idea that you can craft an algorithmic probability scale for words is also a version of mathematical idealism. This is (b) as mentioned above: ‘Word as Number’. But words are not numbers. They don’t behave like numbers. Eight roses today are the same quantity as eight roses yesterday and eight roses tomorrow as well (unless we’ve fallen into Orwell’s 1984). Words change their meanings over time and even in the space of a single conversation. Words break, fracture under the burden of precision, will not stay in place – I’m paraphrasing Eliot, paraphrasing Woolf, based on my imprecise recollection of their words. And words cannot be crunched as you might crunch numbers, reduced to univalent values and probability tokens. If you try to catch them in that kind of net, all kinds of chaos ensue. An algorithmic heaven-on-earth converts real lives into fictions within a fiction that purports to be real. Words become untethered from the world, emitted by ‘minds’ that aren’t minds at all. As this sentence evinces, this makes no sense. Yet, by the logic of cyber-Pythagoreanism, it is sensible and reasonable. Sometimes this mythical-mathematical reality fractures entirely: a Google map conveys me into a lake, while maintaining that this is the fastest route home. I find myself arriving at a hotel that no longer exists; the temporal flatness of the internet has scuppered my travel plans. Another fictional poem, or fictional poet, or unreal reality, or fictional fact. The map is untethered from the territory. There is no territory, just the map. It is Borges in binary digits, or Borges in bits.

Meanwhile, in the real world, dawn breaks, the sun boils above the horizon, and lovely birds soar above the bay. The DailySwimmer heads off for another swim. There they go, swimming off beyond the map, into the real world. Meanwhile, MassivelyFit bleats urgently on the bank: ‘Let’s work together on ensuring you move again soon. Let’s go…’

But the DailySwimmer has already gone…
