# How your beliefs change what you perceive

*The holistic mind and the myth of the given*

**By Christopher Mole** | *21st May 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,817 words*

![How your beliefs change what you perceive](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/modularity-of-the-brain.webp)

---

*Philosophers often fantasize about starting from facts “given” by sense perception, building up the rest of their beliefs from this foundation. But this is a fantasy because sense experiences are themselves partly constructed by our prior beliefs: sense perception and thought seep into one other. But, argues Christopher Mole, this doesn’t mean that perception is always biased in favour of our prior convictions: on the contrary, the influence of belief on perception can help us better navigate the world.*

Solvers of sudoku know that one sometimes gets into such a muddle that there is nothing for it but to start again. Errors in one place bring others in their train, so that the best thing to do may be to go back to a point before any error could have been made, discarding everything except for the numbers that were given initially. Philosophers have often [fantasized about making a similarly fresh start](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logical-empiricism/) with the whole assembled body of our beliefs, putting aside all of our variously acquired opinions – some of which will surely have been picked up erroneously – and going back to whatever facts were given to us in sense perception, before any erroneous assumptions had a chance to lead us astray.

\_\_\_

The experiences that we have when we see or hear something are partly shaped by our beliefs, so that perceiving the world is not an unbiased way of being “given” facts about it.

\_\_\_

Although it may be a useful exercise in humility to imagine making such a fresh start, it would be fanciful to suppose that anyone could really go through with making one. It is just too difficult to know whether one has managed to set one’s beliefs aside: their influence is too pervasive, and too subtle, and this [includes an influence on perception itself](https://academic.oup.com/book/7344/chapter-abstract/152097751?redirectedFrom=fulltext). The experiences that we have when we see or hear something are partly shaped by our beliefs, so that perceiving the world is not an unbiased way of being “given” facts about it. Sometimes this might prevent perceptual experience from providing us with an impartial source of evidence, but it would be a mistake to suppose that it is always a hinderance to our capacity for accessing reality.

Clinical drug trials provide one illustration of the way in which our beliefs can skew our experiences. In most such trials some of the participants are given a drug, and are told about its possible effects and side-effects, while others are told about these same effects and side effects whilst being given a placebo. It is common for around a quarter of those in the placebo group to drop out, on the grounds that they are experiencing unwelcome side-effects. Some of these people will, by chance, have fallen ill, and are mistaking the symptoms of that illness for side-effects of the treatment that they believe themselves to be receiving. Others will be making excuses for withdrawing for other reasons. But there do seem to be some for whom the expectation of side-effects is by itself enough to cause an experience of those effects, just as there are some for whom taking [the placebo](http://video/nature-s-hidden-persuaders) leads to an experience of improvements in their symptoms.

A certain amount is known about [the mechanisms underlying such placebo and nocebo effects](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMra1907805). Less is known about the effects of beliefs on our experiences more generally. It is sometimes suggested that these effects are inescapable, since one’s brain draws on all sorts of beliefs, even in the most basic cases of perception. Some of the psychologists who study the roots of social prejudice have thought that this thwarts any hope of impartiality, taking it that our perceptions must inevitably be biased in favour of our prior convictions. But that last view is unduly pessimistic. In many cases the influence of belief on perception is rather slight. In some cases it may even be non-existent. And in other cases that influence is benign.  As philosophers such as Dustin Stokes have recently emphasised, it can sometimes constitute a form of [perceptual expertise](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/mila.12270) that facilitates our accuracy and objectivity, rather than hindering them.

\_\_\_

The benign influence of belief on perception is clearest when the perception in question is auditory, and when the thing that is being perceived is speech, especially if that speech is in a foreign language. Speakers of an unfamiliar language sound at first as if they were talking at an impossibly fast pace, with scarcely any discernable difference between sounds that can make a great difference as to the meaning of what’s being said. Eventually it becomes possible to keep up, so that one then hears a sequence of distinct words, rather than a gabble of sounds. And at that point there’s no going back. One’s knowledge of the language now seems to be shaping the most basic way in which one’s brain creates an auditory experience from the soundwaves that come into one’s ears.

In this case knowledge influences perception only after long practice, but there is also evidence that the way in which speech is heard can be influenced more immediately, by simple changes in expectation. There are [viral videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8FXQ38-ZQK0) that depend on such an influence, and the effect on which they depend can also be studied in the lab, by manipulating an audio recording so that it sounds sometimes like one word and sometimes like another (by, for example, making it ambiguous between “sack” and “shack”). If literate adults are shown some text that leads them to expect one of these words then that expected word will usually be what they hear, and if the ambiguous sound is presented again later then they will continue to experience it in that way.

Experimental studies of this effect have an advantage over some other studies of belief’s influence on perception because the effect here really does seem to be a perceptual one. A skeptic might suspect there is no real influence *on perception*; that the ambiguous sound continues to be heard as ambiguous although people are more likely to tell an experimenter that they have heard one thing or the other when the context prompts them to do so. That possibility is always worth bearing in mind – experimental participants often do pick up on what the experimenter is expecting, and then fall into line with those expectations – but in the present case there are grounds for ruling it out: you can experience the effect for yourself, and can hear that the ambiguous word really does *sound* different depending on what you tell yourself to expect. And this effect can also be created in an fMRI scanner, where we can see that [different expectations lead to differences in the activity taking place during early stages of the brain’s auditory processing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-05356-3).

In the case of listening to speech the character of one’s perceptual experience does therefore seem to be shaped at a basic level by one’s beliefs and expectations. But in other cases – and especially in cases that involve seeing rather than hearing – it is harder to rule out the skeptic’s suggestion that the character of the experience remains the same, with the only effects here being effects on the way in which people are willing to describe those experiences when psychologists ask about them. In a 2015 article, Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl argued that [this sceptical suggestion was able to account for a great many of the effects](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26189677/#:~:text=We%20argue%20otherwise%3A%20None%20of,only%20a%20handful%20of%20pitfalls.) that had been thought to indicate that our beliefs have a direct influence on vision. Experimental participants might be induced to *report* that hills look steeper in contexts where they have been prompted to think about the difficulty of climbing them, and they may indicate that grey patches look more yellow when those patches are shaped like bananas, but Firestone and Scholl argued that this is probably not because the visual cortex takes account of our beliefs about hill-climbing or about bananas when it performs the computations that turn the light falling onto our retinas into an experience of those hills, or of those patches.

\_\_\_

Such effects show that belief’s influence on perception is real, but they do little to undermine perception’s claim to provide a source of objective evidence for those beliefs.

\_\_\_

Firestone and Scholl’s considerations exposed weaknesses in much of the evidence that was then thought to show that beliefs influence vision, but there is some evidence that survived their onslaught, including the evidence which come from the study of so-called “Mooney images”:

Our experience of such images is somewhat analogous to that of the beginning language learner: they look like an uninterpretable mass of splotches until one catches on to what is being depicted, whereupon the splotches are seen to be grouped into coherent objects, and then there is no going back to the experience that was uninflected by this knowledge. Here too one can experience the visual difference for oneself (by looking at the Mooney image above, looking at its source image at the end of this article, and then looking back at the Mooney image). Here one can experience directly that knowing which thing is depicted really does seem to change the character of one’s visual experience. This effect can also be produced in a brain scanner, where it can be seen that some early parts of the visual cortex [respond differently when the experience is a knowledge-modulated one](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20071627/).

Such effects show that belief’s influence on perception is real, but they do little to undermine perception’s claim to provide a source of objective evidence for those beliefs. Perhaps learning a language makes it harder to objectively perceive the rate at which its speakers produce phonemes, and perhaps knowing which animal is depicted in a Mooney image makes one susceptible to certain illusions, in which [the uniformly black parts of that image now seem to contain subtle contours](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-28845-5). On the whole, however, more knowledgeable perceivers will be able to use their knowledge in exercising some degree of perceptual expertise, and so they will be able to discern more, and will be more astutely responsive to the details of the world that they encounter. Although we may be unable to use perception as a theoretically uncontaminated source of given facts, from which to make a fresh start with the formation of our beliefs, this does not mean that those beliefs must now be as dubious as the numbers in a sudoku where an error might somewhere have been made.
