# We're risking it all for beauty

*The political economy of appearance*

**By Heather Widdows** | *13th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,850 words*

![We're risking it all for beauty](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/risking-it-all-for-beauty-2.webp)

---

*Beauty is often dismissed as superficial, but philosopher **Heather Widdows** argues it has quietly become the most valuable good in modern life. From risky cosmetic procedures to the relentless gaze of social media, we repeatedly prioritise appearance over health, education, even safety. If beauty now shapes our opportunities and life chances so powerfully, it can no longer be dismissed as a private obsession: it is a political and ethical issue that calls for serious public scrutiny and stronger regulation.*

“Don’t judge a book by its cover.” “It’s what’s on the inside that counts.” Beauty, we are told, is trivial and unimportant. To care about how we look, whether we are thin, firm, smooth, and young enough, is to care about the wrong things. Beauty is valued by the superficial, the young, the unserious. Not by the serious, clever and wise.

\_\_\_

Most of us do value beauty and believe that being attractive makes life go better. This isn’t a niche view.

\_\_\_

If this is true in general, it is even more true in philosophy. Socrates said that “the genuine philosopher disdains fashion,” and Kant [dispensed with fashion, declaring it “foolish”](https://aeon.co/essays/why-does-philosophy-hold-clothes-in-such-low-regard). Indeed, so ingrained in philosophy is this view that caring about appearance can be taken as proof someone is unphilosophical, even unintelligent. This is illustrated in a 2019 social media post; in reply to the question “What is the meanest thing one of your superiors ever said to you?” the answer was:

*One of my first ever (male, because of course) ethics professors said ‘there is an inverse relationship between how well a woman philosopher dresses and how smart she is.’*

But philosophers rarely give reasons for why beauty is not valuable. We can only assume that this is because they think it is obvious, a view that doesn’t need defending. To give just one example, consider how  [Elizabeth Anderson](home/speakers-and-authors/elizabeth-anderson) writes of beauty in her famous paper, “[What is the point of Equality?](https://www.philosophy.rutgers.edu/joomlatools-files/docman-files/4ElizabethAnderson.pdf)“:

*When is a deficit in internal assets so bad as to require compensation? One doesn’t want anyone with any trivial personal dissatisfaction, such as having bad hair, to be entitled to compensation.*

“Bad hair,” Anderson assumes, is obviously trivial, and she can count on her philosophical readers agreeing. She knows that not everyone thinks like this, but these people are wrong, morally wrong, suffering from the vice of vanity:

*Suppose a vain person would get hysterical over the prospect of being genetically determined to have a hooked nose. A person’s anxiety over this prospect might be enough to make it rational for her to take out insurance for plastic surgery before knowing how her nose will turn out. It is hard to see how such as preference could create an obligation on the part of society to pay for her plastic surgery. Moreover, many people don’t see hooked noses as such a bad thing, and many of these people have hooked noses: they would rightly feel insulted if society treated having a hooked nose as such a grievous defect that it was entitled to compensation.*

But why is it wrong, anxious, or vain to choose cosmetic surgery, to value appearance, even to think beauty is a good that should be fairly distributed? Anderson does not explain; she does not make an argument, and yet this is a premise on which she builds her argument. Many, perhaps most, people don’t share this view.  On the contrary, most of us do value beauty and believe that being attractive makes life go better. This isn’t a niche view, but a mainstream view. The “[Halo Effect](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/halo-effect)” and “[Pretty Privilege](https://www.verywellmind.com/pretty-privilege-8694834)” are well-known and documented, and even Anderson knows what a “bad hair day” is.

\_\_\_

Try telling a teenager growing up in selfie culture that how they look doesn’t matter. They know it does.

\_\_\_

Comprehensive and uncontested data is hard to come by—in part because beauty has not been taken seriously. But the data we have overwhelmingly points to beauty being extremely valuable. The sheer size of the global market for beauty products and processes speaks volumes (in 2025, [the size of the cosmetics market was estimated at around $450 billion and the skincare market more than $161 billion](https://www.precedenceresearch.com/cosmetics-market)). We spend inordinate amounts of time and money improving our appearance. From the daily application of lotions and potions, to working out, treatments, tweakments, and surgery, the list is endless. Moreover, the beauty market is recession-proof. We don’t give up on how we look. Despite the protestations, then, beauty is valued and valuable.

That this is so is repeated across domains. Attractive people are [assumed to be more competent, intelligent, and with better personalities](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1991-33123-001). Unattractive people are judged negatively, as lazy or untrustworthy. [Teachers treat good-looking students better](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1983-26690-001), and “ugly” criminals have [higher conviction rates and harsher punishments](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12019/w12019.pdf). Study after study tells us that the attractive earn more. In his famous book [*Beauty Pays*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158174/beauty-pays?srsltid=AfmBOoqCMAbe1Q9QuYeuhXFzxhaaVq1lYYFWfKAsb9od3h-bTw8LEFO2), Daniel Hamermesh describes a “beauty premium” and an “ugly penalty.” In the courtroom, the classroom and the bedroom, the attractive are thought to *be* better and are *treated* better. And what’s more, we’ve known this for a very long time. Recall the commentary around the Kennedy/Nixon debate, where TV viewers thought Kennedy won, and radio listeners Nixon. Even in our oldest stories, our myths and fairy tales, goodness is beautiful. Heroes and heroines are young and beautiful, and stepmothers, goblins, orcs, ogres, and ugly sisters are ugly. Even Shrek is cute, in a cuddly dad-bod kind of way.  The evidence is clear. Appearance matters. As Nancy Etcoff wrote in her landmark book, [*Survival of the Prettiest*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Survival-Prettiest-Science-Nancy-Etcoff/dp/0385479425), nearly 30 years ago, “beauty conveys modest but real social and economic advantages and, equally important, ugliness leads to major social disadvantages and discrimination.”

Given this, we can’t, as philosophers have done, simply assume that beauty is obviously trivial and worthless. An argument must be made. We can imagine what kinds of arguments might be made. For example, beauty is transient and passing and therefore not worth pursuing. Perhaps, but so is love, and yet loving, even briefly, is supremely valued. Or perhaps the appearance of the body might be rejected in favor of “higher” pleasures, for “the life of the mind.” But really? Does this old dualist logic hold up? Humans are embodied beings, and how we feel and who we touch and are touched by informs how and what we think. So arguments are possible, but they are not simple, easy or obvious, and many will disagree. Try telling a teenager growing up in selfie culture that how they look doesn’t matter. They know it does. Perhaps it shouldn’t, but, again, that needs an argument. Moreover—and this is new—for many, appearance doesn’t just matter, it matters most of all. More than other goods; more, for example, than health or education.

\_\_\_

Demonstrably, we choose beauty over health every time we willingly undergo a risky beauty practice.

\_\_\_

Health and education are widely recognized as valuable goods. So valuable that many of us don’t think access to them should be left to brute luck. Article 26 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights states “everyone has the right to education” and that elementary education should be free and compulsory. While there is no right to health as such, since that would be impossible, minimal access to healthcare is a basic right. Article 25 states that “everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family, including food, clothing, housing and medical care and necessary social services.” Philosophers generally agree that these are valuable goods, and so valuable that they should be distributed fairly.  But beauty increasingly matters more than these goods, something that our theory, policy and practice have not recognized or caught up with. Just as we continue to buy beauty products in times of scarcity, we choose beauty over health and education. In psychological studies, [teenage girls clearly value beauty over education](https://d1wqtxts1xzle7.cloudfront.net/53076030/jado.2000.035020170510-11483-bzknyh-libre.pdf?1494468482=&response-content-disposition=inline%3B+filename%3DI_would_rather_be_size_10_than_have_str.pdf&Expires=1765199306&Signature=CFCTCtfj7vXyAwM~QV5RoaiLAzlFI-5sIVbUFasC3cLle886S-QFzy8BwQVN-KcEdOzdGW82Lv7uAnfpldypKPklYho5TP2ST9GZ2LrDo1aqFedLl8-2l9gKcea5~Y69ZRz~hvC48hQmc1Aj0JRNEwyMwUSNPlpa8Cdmcnac8zXkmdLldYkFUu8heZfFWmtUPbxU1cvGmQysVcJdwfJalnWLZHJwelRyDlGci~bsR8S7LTJsQRlJEyCqsIzw27lNIjjFPYbLDKZ3FX55eZHOXYHyIZr0fbSOGcqIyPny6QMt3g9Bqt0lcCi87kA2OzzUmurnd5mkGj8kRPsze8y7Fw__&Key-Pair-Id=APKAJLOHF5GGSLRBV4ZA):

“I reckon that if I fitted into size 10 jeans I would be happier. I would rather have that than straight A’s.”

“I think people think oh I have to look like that because they think that they will have a perfect life as well. If I'm beautiful, if I'm attractive, if I'm skinny then everything else in my life has to come up as well, like my school grades will come up, I'll get a boyfriend, you know I'll have a great social life.”

Demonstrably, we choose beauty over health every time we willingly undergo a risky beauty practice. Beauty practices are often risky. Fillers carry serious risks, including vascular occlusion, that can lead to tissue death or blindness. Skin lightening is deemed a public health risk by the World Health Organisation, and yet carried out routinely and extensively in the poorest places on the planet. Brazilian Butt Lifts (BBLs) are notoriously risky, and numerous women have died from them, including Alice Webb, a 33-year-old mother-of-five, and Demi Agoglia, a 26-year-old mother-of-three, who died in 2024. (For information on why BBLs are so risky, read the graphic description from a cosmetic surgeon in [*Perfect Me*](http://www.heatherwiddows.com/perfect-me.html).) Even widely available retinol creams can burn and damage skin. Routinely and constantly, we value beauty over other goods.

Beauty will likely become more valuable if trends continue. Appearance matters most in first impressions, and in a virtual culture, mediated by screens, very many interactions are little more than first impressions. Much of our communication is image-based, most obviously social media. Instagram and TikTok are overwhelmingly visual. Even X, (formally Twitter), which famously began as a text-only medium, is now largely visual—think of [the Pope wearing a puffer jacket](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2023/mar/27/pope-coat-ai-image-baby-boomers) or [Kamala in a CP uniform](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn8jg11ynj7o). Dating apps are visual, work meetings are on screens, and even philosophers have profile pictures. In this brave new world of selfie culture, appearance isn’t just valuable; it is the most valuable.

Yet [that we value beauty most](https://iai.tv/articles/plato-vs-the-algorithm-beauty-is-the-path-to-virtue-auid-3426) has been largely ignored, with disastrous consequences. For example, the primary ethical tool we use to protect individuals from harm in many contexts is informed consent. Only with informed consent can the surgeon operate, or the beauty practitioner inject Botox or fillers. Yet when it comes to beauty practices, informed consent does not protect us. If beauty is valued more than health, we will consent despite the risks to health. And why wouldn’t we, in a world where pretty privilege is real and beauty so valuable? We can still hear the whispers of the older view, the one that dismisses beauty as trivial or worthless. But [it seems less and less likely that we believe it](https://iai.tv/articles/the-pursuit-of-beauty-is-fragmenting-the-self-auid-3412). As we hit the gym, spend money on beauty products, and cut into our flesh, our actions tell a different story. We can say we don’t care about how we look, that we are eating well, dieting hard, and pumping and plumping for health, well-being or self-care. But if the upshot is always a thinner, firmer, younger—*better*—face and body, the pretense wears thin. If we don’t think beauty should be the most valuable good, dismissal and assertion aren’t good enough. [Argument](https://iai.tv/video/the-politics-of-beauty) is required.
