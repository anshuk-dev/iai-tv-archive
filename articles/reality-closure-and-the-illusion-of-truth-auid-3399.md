# Reality, Closure and the illusion of truth

*Science is about invention, not truth*

**By Hilary Lawson** | *24th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,225 words*

![Reality, Closure and the illusion of truth](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/reality-closure-and-the-illusion-of-trutgh-copy.webp)

---

*We assume reality consists of objects, stars and atoms, tables and chairs, animals and people.  But citing contemporary neuroscience, philosopher and closure theorist, Hilary Lawson, argues that it is we who are responsible for the objects that we take to be reality. From the firing of neurons in our sensory response to the world to our theories of the cosmos, our minds close and hold the unfathomable openness of the world, forming rather than discovering a world of things. Though constrained by reality, these closures are not descriptions of reality but are powerful tools for acting within it. Providing a new way to make sense of science, knowledge and the unknown.*

We see the world as divided into objects and things. People and houses, tables and chairs, trees and mountains, planets and stars. And we assume our theories and accounts of the world are true to the extent that they correctly identify these objects, their characteristics and their relations with other things. At first sight it does not seem plausible that it could be otherwise. How could our interventions in the world be so successful if they were not accurately describing reality? How could science work if it did not uncover the essence of the world?

\_\_\_

We see the world as divided into things, characteristics, and relationships not because that is how it is but because this is the structure of human thought.

\_\_\_

But this is a mistake, as the work of an increasing number of leading neuroscientists testifies. Our theories and accounts of the world do not name or describe the underlying stuff of reality. The world is not a list of things, no matter how complex a list, no matter how refined its details. Nor are we gradually accruing knowledge of reality as if our theories have, once and for all, identified the truth about reality, its objects and their relationships.

[We see the world as divided into things](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-not-made-up-of-objects-auid-3373), characteristics, and relationships not because that is how it is but because this is the structure of human thought. It is the way we are able to intervene successfully in the world through thought and language. Dividing the world into bits is the way we make sense of it, the way we refine and improve our understanding, the way we are able to effectively institute change. It is not however a description of reality.

It is hard to catch sight of this profound mistake precisely because all thought and language require the application of identities, require us to hold the world as if it consisted of things. If we question the idea that reality consists of things, wherever we look we appear to find evidence to the contrary. So, to many, it seems impossible that it could be otherwise.

In an attempt to cast light on this deep philosophical puzzle, we need to address two key questions: how are the particulars, the identities we find in the world, formed? And how is it that they are successful if they flow from the way we think rather than uncovering the truth about the world? In seeking to provide answers to these questions I have sought to uncover the underlying process which drives the differentiation of our world. For it turns out that we can identify a single process that underlies not only thought and language, but sensation and perception as well. It is the means by which we are able to hold the world as anything at all. It is the process of closure.

\_\_\_

This process of holding as one is the process of closure. Closure can be defined as “the holding of that which is different as one and the same.”

\_\_\_

When we hold a part of the world as a thing, say a leaf, we hold a combination of different things and characteristics as one thing. We hold the tip and edge of the leaf, its central vein and its offshoots, the blade of the leaf itself and the stalk, all as one thing, the leaf. In doing so we also hold it as one with other things we have previously identified as a leaf. This process of holding as one is the process of closure. Closure can be defined as “the holding of that which is different as one and the same.” We often assume that the overall identity is just a combination of the different parts, but it is something in addition to the parts.

[Human experience can be seen to be made up of layers of closure.](https://iai.tv/video/hilary-lawson-on-reality-and-the-illusion-of-truth) The most elementary closures are those at our sensory interface with the world. Take any one of the 120 million photoreceptor neurons in the retina of the eye. Each neuron responds to the entirety of the world in the same specific way. It either fires or it doesn’t. There is no halfway house, no partial firing. It is all or nothing. The firing of the neuron is an elementary example of the process of closure. For it holds all of the difference of the world as one thing: the firing of the neuron. And that one thing is not present previously; it is realised as a response to the world.

The closure realised in this instance, the firing of the neuron, is self-evidently not a description of the world. It is a response to the world. A sequence of higher-level sensory closure subsequently enables us to hold groups of neurons firing as, say, a patch of blue, or an edge. These higher-level sensory closures are no more a description of the world than the firing of the neurons. They are instead a new and additional closure that holds a very large number of different neuronal firings as one thing through the sensation blue. The patch of blue is no more out in the world than the initial preliminary closure of a single neuron firing. It is not a description of a reality. It is a way of holding reality which in combination with other sensory closures provides an initial basis for our intervention in the world. There could be, and are, other ways of forming sensory closure. Across the animal kingdom we have many radically different ways of responding to the openness of the world, and in principle there is no limit to the ways in which an organism might close the world. The process of closure is not therefore specific to humans but applies to all organisms that are able to intervene in the world to achieve outcomes that they desire.

\_\_\_

At no point do the closures become descriptions of the world rather than responses to the world, and at no point are these responses to the world the same thing as reality itself. There are no leaves out there in reality, any more than there are patches of green, or neuronal firings.

\_\_\_

Closure does not end with sensation. Thought and language are further levels of closure which once again find identity through holding that which is different as one and the same. Through thought we hold the closures of one sense as the same as the closures of another sense. In doing so we do not simply combine different elements, but realise a new closure, a thought. The sensation we get from vision and the green colour and outline of a leaf has nothing in common with the sensation we get from touching the leaf. We hold them as one and the same through the provision of a new closure and the realisation of new identity. At no point do the closures become descriptions of the world rather than responses to the world, and at no point are these responses to the world the same thing as reality itself. There are no leaves out there in reality, any more than there are patches of green, or neuronal firings.

You might initially find this hard to accept, precisely because the assumption that the way we close the world is a description of reality is embedded in the very way that we think. But let me give you an example which might help to demonstrate how the plasticity of our closures does not require truth and reality to make them effective.

When we look up at the stars in a cloudless night sky, we can identify many patterns and shapes. We call them constellations. In most Western countries we identify similar constellations. Most of us know a few: Orion and the Plough (known in the US as the Big Dipper); some will also recognise others such as Cassiopeia, Hercules, Leo. But we could have divided up the stars differently, and different cultures do see them differently.

Across the world there are many radically [different ways of holding the patterns in the stars](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/1js9AnuZYWQ), not just with different names but creating different shapes altogether. In the West we have 88 major constellations with an average of 6 stars each. The Chinese have many more constellations, 318with an average of 4.5 stars. Arabic constellations are many fewer, 44 with an average of 11 stars, while the Tupi, an indigenous population in Brazil, have just 7constellations with an average of 21.5 stars in each. These different constellations are not just different names for the same shapes; they are entirely different patterns.

So we can’t imagine, can we, that any one way of holding the stars is the true correct version? There are in effect a limitless number of ways we can hold the stars in the night sky. Even in 100 stars there are more patterns that can be identified than there are particles in the known universe. Yet each one of those limitless number of ways of patterning the night sky has value. Once a pattern is identified, through the process of closure, be it a square or a house or an arrow, it can be used to point to other stars. “Look at the star beyond the point of the arrow.” And if we pattern the whole sky we can follow the shapes and use it to navigate across the oceans. For millennia this was indeed how humans explored their world.

\_\_\_

Our accounts of the world can be extraordinarily powerful even when it is apparent they cannot be a description of reality. We don’t need our theories to be true for them to be useful.

\_\_\_

We can’t suppose that the effectiveness of the many different ways of holding the stars is achieved because each has uncovered the essential character of reality, and describes objects that are found in reality. Instead, they hold the night sky in an indefinite number of ways each of which has the potential to be effective if refined and developed. So it is with every particular we realise through the process of closure. What we take to be reality is one of countless possible combinations of particulars, or constellations, realised through the process of closure, through which we pattern the openness of the world.

What this example demonstrates is that the so-called ‘No miracles’ argument, that we need truth and reality to explain the effectiveness of science, is based on a fundamental mistake.  Our accounts of the world can be extraordinarily powerful even when it is apparent they cannot be a description of reality. We don’t need our theories to be true for them to be useful. This is why the Aristotelian-Ptolomeic universe with the earth at the centre and the stars carried on giant celestial spheres, which we of course now regard as wildly absurd, was nevertheless capable of precisely predicting the positions of the stars, and planets, enabling sailors to navigate the globe without error. So accurately in fact did it do this that when Copernicus proposed the contemporary version, some two thousand years later, with the sun at the centre, he did not think his theory would be any more accurate because he assumed the Aristotelian account was totally precise.

[Closure theory](https://www.hilarylawson.com/closure/) demonstrates that we do not need reality to explain the effectiveness of our theories and descriptions of the world. And it shows how through the revision of the closures of language and thought, in the light of their ability to enable us to intervene in the world or their failure to do so, we are able to create seemingly ever more precise accounts of the universe. It offers us a means to improve, refine, and choose between these accounts.

Closure theory is therefore a description of the workings of human perception, but it is also a description of how through thought and language we structure reality. At the same time, it provides an account of how closure theory is itself possible. For closure theory does not propose that the theory itself has uncovered the essential character of reality. Like all our attempts to make sense of our circumstances, it is a way of holding the world. There are and will of course be others. But for the time being, it offers a means to make sense of the multitude of competing and often incompatible perspectives that typify contemporary culture. It also provides an overall philosophical framework to make sense of Stephen Hawking’s model theoretic realism, and the claims of leading neuroscientists that reality is a fiction created by the brain. Above all perhaps, it is a means to improve and extend our current scientific theories while at the same time identifying the essential mystery of what it is to be alive.
