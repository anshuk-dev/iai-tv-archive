# The self would not exist without sleep

*Like the rests in music, sleep gives the self its shape*

**By Grace Theodoly** | *30th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,725 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![The self would not exist without sleep](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-self-would-not-exist-without-sleep.webp)

---

*We think of sleep as an interruption of the self. It is a gap in the story of our lives that philosophers from Descartes to Hume have tried to explain away, and that hustle culture would happily abolish. However, philosopher **Grace Theodoly** argues that this assumes waking consciousness is the baseline and sleep a departure from it. Theodoly argues sleep is what lets us have a coherent story of who we are. Our culture’s drive to eliminate sleep would therefore undermine the very self it hopes to enrich.*

On the morning of his great battle against Darius, Alexander the Great overslept. He slept, Montaigne tells us, “so profoundly and so long” that his general Parmenio had to come to his bedside and call him several times by name. He was not the only one. Augustus was fast asleep when the moment came to give the signal for a naval battle. Marius did give his signal, but then lay down under a tree and slept through the flight of his own army.

Montaigne collects these stories in a short essay called “Of Sleep.” He finds it fascinating that even men of the “highest enterprises and most important affairs” will not break their sleep. There is something almost absurd about the spectacle. Great battles are about to begin, and the men charged with fighting them are fast asleep. Montaigne, however, leaves further questioning to scientists, observing that “the physicians may determine whether sleep be so necessary that our lives depend upon it.”

That seemed satisfactory enough for Montaigne. But should it be? Science explains that sleep is necessary for biological mechanisms like synaptic pruning and metabolic clearance, and that it serves the broader purpose of restoration. These explanations address physiological and teleological questions about sleep, but they do not address the inherent strangeness of the arrangement. That restoring ourselves should require the total extinction of subjective awareness on a daily basis, amounting to a third of our lives, is puzzling. Our bodies do plenty of maintenance while we are awake; the lights are on when we digest or fight infections. Why shouldn’t all of it work that way? This peculiarity raises a third kind of question, an ontological question, which inquires into the essential nature of sleep and what it means for the self. It asks why sustaining conscious experience should require the periodic suspension sleep demands, and whether that suspension sits in tension with the way in which we understand ourselves as selves.

It is this question that I am concerned with here, and it is almost as difficult to pin down as it is to answer. The question eludes us because our understanding of sleep is so thoroughly mediated by its restorative function that we tend to overlook what is strange about the state itself. The Merriam-Webster Dictionary defines sleep as the periodic “loss of consciousness of one’s surroundings” that is “usually considered essential to the restoration and recovery of vital bodily and mental functions.” What is striking about this definition is that sleep is understood through its contribution to waking life, rather than as a state of existence in its own right.

Nowhere is this instrumental view of sleep more apparent than in our contemporary obsession with productivity. Traditional hustle culture treats sleep as an obstacle to be overcome by sheer willpower. This is captured in mantras like “sleep is for the weak” or “I’ll sleep when I’m dead”. While these expressions are easy to dismiss as hyperbole, they are nonetheless an outgrowth of a modern ethos that prizes pushing our bodies to their limits for the sake of “the grind”. It is perhaps fitting that Thomas Edison, whose inventions ushered in modernity, stated in an 1889 *Scientific American* interview that he rarely slept more than four hours a night and dismissed sleep as “a heritage from our cave days.”

Today, sleep is increasingly treated as an investment in waking performance, valuable insofar as it makes us more productive. This attitude is particularly apparent in the growing culture of biohacking, which seeks to optimize bodily functions in pursuit of peak performance. Fatigue is seen as an operational constraint that should be worked around. An entire industry has emerged around the promise of optimizing sleep for greater productivity. This includes health tech that tracks our sleep as well as companies that specialize in fatigue analytics and sell fatigue risk management systems.

The overarching trend is that modern culture has denied that sleep has intrinsic meaning or existential value. It is valued purely for what it yields when you wake up. Yet, this framing should not be dismissed entirely as a pathology of modern culture. The instrumental view of sleep exposes a deeper and more ubiquitous assumption that waking consciousness is the primary state of our existence, and sleep merely an interruption of it. We tend, after all, to think of ourselves as the authors of our own lives; our identities are constituted, at least in part, by what we experience, choose and do, and held together across time as a single story. If the self is principally understood through its conscious activity, sleep does more than interrupt our ability to exercise agency; it breaks the thread of the very story through which our identities are constituted.

We thus arrive at a fundamental tension between our conception of the self and the demands of sleep. On the one hand, sleep sustains the self: without it, the waking consciousness through which we experience, choose, and act degrades and eventually fails. On the other hand, sleep interrupts the self: it presses pause on consciousness, and with it the thread of experience out of which our identities are spun. Paradoxically, then, the process that is supposed to sustain our conscious experience requires us to suspend that very experience. And to make matters worse, this suspension of conscious experience seems to undermine something fundamental to our conception of what it means to exist as a self at all. Because this arrangement seems so remarkably strange, it becomes increasingly difficult to imagine why our existence should take this form at all.

This difficulty is not unfamiliar to philosophy. The trouble posed by any interruption of consciousness to a tidy theory of the self has occupied philosophers for centuries. Even so, when philosophers have turned their attention to sleep, their concern has typically been the nature of the self, and sleep has mattered insofar as it bears on that question. Sleep has thus tended to enter philosophical discussion as a test case for theories of personal identity, rather than as a phenomenon to be explained in its own right. Rather than asking what the periodic interruption of consciousness means for the self that goes to sleep, philosophers have generally asked how that self can persist through the interruption. That said, the very form these traditional approaches have taken is telling, and it is worth briefly surveying a few of them.

\_\_\_

The continuity of personal identity is something we attribute to ourselves, rather than something that actually exists.

\_\_\_

Descartes, Locke, and Hume each confronted the tension between the interruption of consciousness and the persistence of the self, but arrived at strikingly different conclusions. Sleep posed a problem for Descartes’s famous cogito because if thinking is indeed essential to our being anything at all, we must then cease to exist the moment we shut our eyes. To resolve this worry, Descartes adopted the view that we always think, even in the deepest sleep, and merely forget by morning. While one might willingly concede that we sometimes think while asleep, as in dreams, the notion that there are no stretches of thinkingless (or dreamless) sleep is harder to swallow. Locke evidently shared this skepticism, [remarking that](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/locke1690book2.pdf) “every drowsy nod shakes their doctrine, who teach that the soul is always thinking.”

Locke, for his part, granted that in sleep we have no thoughts at all; thus, that consciousness is interrupted each night. Yet, this posed a problem for his theory that personal identity consists in the identity of consciousness. Since sleep interrupts consciousness, “we los[e] the sight of our past selves, [and] doubts are raised whether we are the same thinking thing.” To rescue the continuity of personal identity from such interruptions, Locke appeals to memory, which connects our present consciousness to our past experiences.

Finally, unlike Descartes and Locke, Hume bites the bullet. “When my perceptions are remov’d for any time, as by sound sleep,” [he writes](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6), “so long am I insensible of myself, and may truly be said not to exist.” For Hume, there is no permanent, underlying self that persists independently of conscious experience. What we call the self is merely a bundle of successive perceptions, held together by memory and the imagination. The continuity of personal identity is something we attribute to ourselves, rather than something that actually exists. When consciousness ceases, as it does in dreamless sleep, there is simply no self to speak of. The apparent continuity between our waking selves is, on Hume’s account, a fiction constructed by the imagination.

What unites these three accounts is not the belief that consciousness must be uninterrupted, but the assumption that its interruption presents a problem for personal identity. In each case, the apparent discontinuity of consciousness demands an explanation. For Descartes, the self must remain secretly awake; for Locke, it must be stitched together across the gap by memory; for Hume, its apparent continuity must be dismissed as a fiction. All three, in other words, approach sleep as something that threatens the continuity of the self.

I propose that resolving the ontological question will depend on questioning the assumption that these philosophers’ accounts depend on. If we begin from the premise that sleep is an unwelcome interruption, a problem that any theory of the self must patch up, the ontological question remains insoluble. We will forever view the extinction of awareness as a crude compromise. Fortunately, the assumption can be challenged on two fronts: first, with respect to which state is primary, and second, with respect to what the continuity of the self actually consists in.

To begin with, to see sleep, or any lapse in consciousness, as an interruption is already to take a side. It assumes waking consciousness is the baseline and sleep the departure from it. Yet, the ordering could just as well run the other way. Sleep-like states appear in organisms without centralized brains, let alone anything resembling human consciousness. The upside-down jellyfish Cassiopea and the tiny freshwater Hydra have only diffuse networks of nerves, and yet both satisfy the behavioral criteria biologists use to identify sleep. Whatever one thinks about awareness in such creatures, they have nothing like [the waking self that sleep is supposed to interrupt](https://iai.tv/articles/predicting-your-self-auid-1608). Measured by time, consciousness is the exception, not the rule. As Nabokov writes in the haunting opening line of [his memoir](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/119454/speak-memory-by-vladimir-nabokov-introduction-by-brian-boyd/), “the cradle rocks above an abyss, and common sense tells us that our existence is but a brief crack of light between two eternities of darkness.” Perhaps, then, consciousness could just as easily be not the baseline interrupted by sleep, but the rare interruption of sleep. From this perspective, it would seem that the familiar framing, with consciousness as the natural state and sleep as its suspension, need not be accepted off the bat.

But questioning which state interrupts which only gets us so far. Even if we grant that sleep is more fundamental than waking consciousness, we have not yet resolved the problem it poses for personal identity. Descartes, Locke and Hume all take the interruption of consciousness to be something that requires explanation in relation to the self. Each offers a different response to the apparent tension between the two, but none questions whether the tension itself might be misplaced.

\_\_\_

Being the author of one’s life requires more than the infinite accumulation of experience. It requires the capacity to organize experience into a coherent narrative.

\_\_\_

What if we reversed the direction of explanation? Rather than asking how the self can persist despite the periodic interruption of consciousness, we might ask whether those very interruptions are in fact necessary to make [the continuity of the self](https://iai.tv/video/the-self-and-the-world) possible in the first place. On the conventional picture, something is continuous if it runs on without a gap, as a line or a stream does. There is, however, another way of understanding continuity, and it is the one we already use when we speak of the continuity of a melody or a story. A story is continuous not because it is told without pause, but because later episodes take up earlier ones and the whole hangs together as one. Its continuity lies in how its parts relate, not in whether anything comes between them.

Compare a melody. It consists of nothing but notes, yet what makes it the same melody from start to finish is the relations among the notes, not the absence of silence between them. It would be wrong to say that the rests interrupt the melody. On the contrary, they are necessary to it. Indeed, if a composer wrote a piece without pauses, sustained endlessly without breath or measure, the listener would not experience hyper-music; they would just experience noise. In other words, it seems that we actually need rests to hear the notes.

Similarly, being the author of one’s life requires more than the infinite accumulation of experience. It requires the capacity to organize experience into a coherent narrative. Without structure, more experience simply becomes noise. This offers an explanation for why the gaps in experience pose no threat to the self. It also begins to explain why the self might need them. Pure experience is undifferentiated; the stream of experience does not divide itself. As such, if structure depends on division, something must do the dividing. In this way, rather than being an interruption of the self, sleep may instead be a foundational, organizing principle through which the narrative self becomes coherent. It is perhaps a telling quirk of language that we use the very same word, rest, for the silences that structure music and for our nightly collapse into unconsciousness.

Returning to the ontological question with which we began, we can ask once again: Why must a being whose existence consists in conscious self-authorship periodically cease to be conscious at all? As bodies, we persist by continuing. A body is maintained the way a machine is kept running, through repairs carried out while it operates, and it needs no interruption to remain itself. Digestion, for example, is a material process that can go on while the engine remains running. As selves, however, we persist differently. [The self is a narrative structure](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-exists-and-it-is-an-illusion-auid-3347), and consciousness is an active holding-together of perspectives, experiences, and memories. Under the account I have proposed, sleep can thus be conceived of as a constitutive condition of narrative selfhood. Consciousness provides the raw material (experience, sensation, action), and sleep provides the structure that [shapes that raw material into a coherent whole](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-exists-and-it-is-an-illusion-auid-3347). Indeed, if consciousness were to tick over indefinitely, never going out, merely running at a low background level while repair took place, the stream of experience would remain an unpunctuated wall of noise.

This also tells us what is wrong with the instrumental picture of sleep. Productivity culture treats sleep as an investment in waking performance, a block of lost time to be compressed in the service of the life one is trying to build. On this view, sleep is a mere tax on productivity, which we would readily eliminate if only we had the power to. But if sleep is a constitutive condition of narrative selfhood, that ideal is not merely biologically improbable but incoherent. In seeking to maximize the time available for living, we would eliminate one of the very conditions that makes a life intelligible as a coherent whole. The pursuit of a more productive, fuller life would thus become self-defeating because it would undermine the coherence of the very self it seeks to enrich.

In this way, we have arrived at a picture of sleep for which the total extinction of subjective awareness is not a crude biological tax on physical maintenance, nor is it a dangerous gap that threatens our identity. Instead, it can be seen as the necessary condition for narrative selfhood. Consciousness must go out because a self cannot be structured while it is being actively authored. It must periodically surrender its agency so that the [raw material of experience](https://iai.tv/articles/dreams-show-us-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3654) can settle into a coherent story.
