# Quantum realism is impossible

*Abandoning the dream of an observer-free physics*

**By Paul Davies** | *2nd December 2025* | *Read time: approx. 16 mins* | *3,119 words*

![Quantum realism is impossible](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/quantum-realism.webp)

---

*Quantum mechanics started out by saying access to fundamental reality was impossible. And from the outset, some imagined that quantum mechanics would eventually eliminate the importance of human measurement in determining reality. Yet a century on, the measurement problem still refuses to disappear, and the new generation of quantum technologies, from quantum computers to quantum sensors, only makes it harder to ignore. As theory becomes engineering, **Paul Davies** argues that every attempt to “save” traditional views of realism fail, and that physics can no longer avoid confronting the role of observation in the structure of reality itself.*

**Omari Edwards: Quantum mechanics is a century old this year, and you have been working in the field your whole career. Why did it feel like the right moment now to write a book explicitly about quantum mechanics and call it [Quantum 2.0?](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/457920/quantum-20-by-davies-paul/9780241655801)**

Paul Davies: I’ve worked on quantum mechanics my entire career, but I’d never actually sat down and written a book about it. This year marks one hundred years since the birth of quantum mechanics, and that seemed like a good enough reason in itself. But on top of that, over the last few years it has become very clear that the entire field is about to undergo a stupendous transformation.

\_\_\_

Quantum mechanics does not uncover facts that were already there. Measurement brings reality into being.

\_\_\_

The transition from classical physics to quantum mechanics a century ago was a dramatic change in how we understood the world, and it reshaped the technologies that followed. Now we are on the cusp of what I call “Quantum 2.0”. It promises to be just as transformative, not only in our understanding of reality, but in the commercial and technological consequences that will flow from it.

**For a reader coming to this cold, what does quantum mechanics really change about our picture of reality, compared with the common-sense view of solid tables and chairs that exist whether we look at them or not?**

The thing that will probably surprise people most is what quantum physics tells us about the nature of reality. We tend to think that objects like tables and chairs really exist out there in the world, whether anyone looks at them or not. But if you keep zooming in, that picture dissolves. At the atomic level, solid objects turn into vibrating patterns of energy. Everything becomes fuzzy and uncertain.

The uncertainty is built into the laws of physics themselves. You can know everything there is to know about a system and still not be able to predict reliably what will happen next. Quantum mechanics does not tell you what will happen, it gives you the betting odds.

People often think this is no different from a coin toss or the weather, where we also deal in probabilities. But in those cases, the uncertainty is only due to ignorance. If you knew every detail about a tossed coin, you could work out which way it would land. Quantum uncertainty is different. It cannot be eliminated, no matter how much information you have. It is irreducible.

That means reality itself is not fully settled until something is observed. If you ask where an atom is and you measure it, you will find it in a definite place. But quantum mechanics says it was not already there waiting to be discovered. Before the measurement, the very idea of position has no meaning. The atom existed in a blurred state containing many possible outcomes at once, and the act of observation brings one of them into being. This is the Measurement Problem.

**You stress that quantum mechanics only ever gives us betting odds, even when we know everything about a system. What is the crucial difference between that kind of intrinsic uncertainty and the uncertainty in something like a coin toss or the weather?**

Measurement is not just another physical interaction in quantum mechanics. That is the heart of the problem. You can have complete knowledge of a system and still not be able to say what will happen next, only what might happen, with certain probabilities. But when you perform a measurement, something very special occurs: one concrete outcome appears out of many possible ones.

If you observe where an atom is, you find it in a specific position. But quantum mechanics says that before the measurement, the atom was not secretly in one place or another. It had no definite position at all. The act of measurement does not uncover a hidden fact. It produces a fact.

\_\_\_

Quantum mechanics works astonishingly well for atoms, but we have no right to assume it applies unchanged to the entire universe.

\_\_\_

You can think of the quantum state as an amalgam of all possible outcomes blended together. The system is not in one state or another, but in a mixture of possibilities at the same time. Measurement somehow takes this blurred collection of alternatives and projects a single reality out of it. What we do not understand is why or how that happens.

We have a name for this impossibility, but not an explanation. It is called the measurement problem. The problem is not mathematical, because the equations work perfectly well. The problem is that quantum mechanics gives no account of what makes a measurement a measurement. What is it about coupling an atom to a piece of apparatus that causes a single reality to emerge from a cloud of alternatives? We do not have a good answer.

**Physicists have been wrestling with this for decades. How did Niels Bohr and the so-called Copenhagen interpretation try to let quantum mechanics carry on working while postponing the question of what is really going on?**

One very well-known way of dealing with the measurement problem is the many-worlds interpretation. The idea is that when a quantum system exists in a mixture of possibilities, all of those possibilities are real. When you make a measurement, rather than one outcome being selected, the universe effectively splits. One branch contains one result; another branch contains another.

If you take the simple example of a coin toss at the quantum level, instead of saying the system ends up as either heads or tails, you say both happen. The universe divides into two versions: one in which the coin is heads, and one in which it is tails. This way of thinking is often illustrated by Schrödinger’s cat, where the cat is said to be both alive and dead until an observation is made. In the many worlds view, both outcomes exist in parallel realities.

The appeal of this interpretation is that you do not have to add anything to quantum mechanics itself. You do not need [a mysterious “collapse” of the wave-function](http://iai.tv/articles/the-many-answers-to-the-quantum-measurement-problem-auid-2871). You simply accept the mathematics literally and treat every outcome as equally real. Ontologically, the theory is very clean. Nothing special happens at measurement.

But the cost is enormous. If you take the idea seriously, the universe does not just split once or twice. It branches endlessly, at every moment, for every microscopic quantum event. That means you are committed to an infinite number of parallel worlds.

Some people find that an elegant solution. Others find it deeply implausible. To explain what happens when we measure an atom by postulating an infinite number of unseen universes seems to me a very high price to pay.

**Another route, championed today by people like Sabine Hossenfelder, is superdeterminism, where everything, including our choices of what to measure, is fixed from the beginning of time. Does that really solve the measurement problem, in your view, or does it come at too high a price?**

[Superdeterminism is an attempt to remove the mystery](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2024/03/superdeterminism-quantum-mechanics-demystified-or-the-end-of-science.html) by saying that nothing is really uncertain at all. According to this view, it is not only the behaviour of particles that is fixed in advance, but also our choices of what to measure. Even when you think you are freely deciding whether to measure position or momentum, that decision was supposedly determined from the very beginning of the universe.

In that picture, there is no genuine randomness and no genuine freedom. Every outcome and every choice is part of a vast cosmic conspiracy in which everything has been nailed down in advance. The appearance of uncertainty is an illusion created by our ignorance of the underlying hidden variables that govern everything.

I don’t like that idea at all. It saves determinism at the expense of something central to our experience, which is agency and choice. It turns science into a story in which neither the experimenter nor the experiment could ever have been otherwise. That strikes me as too great a price to pay just to avoid facing the strangeness of quantum mechanics.

I’m unhappy with both many worlds and superdeterminism for the same reason. They both try to explain away something I regard as one of the most important features of our experience, which is consciousness.

When physicists talk about “observers”, they rarely mean literal human beings every time a measurement is made. Most experiments today are automated. But that doesn’t mean consciousness has disappeared from the story. There is what I call a “ghost in the design”. Measuring devices do not appear on their own. They are built by people, for particular purposes, to measure particular things. Whether the human being is present when the measurement happens or whether it runs overnight makes no real difference. Agency has already entered the picture in the very construction of the experiment.

That seems to me an opportunity rather than a nuisance. Quantum mechanics, if it claims to describe reality completely, must eventually have something to say about consciousness. But both many worlds and superdeterminism avoid that confrontation. They effectively define consciousness out of the picture, and I think that is a huge missed opportunity.

I am also uneasy about the idea that quantum mechanics must apply without exception to the entire universe, from electrons all the way up to cosmology. It works astonishingly well for atoms and small systems, but that does not mean it must work at every scale and level of complexity. It might be correct, but I don’t think we have any right to assume it.

\_\_\_

The fragility of quantum systems, once a problem for physicists, has become one of the most powerful resources in modern technology.

\_\_\_

That is why I’m open to the possibility that quantum mechanics breaks down somewhere, not at a particular size or mass, but at a certain level of complexity. Somewhere between an atom and a cat, or perhaps in living systems, the rules may change. That does not mean we know what replaces quantum mechanics, but it leaves open the possibility of some future physics that bridges the quantum world and the classical world in a deeper way.

At the moment, all our experiments still obey the quantum rules. We can carry out quantum experiments on increasingly large molecules and complex systems, and quantum mechanics still seems to work. But we have not gone very far along that road. The decisive experiments have not yet been done.

**If neither many worlds nor superdeterminism satisfies you, where does that leave you? What, in your view, is the most plausible alternative?**

What happens when nobody is watching depends entirely on your interpretation of quantum mechanics. If you are a many-worlds person, then it makes no difference at all whether anyone is observing or not. Splitting into different branches is supposed to be happening all the time, whether there are people around or not.

On my own view, the question becomes much more interesting. I do not think the collapse of a quantum system requires a human being to be present, but I also do not think the system simply remains in a superposition forever. Somewhere along the line, something happens.

I think the key idea is not size but complexity. A cat is certainly complex enough. A bacterium probably is. But for molecules, it is not obvious where the line should be drawn. What matters is not the object itself, but the complexity of the process the system is part of. At some point, when a quantum system becomes coupled to a sufficiently complex environment, the transition from many possibilities to one outcome occurs.

We already know that quantum systems become entangled with their surroundings all the time. That muddles the system up with the wider world, but it does not, by itself, select a single outcome. It still leaves everything as one large quantum blur. That is where people often get confused. Entanglement does not solve the problem of measurement.

What I am suggesting, speculatively, is that when a system reaches a certain threshold of complexity, a collapse really does occur. One outcome becomes real. There is randomness in that process because quantum mechanics builds randomness in at the most fundamental level. But I do not have a finished theory to offer. I can only say that if we ever see quantum mechanics fail, it is likely to be when we test it on complex systems, possibly living ones.

Physicists like Schrödinger already suspected this. When he asked, “What is life?” he was not just being poetic. He thought [biology would force us beyond quantum mechanics to some new type of physical law](http://iai.tv/video/johnjoe-mcfadden-life-in-the-quantum-universe). I think he may well have been right, but that is going to be work for the next generation.

**You argue that information has become a physical quantity, not just an abstract idea. In what sense is information now treated in physics like energy, and how does that change the way we think about physical laws?**

What has changed in fundamental physics is that information is no longer just an abstract concept. It has become a physical quantity in its own right, closely tied to the laws of thermodynamics. Information now enters our equations in much the same way as energy or temperature.

In everyday life, information simply means a reduction in uncertainty. If I know that a train runs once an hour, I still do not know exactly when it will leave. If I discover it departs at 12:48, my uncertainty has been reduced. That reduction is information. But in physics, information has taken on a deeper status. It is not just something we calculate with. It is something that can have physical effects.

A striking illustration of that is an old thought experiment from the nineteenth century known as [Maxwell’s demon](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-maxwells-demon-continues-to-startle-scientists-20210422/). Imagine a box of gas divided into two halves by a partition with a small door. A tiny hypothetical creature opens and closes that door depending on whether fast or slow molecules approach. Over time, it sorts the gas, letting fast molecules collect on one side and slow ones on the other, creating a temperature difference between the two halves of the box.

That temperature difference can then be used to run a heat engine and perform physical work. So, in a very literal sense, information about which molecules are fast and which are slow is being converted into energy. Information is acting as fuel.

This is why information now plays such a central role in modern physics. When we move into the quantum domain and start working with qubits rather than bits, this becomes even more powerful, because we are no longer dealing with simple yes-or-no information, but far richer quantum states that obey their own physical laws. And it is these laws, still being worked out, that I think will eventually be written into new versions of physics itself.

**Has quantum technology made it impossible to treat the oddities of quantum mechanics as mere mathematical tricks?**

Yes, I think it has. For a long time, physicists could adopt what is sometimes called the ‘shut up and calculate’ attitude. Quantum mechanics worked extraordinarily well, so people were content to use it as an algorithm without worrying too much about what it meant. If all you wanted to do was build a better laser or design a new material, the weirdness could be quietly ignored.

Quantum 2.0 changes that. We are no longer just applying quantum rules in bulk systems, where trillions of particles average out into something that looks classical. We are now manipulating individual atoms, electrons and photons, and shaping their quantum states deliberately. The strangeness is no longer a side effect. It is the resource.

Technologies like quantum computing, cryptography and sensing work precisely because superposition, entanglement and quantum fragility are real features of the world, not just mathematical conveniences. A quantum computer does not succeed in spite of quantum weirdness. It succeeds because of it.

And that makes it much harder to pretend that quantum mechanics is merely a calculational tool. If the theory were just a clever set of mathematical tricks, it would be astonishing that engineers could build working machines by exploiting things like entanglement and fragility. What quantum technology shows is that the strange aspects of quantum mechanics are not optional extras. They are woven into the structure of reality itself.

**You suggest, in Quantum 2.0, that the old debate between a purely mechanical universe and a purposive, agent-filled one may itself be the wrong framing. What kind of new conceptual framework do you think quantum 2.0 might push us towards, and how might that change the way we talk about meaning and purpose in the universe?**

I’m uncomfortable with the word “purpose” because it is something human beings have, and when we project it onto the universe as a whole, it quickly becomes anthropocentric. I feel much more comfortable talking about agency.

Traditionally, there have been two ways of explaining the world. One begins with agency: why did it rain? Because a god or spirit intended it. The other is mechanism: the universe is a machine that runs according to fixed laws. But both ideas ultimately come from us. We are agents, and we build machines.

Why should we assume those are the only two ways of making sense of reality?

It may be that we need an entirely new conceptual framework. Not just agency, and not just mechanism, but something else altogether. Some third category, which we do not yet have a name for.

For centuries, we have gone back and forth between the same exhausted positions: does the universe have a mind, does it have a purpose, or is it just a blind machine? And we get nowhere. That usually means we are asking the question in the wrong way.

What we need, I think, is not just new physics but new categories of thought. Quantum 2.0 may well push us in that direction. It may force us to develop a new way of thinking that transforms how we talk about mind, purpose, and meaning altogether. In a hundred or a thousand years, the questions themselves may look completely different from how they look today.

Thank you.
