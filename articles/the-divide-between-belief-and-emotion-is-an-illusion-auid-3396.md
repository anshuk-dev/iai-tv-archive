# The divide between belief and emotion is an illusion

*Belief is an emotion*

**By Miriam Schleifer McCormick** | *21st October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,882 words*

![The divide between belief and emotion is an illusion](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-divide-between-belief-and-emotion-is-an-illusion-2.webp)

---

*We like to think belief and emotion belong in different boxes—one ruled by evidence and truth, the other by feelings and moods. But that divide is an illusion, argues philosopher Miriam Schleifer McCormick. We’d do better to think of beliefs as emotions, because believing involves feeling something to be the case, and the worth of a belief depends not just on the evidence for it but on whether it helps us live well. The ideal believer is not, as the AI industry might have us think, a flawless information-processing system, but a being whose reason breathes with emotion.*

Beliefs figure prominently in how we understand each other, are essential for navigating the world, and help define our characters and motivations. But what are beliefs? According to standard philosophical theories of belief, an ideal believer is a flawless information-processing system. Of course, we fail to live up to this ideal because of our human limitations. We get angry, despondent, dazzled, exuberant, grief-stricken, horny, hungry, lazy, sick, tired, wonderstruck. The standard view sees these human traits as *infecting* our belief-maintenance systems, sometimes to explain our *bad beliefs* and sometimes to show that what may look like a belief, say that God is a comforting presence, is not a belief at all because it is not even aiming at this ideal.

\_\_\_

When I am afraid, I feel that there is danger. Similarly, I contend, when I believe something, I feel that the way I am representing the world is accurate.

\_\_\_

In my book [*Belief as Emotion*](https://academic.oup.com/book/59039)(OUP, 2025) I offer an alternative picture of belief. I propose that we view belief as a type of emotion. Most emotion theorists view emotions as essentially blended states, as involving both feelings as well as some kind of cognitive representation. The elements that make up the blend are not separable mental states that are added together. When I am afraid, I *feel* that there is danger. Similarly, I contend, when I believe something, I *feel* that the way I am representing the world is accurate. Emotion is such a wide tent, encompassing everything from fear and anger to curiosity and contentment; I propose that “belief” be added to the list. Thinking of beliefs as emotions helps to explain several puzzling phenomena related to belief. Why, for example, do we continue to act on implicit biases even when we are aware of them? Why do many people claim to hold religious beliefs while not acting in accord with religious doctrine? Ideal emotional maintenance is complex; thinking of beliefs as emotions acknowledges and embraces this complexity in our lives as believers.

The “standard” philosophical view of belief, which emphasizes a special connection to evidence and action, is deeply problematic. It commits its proponents to holding that many states commonly understood as beliefs by psychologists and in everyday life are actually *not* beliefs after all because they do not conform to this narrow definition. Such “problematic states” include cases of political and religious convictions, the attitude of trust, delusions, attitudes involved in self-deception, and implicit attitudes. In all these cases, people either have beliefs that are misaligned with their actions, or recognize that the evidence does not support their beliefs, or both. Those who argue they are not beliefs often introduce unfamiliar mental states to account for such cases. Suggestions for what to call these problematic states include “[quasi-beliefs](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137026521_7),” “[in-between beliefs](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137026521_5),” “[aliefs](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-011-9801-7),” “[besires](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-012-0006-5),”and “[bimagination.](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5bbb78d577889768f32c38f5/t/5bc792af9140b756d663bfbd/1539805871464/bim.11.11.pdf)” Others argue they should be reclassified as imaginings or acceptances. The main defense of my view of belief as emotion is that it can do a much better job at addressing these “edge” cases, or what [Bayne and Hattiangadi (2013)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137026521_7) call “bedfellows of belief.”

\_\_\_

We should recognize that there can be times when a belief is a good one, even if it’s not based on evidence.

\_\_\_

It is widely held that the only genuine reasons for believing are evidential. In other words, reasons for a belief must relate to evidence that increases the likelihood of that belief being true. If my belief is based on a non-evidential reason (if such a thing is even possible), then, according to the standard view, it must be defective. Yet, many do not want to treat all these problematic states as being on par, and want some way to allow for the possibility that they are not all irrational. And so they argue that one can, for example, trust without believing, or have a religious conviction that is not a belief. If they are not beliefs, then they can be held to different standards.

Instead, I think we should recognize that there can be times when a belief is a good one, even if it’s not based on evidence. In assessing the rationality of emotions, evidence matters a lot but it is not the end of the story. An emotion can sometimes be valuable even when not based on evidence, and this can be a reason to have it. For example, suppose I am angry at my neighbor because I think he crashed into my boat on a foggy day. If the fog lifts to reveal no one is in the neighbor’s boat, my anger will disappear, and if it does not, then I can be rightfully criticized. My anger is not fitting in such circumstances, given that there is no agent who has wronged me and so is worthy of this response. Yet there can also be value-related reasons that render my anger subject to criticism. Even if it turns out that I was correct that my neighbor carelessly crashed into my boat, other considerations can support being critical of my anger. These may include, for example, that I have a disposition to become angry too quickly, which has been detrimental to me and my relationships. If I am trying to alter this disposition, then it can be said that I ought not to have become angry despite the evidence. If beliefs are emotions, then we can likewise distinguish between cases where a non-evidentially based belief is warranted and when it is not.

\_\_\_

The aims that are often assigned to “problematic states” like political and religious beliefs—aims such as social cohesion and agential flourishing—apply to all our beliefs.

\_\_\_

Those who argue that religious and political convictions differ from ordinary factual beliefs ([Van Leeuwen, 2023](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=bifcEAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PP1&ots=vHgfu0k6gC&sig=nATL3KApQkCIx9wtLlmylkdAZuk#v=onepage&q&f=false); [Ichino, 2024](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/belief-imagination-and-delusion-9780198872221?cc=us&lang=en&); [Hannon & de Ridder, 2021](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/153352036/2021_The_Point_of_Political_Belief.pdf)) claim that they differ in their aims and/or functions. Ordinary, factual beliefs, it is claimed, aim at truth; their function is to represent the world. In contrast, religious and political beliefs “often appear to serve different functions: bolster group loyalty, protect identities, or receive psychological gratification” ([Hannon & de Ridder 2021](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/153352036/2021_The_Point_of_Political_Belief.pdf)). I argue, as do others ([McKay & Dennett, 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/evolution-of-misbelief/F817ED84D1FFD8650D61FB61301203E8); [Elgin, 2008](https://www.catherineelgin.com/undg/emotion.pdf)), that the nature and function of belief is more complex than simple truth-aiming. Recognizing that these beliefs are not as sensitive to evidence, or not as connected to behavior as the standard view of belief predicts they should be, provides a reason to rethink the standard view of belief. The aims that are often assigned to “problematic states” like political and religious beliefs—aims such as social cohesion and agential flourishing—apply to *all* our beliefs. In a sense, these “borderlands” are where to look to understand what it means to believe. Any time you believe something, even about the most seemingly trivial matter, you are taking a stand; you are making an evaluation.

The view of belief as emotion can also help illuminate the phenomenon of deep disagreement. In particular, it provides a model for engaging with deeply entrenched but counter-evidential beliefs such as those held by some conspiracy theorists. Understanding beliefs as emotions illuminates why some beliefs are resilient despite contrary evidence and helps explain the mechanisms of belief-change. Thinking about how we engage with other problematic emotions can be helpful in thinking about how to engage with evidence-resistant beliefs that are clearly false. Put simply, evidence does not immediately affect feelings.

If you are very angry or sad and you are told not to be and given good reasons why you shouldn’t be, these reasons will not immediately lead to a change in your emotional state. But this does not mean there are not ways that I can engage with you so that you come to see your emotion is misplaced. The way to get people to be open to [changing their beliefs](https://iai.tv/articles/how-to-break-free-from-political-groupthink-auid-3099) is to find ways to make them more secure and less defensive, so that the presentation of facts does not feel like an assault on who they are. This is not to say that there is no possibility of discussing evidence. As [Scott Aikin and Robert Talisse (2021)](https://3quarksdaily.com/3quarksdaily/2021/01/deep-disagreement-and-the-qanon-conspiracy-theory.html) argue, evidence does matter to some degree; QAnon believers needed explanations when QAnon predictions failed to come true. In the face of evidence, some gave up their beliefs and others found their convictions weakened. But to even hope to have any kind of discussion of evidential reasons for such beliefs, it is important to understand the practical reasons that make them so attractive to many.

My view of belief as emotion does not undermine the importance of generally following evidential norms in forming and maintaining our beliefs. Our survival and flourishing depend on our having [mostly true beliefs](https://iai.tv/articles/in-a-post-truth-world-truth-seeking-is-more-important-than-ever-auid-3382); I view our system of epistemic norms and reasons as being grounded in this practical base. At the most general level, the overall function of each and every emotion is to help individuals or genes survive. This is certainly true of belief; our belief-system would not be well functioning if we had too many false beliefs. But it would also not be well functioning if we only retained beliefs for which we had incontrovertible evidence; we only need it to be that they are accurate enough. Further, a cognitive system that provided us with all the true beliefs that exist, including completely trivial ones, would not be well functioning

Why does it matter whether a state is classified as a “belief ” or whether it is appropriate to attribute a certain belief to someone? In presenting my view, I am often asked why I am so hung up on words. I think philosophical theories should help us illuminate and understand our practices, and if a view of belief ends up describing a mental state that bears little resemblance to belief as ordinarily understood, I see this as a strike against the theory.

Further, that a certain view of belief has become dominant in the last century or so is not an accident. The idea that proper believing privileges a certain way of gaining truth and knowledge, one that is uninfected by emotion, is articulated in the work [Audre Lorde](https://www.centraleurasia.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/audre_lorde_cool-beans.pdf). Lorde refers to the powerful source of knowledge that is grounded in feeling as “the erotic” and argues that to maintain a system of patriarchal oppression “we have been taught to suspect this resource, vilified, abused, and devalued within western society.” How we categorize beliefs and their norms has deep implications for how we see ourselves and each other. A half-century after Lorde’s writing, it may be that a shift in perspective concerning the value of emotion is beginning to take place. As artificial intelligence increases in its intellectual capacities, the import of humans’ emotional capacities is being recognized. Our ability to *feel*, rather than reason, may be what is special about us; it could be what allows for a kind of knowledge or understanding that transcends a purely intellectual grasp.
