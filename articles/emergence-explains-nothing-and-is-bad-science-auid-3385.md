# Emergence explains nothing and is bad science

*Life, consciousness and the cosmos are not “emergent”*

**By John Heil** | *13th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,914 words*

![Emergence explains nothing and is bad science](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/emergence-explains-nothing-and-is-bad-science.webp)

---

*Scientists and philosophers have fallen for a seductive buzzword: “emergence.” It’s invoked to explain life, consciousness, and the flow of time: when simple parts combine, it is claimed, they sometimes produce new entities with powers their parts could never predict. But philosopher John Heil calls this out as an intellectual sleight of hand. “Emergence,” he argues, doesn’t reveal hidden truths—it masks our ignorance, mistaking gaps in explanation for gaps in reality. It’s time to drop the magic word and face the real challenge: uncovering, in concrete detail, how simple parts can give rise to complex wholes.*

**1. The so-called “emergence” of life and particles**

[Emergence](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-emergence) was [much discussed](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Mind_and_its_Place_in_Nature.html?id=jLq3AwAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y#:~:text=Originally%20published%20in%201925%2C%20this,of%20Existents%20and%20the%20Unconscious.) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries when leading scientists and philosophers came to see that the physical sciences lacked the resources to explain a range of complex chemical and biological phenomena. Today, [much of the focus on emergence pertains to *consciousness*](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1004563122154), stemming from the apparent inability of the sciences as they stand to accommodate conscious experiences, coupled with a long-standing conviction that [a respectable physical account of consciousness is not on the cards](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-goedel-and-the-incompleteness-of-science-auid-3042).

I shall forebear discussion of consciousness here. The topic raises a host of questions of its own and would serve as a distraction. I shall focus, instead, on the mechanics of emergence. Before getting down to business, however, let me provide two concrete examples of appeals to emergence in ongoing scientific work.

One longstanding example of purported emergence is [life itself](https://iai.tv/articles/the-mystery-of-living-things-addy-pross-auid-2608). Living organisms, from single cells to human beings, are said to emerge from combinations of non-living ingredients in ways that cannot be predicted by or extracted from knowledge of the properties and laws governing the ingredients alone ([see Roselli under review](https://www.centerphilsci.pitt.edu/event/ltt-andrea-roselli/#:~:text=Title%3A%20Emergent%20beasts%20and%20where,way%20to%20finally%20catch%20them.)).

\_\_\_

Philosophers disagree on many things—on the nature of causation, for instance—but disagreements over emergence are more unsettling.

\_\_\_

A second putative example of emergence is much discussed in contemporary physics. This concerns the emergence of particles from a primitive microwave radiation thought to have been the product of the Big Bang. The particles posited in today’s Standard Model—quarks, leptons, bosons, and their offspring—appear to have arisen from such radiation, although the properties of the particles and laws governing them are not extractable from what we know about the state of the universe immediately following the Big Bang ([see Cléry et al. under review](https://philarchive.org/rec/CLRDTL)).

**2. What *is* emergence?**

Although the literature on emergence is vast, there is scant agreement on what it means to describe the *A*s as emerging from the *B*s. Philosophers disagree on many things—on the nature of causation, for instance—but [disagreements over emergence are more unsettling](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-006-9025-0). Few scientists or philosophers doubt that causation occurs. Apparently uncontroversial examples of causal processes are ready to hand, anchoring competing accounts of causation. In the case of emergence, however, competing accounts appear to be accounts of entirely different phenomena.

For many, particularly in the sciences, emergence concerns prediction and explanation. Emergence of this kind is characterized deductively: when the *A*s emerge from the *B*s, descriptions or explanations of *A*s cannot be extracted from, or predicted on the basis of, what is known about the *B*s.

\_\_\_

Genuinely emergent entities or properties would, it seems, be accidental additions to the cosmos.

\_\_\_

Emergentists embrace this “explanatory gap” and argue that, under the right conditions, entirely new entities come into being. When you put things together in the right way, the result is a new kind of entity with new kinds of property, something more than the sum of its parts duly organized. Although the terminology is unsettled, emergence delineated by reference to explanation is sometimes called “[weak emergence](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2216138).”

Weak emergence could be contrasted with “strong emergence,” a more robust metaphysical thesis. The *A*s strongly emerge from the *B*s only if . . . only if *what*? To count as emergent, the *A*s must be something altogether new, something *in addition to* aggregates of *B*s. If the *A*s are not prefigured in the nature of the *B*s, from the perspective of scientists studying the *B*s, the appearance of the *A*s would be *accidental*. This is the sense in which life and the particles of the Standard Model are held to be emergent.

If the *A*s are emergent properties, the *A*s might be thought to contribute “causal powers” to their possessors that outstrip those contributed by the *B*s alone. A tomato has the power to roll owing to its spherical shape. The tomato’s parts, however, need not be spherical, need not themselves have the power to roll. Yet it does not seem that the tomato’s shape is, in any interesting sense, an emergent property. The shape is just what you get when you put the tomato’s parts together in the right way. The tomato’s shape is what emergentists call a merely *resultant* property.

Critics of emergence argue that [every feature of a complex whole, such as a tomato or a living creature, is resultant](https://doi.org/10.11612/resphil.2302). Complex systems comprise many, many parts dynamically and interactively organized. In interacting, the parts can change one another, often in dramatic ways. When you have all this, say the sceptics, you thereby have something—a complex system—that does indeed exhibit new kinds of characteristics that endow the system with powers not possessed by its individual parts. But these characteristics are nothing “over and above” these parts with these properties interactively organized as they are.

In the case of simple, partless entities, such as quarks, leptons, and their cousins, emergence would be *dynamic*. Particles and their properties issue over time from a formless stuff. This might seem a better example of something worthy of being called emergent, but the sceptics have more to say. Whatever the state of the universe following the Big Bang, that state had whatever it takes to yield discrete particles. What occurred was in no sense accidental, any more than the decay of an unstable element—radium, for instance—is accidental. *When* the element decays might be indeterminate, but not the character of its decay into an atom of radon and an α-particle. Genuinely emergent entities or properties *would*, it seems, be accidental additions to the cosmos.

**3. “Downward causation”**

Maybe this is too hasty, however. Maybe the key to making sense of emergence rests on the possibility of what social psychologist [Donald T. Campbell (1916-1996) christened “downward causation](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-349-01892-5_11).” According to Campbell, downward causation occurs when “all processes at the lower levels of a hierarchy are restrained by and act in conformity to the laws of the higher levels.”

Campbell’s levels reflect the “hierarchical organization in biological systems. The organizational levels of *molecule*, *cell*, *tissue*, *organ*, *organism*, *breeding population*, *species*, and in some instances *social system*, and perhaps even *ecosystem*” (179). This conception of levels concerns parts and wholes: lower-level items are parts of higher-level wholes. Downward causation would occur when encompassing wholes exert causal influence over their parts: wholes made up of assemblages of parts [causally constrain the parts’ behavior](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1358246117000145).

Emergent wholes might be thought to exert what [Brian McLaughlin calls “configurational forces”](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110870084.49) on their parts. Wholes exerting such forces might plausibly be regarded as emergent. Downward configurational forces would supplement gravitational and electromagnetic forces and the weak and strong forces that govern individual particles.

Roger Sperry (1913–1994), an influential American neuropsychologist, puts it this way in the case of mental states:

Subjective mental phenomena are conceived to influence and govern the flow of nerve impulse traffic by virtue of their encompassing emergent properties. Individual nerve impulses and other excitatory components of a cerebral activity pattern are simply carried along or shunted this way or that by the prevailing overall dynamics of the whole active process (in principle—just as drops of water are carried along by a local eddy in a stream or the way the molecules or atoms of a wheel are carried along when it rolls down the hill, regardless of whether the individual molecules and atoms happen to like it or not). ([Sperry 1969: 534](https://www.informationphilosopher.com/solutions/scientists/sperry/Sperry_Modified_Consciousness.pdf))

Sperry’s focus is on consciousness, but, as his examples make clear, it is meant to cover emergent phenomena generally. In the case of consciousness, it promises to remove the mystery of what it would mean for consciousness to arise from or emerge in complex systems rightly organized. We cannot explain or predict how consciousness arises in various complex systems *because* consciousness is an emergent phenomenon, and an emergent phenomenon is one that (1) could not be predicted on the basis of knowledge of the parts of the whole in which it emerges and (2) exhibits “downward,” whole-to-part causal influence.

\_\_\_

When you have parts and their properties interactively organized into wholes, do you have, in addition, a whole, an entity furnished with distinctive properties of its own?

\_\_\_

Such optimistic appraisals depict downward causation as a ubiquitous, well-understood phenomenon. The powers or capacities of certain complex wholes exceed those of the parts taken individually. You have the particles with their various powers, and you have emergent powers, powers belonging to organized systems of parts, powers of wholes capable of affecting members of the collections making them up. Easy to say, not so easy to understand.

Suppose you distinguish (1) cases in which parts’ behavior is affected by their membership in dynamic, interactive collections from (2) cases in which a property of a collection, a property of the whole, “downwardly” affects the collection’s members. Atoms and molecules making up complex molecular systems can affect—even modify—one another in myriad ways. Particles in combination unquestionably constrain one another’s behavior. Interactions of this kind, however, are not instances of downward causation and not cases in which wholes—which after all *include* their parts—exercise causal influence over those parts.

When you have parts and their properties interactively organized into wholes, do you have, *in addition*, a whole, an entity furnished with distinctive properties of its own?

Jaegwon Kim (1934–2019) [puts it this way](https://philpapers.org/rec/KIMMSO-2) in addressing Sperry’s depiction of drops of water making up an eddy being “carried along” by the eddy:

An eddy is there because the individual water molecules constituting it are swirling around in a circular motion; in fact, an eddy is nothing but these water molecules engaged in this pattern of motion.

Were something like this right for ordinary objects, downward causation would drop out of the picture. Characteristics of wholes are not something in addition to interactive arrangements of their propertied parts. Parts of the whole causally interact with one another and with entities outside the whole, and the whole can undergo vast changes as a result of these interactions, but it would be misleading to imagine that the wholes themselves could somehow act on their parts.

These points can be difficult to appreciate because they are often presented in a way that dramatically under-describes the arrangements that constitute wholes. These arrangements are often presented as aggregates of atoms or molecules, the properties of which could not possibly add up to the wholes and their characteristics that the parts make up. The wholes at issue, however, far from being mere aggregates, are highly interactive dynamic affairs, the parts of which can undergo significant modifications in concert with other parts. The fact that the parts behave very differently in these arrangements than in isolation or in pairs does not license the idea that the arrangement exerts a downward “configurational force” on the parts, or that the wholes and their characteristics are emergent.

More needs to be said, but I hope that I have managed at least to cast a shadow of doubt on the credibility of the phenomenon of emergence.
