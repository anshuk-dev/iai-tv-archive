# Pinker vs Nietzsche: Is music the basis of language?

*Language is born out of music*

**By Kathleen Higgins** | *4th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,989 words*

![Pinker vs Nietzsche: Is music the basis of language?](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Pinker-vs-Nietzsche-is-music-the-basis-of-language.webp)

---

*According to psychologists like Steven Pinker, music is a peripheral part of our humanity. If music vanished overnight, Pinker argues, "the rest of our lifestyle would be virtually unchanged." For Nietzsche, this is a radical mistake. In this article, Kathleen Higgins presents Nietzsche's argument for music being the foundation of language, without which our lives would not be recognizably human at all. Far from being peripheral, music is essential to our humanity. As Nietzsche wrote late in life: "Without music, life would be a mistake."*

Music is a pervasive presence, not only marking special occasions like birthdays and weddings, but also serving as background for daily activities like driving, exercising, or watching a show. So prevalent is music in our ordinary routines that it is hard to imagine what our lives would be like without it.

But cognitive psychologist [Steven Pinker considers music more or less dispensable](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/how-mind-works-19972009). “Compared with language, vision, social reasoning, and physical know-how,” he remarks, “music could vanish from our species and the rest of our lifestyle would be virtually unchanged.” He contends that music is a “spandrel,” in the terminology of biologist Stephen Jay Gould, a by-product of the way we have evolved, but without evolutionary value itself. Pinker calls music “auditory cheesecake.” It “tickles” a number of our mental faculties, but is no more essential to human life than cheesecake is to our diet. In this respect, music is unlike language, which is an important evolutionary adaptation that aids in our survival.

\_\_\_

Our ability to communicate meanings linguistically presupposes our musicality.

\_\_\_

Nineteenth-century German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche would argue that Pinker has it all wrong, particularly in his comparison of music and language. Nietzsche rejects the idea that language is more fundamental than music to the life of our species. According to him, our ability to communicate meanings linguistically presupposes our musicality. Without music, he argues, we would not have language as we know it.

The idea that music is a precondition of language may sound far-fetched, but debates about whether music or language is more fundamental have been longstanding in Western thought. Ancient Greek philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle examined the connection between music and language when they considered poetry set to music. They argued that in such settings, musical rhythms and melodies should support the texts that they accompanied. This view led late Renaissance opera composers to develop the recitative, which involved setting texts so that the music mimicked the rhythms and contours of speech. The recitative was so musically constrained that composers interspersed recitatives with arias, providing opportunities for singers to show off their virtuosity.

Early Christian thinkers debated whether God had endowed human beings with music as part of their nature or whether music was a later human invention. Modern thinkers disagreed on the origin of music and language and whether one preceded the other. Jean-Jacques Rousseau thought that language and music emerged from a common expressive mode—a view that in broad terms continues to have some currency. Herbert Spencer maintained that language came first, with music developing as a way of heightening emotion in speech. Charles Darwin, by contrast, contended that the melodic and rhythmic features of nonhuman animal vocalizations developed as means for attracting members of the opposite sex, suggesting that music (in some sense of the term) was prior to language.

\_\_\_

We recognize this commonality with others through the tonal subsoil, the musical aspect of language.

\_\_\_

Nietzsche considers the relationship between words and music in notebook fragments that he never published, written a year before the publication of his first book. There, he proposes that language involves both tone and gesture-symbolism. Tone is what is generally meant by “prosody,” those rhythmic, melodic, and timbral features of speech that constitute tone of voice. [Gesture-symbolism in Nietzsche’s discussion](http://www.nietzschesource.org/#eKGWB) is the arrangement of consonants and vowels (gestures because they are “nothing but *positions* of the organ of speech”), to which conventional meanings have been assigned.

The meaningfulness of the gesture-symbolism depends on the “tonal subsoil,” the nuanced expression of “degrees of pleasure and pain” that prosody reflects. Nietzsche takes the tonal subsoil to be evident universally, regardless of whether one knows the speaker’s language. This is because we recognize the biological, affective aspects of experience that we share with the speaker. According to Nietzsche, without the recognition of a common biological and affective basis for our experience, we could not be confident that [our words are capable of communicating to others.](http://iai.tv/video/dostoevsky-vs-nietzsche) But we recognize this commonality with others through the tonal subsoil, the musical aspect of language.

We might think that Nietzsche’s account of music and language relies on questionable definitions. He defines whatever is prosodic as music, while “language” includes only the conventional, conceptual associations with specific “gestures” made by our mouths. And perhaps we should take this discussion as tentative since Nietzsche himself never published it.

\_\_\_

Music was the presupposition of language.

\_\_\_

In a published discussion of the origins of language, however, Nietzsche makes it clear that he thinks the power of language to communicate would be quite limited if it were nothing more than the use of words as tokens for conventionalized categories. He argues that language emerged among our proto-human ancestors as a mode of social communication, used to quickly convey information to others in one’s group, and particularly to alert them to dangers. This motivated the development of consciousness, since for language to be useful in communicating information, those initiating linguistic messages must be conscious of what they take to be sources of pain or threats. But even with consciousness, these proto-humans were aware of themselves only as members of a larger herd, and they developed language for categories that were useful to the group.

Nietzsche argues that this social origin of language is still evident in the restricted way that language can refer to meanings that are specific to individuals. As speakers, we can convey such meanings only to the extent that they conform to the general categories conventionally associated with words, and this distorts the particularity of what we experience. Again, without the tonal features of prosody and bodily gesture, we would have limited means for communicating our meanings as particular speakers, including how we feel about what we say.

But the musical features of speech were not further refinements that developed after language was already in use. Music was the presupposition of language. Nietzsche considers the fundamental role of music in communication in his first book, [*The Birth of Tragedy Out of the Spirit of Music*.](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/51356/51356-h/51356-h.htm) There, Nietzsche follows his philosophical predecessor, [Arthur Schopenhauer](http://iai.tv/articles/schopenhauer-vs-nietzsche-the-meaning-of-suffering-auid-1801), who describes music as the most direct reflection we have of our fundamental nature.

Schopenhauer contrasts music with other art forms, which enable us to recognize types of things in their universal character and to see ourselves in universal terms as instances of humankind. Thus, we contemplate the nature of the things represented in art, and we do so without reference to our personal agendas, but as will-less subjects. By contrast, music taps directly into the will, the fundamental reality that is manifest not just in human beings, but in the world in general. Music reflects the nuanced dynamics of reality, including the dynamics of our own psychological nature. The patterns of tensions and resolutions we experience in music reflect our inner lives, [according to Schopenhauer](https://ia904500.us.archive.org/19/items/the-world-as-will-and-representation/The-World-as-Will-and-Representation.pdf), and this explains music’s emotional power.

\_\_\_

It is infinitely more nuanced than language.

\_\_\_

We recognize our own inner life in music, not as connected with our egos and personal projects, but as manifestations of our fundamental nature, which we share with everyone else. Thus, music draws our attention not to the emotions associated with particular situations in our lives, but to emotions in their essential character. Music does not express “this or that particular and definite pleasure, this or that affliction, pain, sorrow, horror, gaiety, merriment, or peace of mind, but joy, pain, sorrow, horror, gaiety, merriment, peace of mind themselves... without the motives for them.”

Although music makes us aware of the common ground of reality and emotional possibilities that are universally available to human beings, it is infinitely more nuanced than language. Thus, according to Schopenhauer, words that accompany music should always be subordinate to the music. Texts can offer particular examples of the more universal emotional conditions that the music manifests, but music is what accomplishes the powerful emotional communication.

\_\_\_

Music gave Greek tragedies their profound psychological and spiritual power.

\_\_\_

Nietzsche draws directly on Schopenhauer’s account in [*The Birth of Tragedy*](http://iai.tv/video/babette-babich-nietzsche-the-birth-of-tragedy-and-the-technology-trap), where he uses the idea that music relates us affectively to everyone else to explain the importance of tragic dramas for the ancient Athenians. Nietzsche claims that the emotional impact of these plays depended on the music provided by the chorus. The chorus communicated emotional states through music, and the audience absorbed them through emotional contagion. Affected by the emotion conveyed by the music, the audience was already primed for and collectively attuned to the significance of what the play would present. Music gave Greek tragedies their profound psychological and spiritual power. The plays were not internalized simply as providing performances of interesting plots. Instead, they were recognized as manifestations of human possibilities that were the spectators’ own and those of humanity at large.

Not only did the music suggest the emotional tenor of the play to be performed. It also transformed those in the audience from separate individuals with varying personal motives to a throng conjoined by an ecstatic sense of themselves as co-participants in the flux of life. Nietzsche describes this experience of being one with the entire flow of reality as inherently joyous, so much so that it overwhelmed all concern for one’s personal goals or any sense of vulnerability. Because of this, tragic dramas did not impress their audience with the dangers to which they were exposed as individuals, even though the plots might draw attention to human vulnerability. Instead, the plays enabled the audience to feel connected with a life much larger than themselves and to feel that they themselves were a part of nature, which is immortal.

\_\_\_

We need this immediate sense of connection in order to engage in the mediated communication of language.

\_\_\_

Nietzsche’s view may seem wildly speculative and overblown—he himself later criticized the excessively romantic character of his early writing. But toned down, Nietzsche’s account in *The Birth of Tragedy* suggests a basis for understanding why soundtracks have such a powerful effect on our experience of movies, why others’ songs seem to express our own experience, and why we are more confident that we know the intended meaning when we hear a speaker’s voice than when we read that person’s texts. Tuned in to the affective states that music and musical features communicate, we know how to make sense of someone’s words.

Nietzsche’s account of the role of music in Greek tragedy also enables us to see why he thinks music is a precondition of language. His point isn’t that we have to make musical sounds before we can speak (although the gurgling of babies before they learn to talk may make this plausible). Instead, he sees our use of language as sensible only because we already consider ourselves to be participants in a common life. Using words, with their general categories, presupposes awareness that we share a biological and affective nature that enables us to recognize objects and their significance in similar ways. Music (and musical prosody) is what makes us aware of our connection. Music synchronizes us and prompts us to move and express ourselves in ways that we can mutually and immediately grasp. We need this immediate sense of connection in order to engage in the mediated communication of language.

If music disappeared, we would not be otherwise unaffected, according to Nietzsche. We would lack the basic means through which we become aware that we share a world and common faculties for experiencing it. Without this, we would not have language, and our lives would not be recognizably human at all. Perhaps this is part of [what Nietzsche meant when he said](https://archive.org/details/twilightofidolsa0000niet), “Without music, life would be a mistake.”
