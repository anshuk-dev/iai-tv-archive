# Atheism cannot escape its religious origins

*How religion, not science, created the secular*

**By Tomer Persico** | *19th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,054 words*

![Atheism cannot escape its religious origins](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/atheism-cannot-escape-its-religious-origins.webp)

---

*The Enlightenment presents atheism as the logical consequence of science. After Copernicus, Newton, and Darwin, the existence of God became less credible and was let go. But for **Tomer Persico**, author of the Yuval Noah Harari-endorsed* In God’s Image*, this origin story is a delusion. Drawing on thinkers from Voltaire to Nietzsche, Persico argues that the modern dominance of atheism in the West isn’t the result of science. It is, as Nietzsche argued, the result of religious values that atheism has not escaped.*

If the absence of God were simply a fact, like the absence of rain, noting it would take no more courage than looking at the sky. No one thinks it brave to disbelieve in unicorns. Yet atheism has always presented itself as a matter of character: of honesty, of backbone, of the willingness to look reality in the eye. Atheism, in other words, is not only a claim. It is an ethos.

This is all the more striking when we remember what the atheist used to be. For much of Western history, the atheist was not a hero but a monster, an abomination we might liken to what the pedophile is today: not only repellent, but frighteningly incomprehensible.

Case in point: in 1689 John Locke published *A Letter Concerning Toleration*, which promoted toleration, though not for atheists. “Those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the Being of a God,” Locke writes. “Promises, Covenants, and Oaths, which are the Bonds of Humane Society, can have no hold upon an Atheist. The taking away of God, though but even in thought, dissolves all.”

Coming from one of the most liberal minds of the era, we might imagine what others thought. Indeed, atheists, the very few that existed, kept silent about their disbelief, risking imprisonment or worse if letting it slip. The problem was not only theological; atheists were considered inherent dissidents, endangering social harmony and lawfulness. To deny God was to cut the cord on which morality, society and reason itself were strung.

How, then, did the West’s most frightening vice become not only mundane, but, for some, one of its most admired virtues?

The obvious answer is science. [Copernicus de-centered the earth](https://iai.tv/video/the-centre-of-the-universe), [Darwin de-centered humanity](https://iai.tv/video/darwin-vs-consciousness), and God, having less and less credible agency, was let go. This is, of course, the Enlightenment’s own genesis myth, with a conversion narrative along the pattern of “I once was blind, but now I see.” Like most myths and conversion narratives, we shouldn’t take it at face value.

Knowledge alone rarely dislodges a faith. Persuade an ancient Athenian that lightning is an electrical discharge, and he will not conclude that there is no Zeus, but that Zeus is a fantastic electrician. God isn’t proven away by having his holy scriptures proven wrong. Literal text can always be made into an allegory and theological principles can be altered while keeping the core—God—preserved.

And indeed, one of the first fruits of “natural philosophy” and the emphasis on reason was not atheism but Deism, a bare-bones religion of pure reason that swept the educated elite of the eighteenth century. “I believe! I believe in you! Powerful God, I believe!” cried Voltaire, before adding: “As for monsieur the Son, and madame His Mother, that’s a different story.” For Voltaire, the existence of a supreme intelligence was “no matter of faith, but of reason.” It was logical, even essential. Reason had been turned against the Church. It had not yet been turned against God, which was considered its source. For that, something more than information was required.

That something was a moral imperative, and its development can be followed through three steps in European intellectual history.

The first can be illustrated through the thought of René Descartes. For Descartes, writing in the mid-17th century, reason was the image of God in us, the mark, as he put it, “by which the craftsman makes himself known in his handiwork.” It lets us master nature and, no less important, ourselves, examining “without passion” what is worth pursuing. This was the birth of a new ethic, in which the inspiration to live a worthy life comes from a person’s sense of their own dignity as a rational being.

Descartes was a sincere Roman Catholic who viewed his philosophy as fully compatible with his faith. Mysteries such as the Trinity, as far as he was concerned, belonged to revelation, while natural philosophy and much of metaphysics belonged to reason. In the next stage in our journey this separation collapses: human dignity requires anything and everything to be subjected to the trial of reason.

This we find in the thought of the English Deist Matthew Tindal. For Tindal, writing in the 1730s, reason and dignity became sacraments in and of themselves. “’Tis our Reason which makes us the Image of God himself,” he wrote, and so to surrender one’s reason to revelation is to “deface this Image, and give up the Dignity of human Nature.” Honoring God and respecting his image in every human being now meant rejecting whatever reason could not vouch for—miracles and mysteries and much of Christian theology included. All it took, said Tindal, was “Courage and Honesty.”

For Tindal, one of the most celebrated Deist thinkers, in order to preserve our dignity we must be uncompromising in our adherence to reason, which is the image of God in us. Reason instructs us that there is no realistic basis, nor actual evidence, for theological propositions like the Virgin Birth, Original Sin, or Jesus’ miracles. God wouldn’t want us to blindly believe in such fancies, and indeed instructs us to put them to the test of reason and reject them. Deism, in large part, is what’s left when we do that.

Of course, for Tindal, like Voltaire, God himself obviously existed—how else can we explain our ability to think, to reason, to know? Flesh and blood alone, as we can see in the animal kingdom, allow only brutish beasts. It is only humanity, which God made in his image, that has the ability to think. This is exactly why, in their opinion, we must insist on thinking and reject everything unreasonable. Reason exalts us and upholds the dignity of man.

The third stage in this intellectual journey comes quickly. In 1770 Baron d’Holbach picked up Tindal’s sword and used it to strike Tindal down. In his *System of Nature* (published, prudently, under a dead man’s name), he asked why the rational purge should stop at miracles and saintly virgins. The Deists, “contented with having trampled under foot the grosser prejudices of the illiterate,” he writes, but had “not dared to go back to the source.” They lacked the nerve to deny the greatest prejudice of all: God.

It requires “great courage” to break the spell, but it must be done in order to be totally and unequivocally loyal to reason. In truth, claims d’Holbach, there is no valid basis to believe in the existence of God. Man is a machine; God is an invention. Acknowledging this is a moral imperative. Only a person willing to embrace this predicate of reason, claims d’Holbach, “respects himself” and “feels his own dignity.”

What allowed d’Holbach to imagine humanity as clever machines was the new Newtonian universe that presented matter as a single, uniform field of bodies moving in understandable patterns, governed by fixed, mathematically expressible laws. No spirits or wills were needed to account for these motions, and there was no reason to presume humans weren’t also elaborate mechanisms that ran by themselves.

That takes care of our ability to think. But d’Holbach was also informed by another strain of cultural development, one that transferred humanity’s sources of morality from God, scripture and tradition to our inner worlds. As Canadian philosopher Charles Taylor masterfully explains, by the late eighteenth century two new, personal, internal, sources of morality were made available: one was reason, disconnected from God and displayed by the autonomous rational agent, bearer of an inner law (think Kant). The other was nature, or rather our own inner contact with our natural essence, which was sanctified by Romantic thought and could be manifested as conscience, intuition, emotions, authenticity (think Rousseau).

Morality could now be drawn from within. Our ability to be moral ceased to rely on our obedience to God or his representatives on earth. It could be realized by adhering to those sources of morality within ourselves: [following the directives of reason or being true to our conscience, feelings or intuition](https://iai.tv/video/the-passion-of-reason). Indeed, as Kant will insist, it depends on it. Morality demands independence, self-rule and free will. To act from obedience, even obedience to heaven, is not to act morally.

All this corresponds to an ever-increasing emphasis on autonomy. Modern individuals arise, and they—we—sanctify the ability to do as one pleases. Liberty itself changes its meaning and becomes what Isaiah Berlin calls “negative liberty,” the sole condition of which is that there is no external obstruction to one’s will. The liberal order begins to coalesce around these premises, and they begin to be implemented in political reality. A mere six years after d’Holbach’s book is published, the US Declaration of Independence proclaims all humanity’s unalienable right to liberty.

From there it is a short walk to secularization, and indeed atheism. If my dignity depends on rejecting all beliefs, then faith in God is an affront. If my worth lies in ruling myself, an omnipotent ruler is anathema. If moral action must be free of reward and threat, then paradise and hell are bribery and blackmail. Locke had refused to tolerate atheists because “the taking away of God… dissolves all.” Within a century of his death, it was God’s presence that threatened to dissolve all.

“As long as we have a master in heaven,” declared Mikhail Bakunin in 1871, “we will lie slaves on earth.” God, who had been the condition of reason and morality, becomes their chief obstacle. Humanity’s majesty, its freedom and its dignity, depend on its autonomy. This is the modern subject’s exodus from slavery in Egypt, with God cast as Pharaoh.

The image of God was internalized, and then God himself, as the source of moral authority, was internalized after it. Once that happened, there was no room left for God outside. The world cannot be shared between two gods—such is the nature of monotheism. Loyalty to one’s own inner voice demands the dismissal of every external one—such is the nature of secularism.

Nietzsche, who had the sharpest nose for genealogies, understood where all this came from. What defeated the Christian God, he wrote in *The Gay Science* (1887), was

Christian morality itself, the concept of truthfulness that was understood ever more rigorously, the father confessor’s refinement of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into a scientific conscience, into intellectual cleanliness at any price… In this severity, if anywhere, we are good Europeans and heirs of Europe’s longest and most courageous self-overcoming.

The confessional trained Westerners to search themselves for [the slightest self-deception](https://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-was-not-a-relativist-auid-3497). Eventually they searched away faith.

There’s a sweet irony here. The atheist’s virtues (truthfulness, courage, the refusal of comfort, the dignity of reason) are not the virtues of someone who has left the tradition behind. They are that tradition’s virtues, turned upon itself. [The image of God was first identified with reason](https://iai.tv/video/science-religion-and-the-quest-for-reason), then moved inside the individual, then made the seat of dignity and of moral authority; and at last it found that it could not share the world with its origin.

Moreover, the believer who thinks the atheist merely wants license has it backwards: the atheist is, if anything, a moral rigorist, a Puritan of reason. And the atheist who thinks he merely follows the evidence is [flattering himself in a different way](https://iai.tv/video/in-the-name-of-reason). He is following a calling, with its own ascetic disciplines, its own temptations to be resisted, its own unequivocal dogma.

Article 1 of the UN’s Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) states that all human beings “are endowed with reason.” Endowed by whom? The text does not say. The drafters, seeking a declaration that all nations could sign, left God out. But isn’t it marvelous that reason—exactly what Descartes and Tindal identified as the image of God in us, exactly what d’Holbach set out to exalt by rejecting God—is right at the top? Another chapter in the book is written. Not the last.
