# Why we live in alternate political realities

*The psychology behind Trump's polarisation*

**By Steven Sloman** | *24th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,473 words*

![Why we live in alternate political realities](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Why-we-live-in-alternate-political-realities.webp)

---

*When almost any event happens, people on opposing political sides reach wildly different conclusions about what actually happened. So much so that it feels like we are living in alternate realities. Steven Sloman, Professor of Cognitive and Psychological Science at Brown University, argues that in politics, we rely too much on our own sets of sacred values when we interpret political events. We need to understand the psychology that drives our [political convictions](https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/The-Cost-of-Conviction-by-Steven-A-Sloman/9780262049825), and discover how we can use consequences to bridge the gap between each of our sacred values, if we are ever to find common ground.*

When a military helicopter fatally crashed into an [American Airlines plane in January](https://apnews.com/article/dc-crash-investigation-american-airlines-helicopter-ntsb-acc8bf751427eb6824d9d1fad30895b8), President Donald Trump placed blame, without a moment’s reflection, on DEI initiatives in the military. This reflex captures the essence of what is happening today in America and in many parts of the world. Air crashes occur for complicated reasons. One must consider the possibility of pilot error, weather conditions, air traffic issues, aircraft malfunctions, and, yes, training issues. It turns out that the helicopter crew were training using night-vision goggles, and this likely had something to do with the crash. President Trump didn’t consider any of this. Rather, he jumped [to a conclusion that was politically expedient](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwyeg61pnl5o). In his world, DEI is the enemy of the people, and so he blamed DEI.

Trump’s leap of attribution is not only paradigmatic of his behavior; it’s exactly the kind of conceptual leap that most people make all the time. The world is complicated – too complicated for mere mortals to understand in any detail – and so we simplify it in all sorts of ways. For one, we use simplifying heuristics to make decisions. For instance, most people just vote for the political party that they voted for last time. This can save a lot of time and annoyance. Other tricks we use to simplify include ignoring aspects of a decision problem. We might vote for the candidate who shares our most [cherished value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer_question), and just not worry about where the candidate stands on other issues.

\_\_\_

His decision to blame DEI was grounded in a sacred value that actions related to DEI are bad, and he takes whatever opportunity he can to further that claim.

\_\_\_

We have a special trick to simplify things when it comes to many decisions, especially policy-related issues. We choose based on sacred values and ignore the consequences of our choices. When attributing blame in an air disaster, the National Transportation Safety Board is extremely careful. It does a meticulous and time-consuming investigation of the state of the players and the situation at the time of the catastrophe, identifying causes only when it has solid evidence that gives it a strong degree of belief that would stand up in a court of law or in the face of skeptical scientists and engineers. It is concerned with consequences in two senses. First, it wants to be sure that its conclusions explain how the consequences of the disaster came to be. It wants an accurate causal model of what actually happened. Second, it wants to be sure that the consequences of its verdict are fair and that the actual perpetrator (human, mechanical, legal, or whatever) is identified.

Trump did not show interest in either of these consequences. His decision to blame DEI was grounded in a sacred value that actions related to DEI are bad, and he takes whatever opportunity he can to further that claim. Sacred values are like that. Because they are absolute, ruling out reasonable trade-offs, they allow no compromise and encourage strong claims regardless of how much support they have. They don’t require evidence because [they are not about what actually happened](http://iai.tv/articles/how-to-break-free-from-political-groupthink-auid-3099). They are about principles of right and wrong action.

\_\_\_

They were thinking about which actions were right and wrong; they were motivated by sacred values.

\_\_\_

Trump is not the only politician to ignore facts and rely instead on sacred values. In fact, Democrats do it too. That’s how DEI became such a target in the first place. Rather than seriously consider the actual consequences of DEI programs, DEI supporters instituted programs willy nilly, fired up by a belief in the benefits of equity, inclusion and antiracism. This belief in sacred values created blindspots: it’s been known for years that DEI training is of [questionable value](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1816076116), yet schools, companies, and other institutions continued to require such training, and it became an industry. The fact that it rarely works, and sometimes backfires, had little influence on proponents because they weren’t thinking about the consequences of the training. They were thinking about which actions were right and wrong; they were motivated by sacred values.

When sacred values clash, as they do in this case, polarization results. It’s a cycle with a history as old as humanity. Sacred values are the rhetorical aspect of what holds ideological communities together. Religious communities are united by their shared faith that certain actions are necessary – be it prayer, sacrifice, fasting, or battle – or that certain actions are not allowed, like certain foods, abortion, murder, or battle.

\_\_\_

Sacred values are critical for a functioning society. The problem comes when they are used as the only frame for a complex issue that matters.

\_\_\_

When hostility erupts with other groups, generally because of competing interests, the community wants to distinguish itself from its enemy. They do this by making their sacred values more and more distinct. Railing against abortion, [with no exceptions for rape or incest](https://time.com/6213568/republicans-abortion-bans-divided/), becomes the rallying cry of one group while its opponent becomes more and more pro-choice, arguing for [legal abortion up to the point of birth](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-us-canada-47066307). Or one side becomes increasingly identified with the idea of free love, arguing against constraints on sexuality or relationships, and [even against the family itself](http://iai.tv/articles/the-case-for-abolishing-the-family-auid-2267). This drives the other side to double down on what they call “family values” and seek [limitations on divorce](https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2024/08/04/no-fault-divorce-jd-vance-women/). The list of sacred values that have motivated people over the course of history is long, but their purpose is singular: to create a sense of identity that distinguishes a group from those it perceives as competitors.

None of this is meant to imply that one should not have sacred values. Indeed, few of us would trust someone who didn’t. I hope that everyone reading this holds the sacred value that one should never hurt someone else without good reason, or that one should shape one’s beliefs around solid evidence to the extent one can. Sacred values are critical for a functioning society. The problem comes when they are used as the only frame for a complex issue that matters. Generally speaking, consequences cannot just be ignored. Sometimes, there are good reasons to hurt others (for instance, when one is attacked without provocation), and the false belief that one’s nation is powerful might just deter an enemy. Doing the right thing often requires considering outcomes.

\_\_\_

But framing issues consequently can force us to consider the future with due attention to its complexity.

\_\_\_

Politics in America today is dominated by discourse about sacred values. So much of our conversation concerns abortion, critical race theory, or what constitutes violating the Constitution. [Consequences are too often ignored](http://iai.tv/articles/we-need-a-new-political-theology-auid-3145). Instead of seeing issues through the eyes of a lawyer, we need to be more like engineers, focused on the effects that our policies and actions will have. One way to achieve this is by requesting causal explanations. [Asking people to explain how a policy works reduces their sense of their own understanding and can even make them less confident in their position](https://www.amazon.com/Knowledge-Illusion-Never-Think-Alone/dp/039918435X). But achieving this broadly requires a change in the norms of discourse. We have to demand real answers from politicians and others about empirically verifiable causes and consequences, and express our ultimate dissatisfaction with slogans expressing sacred values. This is already the norm at respectable news organizations, productive scientific laboratories, and elsewhere. Society would benefit by making it a part of all political discourse.

It therefore behooves us to do the hard work necessary to try to figure out what the outcomes of our decisions will be. We can rarely be sure; indeed, we are guaranteed to be surprised. Who knew that the invasion of Iraq by the US in 2003 would lead to the rise of the extremist Islamic State over the next 10 years? But framing issues consequently can force us to consider the future with due attention to its complexity. It also [makes conversation possible](http://iai.tv/video/overcoming-opposition-paul-dolan). It’s hard to talk to someone when you disagree with their intransigent beliefs concerning which actions are appropriate and which are forbidden. But only those whose hubris is over the top will have certainty about what the future holds. Focusing on that space of uncertainty, however small, can offer an opening for discussion even with those whose worldview seems outrageous.
