# There is no Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics

*Why we should stop playing the straw man game*

**By Noemi Bolzonetti** | *22nd September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,437 words*

![There is no Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/There-is-no-such-thing-as-the-copenhagen-interpretation.webp)

---

*The so-called Copenhagen interpretation has long been taught as quantum physics’ reigning orthodoxy, a neat tale of collapsing wavefunctions and observer-dependent reality. But the history tells a stranger story. Noemi Bolzenetti, Utrecht University Historian and Philosopher of Science, reveals the patchwork of competing ideas, stitched together into a myth that still dominates textbooks and surveys today. Unpacking Bohr and Heisenberg’s actual disagreements reveals not closure, but a richer set of puzzles still waiting to be faced.*

**1. Introduction**

What if the most famous interpretation of quantum physics never actually existed? We have been taught to picture Niels Bohr as the father of a mysterious doctrine called Copenhagen interpretation, where quantum reality collapses under the gaze of an observer. But dig into the historical record and a very different picture emerges: not a single doctrine, but a patchwork of views occasionally in tension with one another and developed over decades. The actual story is stranger and more revealing than the myth.

But first, a reminder for readers of one of the quantum riddles we are going to deal with in this article. In typical textbook quantum mechanics, a quantum system can be described by a wavefunction—a mathematical object that fully encodes the state of the system—as a superposition of possible states. This means, for instance, that an electron can be described as being in multiple positions at once, each position associated with a certain probability of being measured. The evolution of this wavefunction through time is continuous, or “smooth,” and deterministic, governed by the Schrödinger equation. Yet, when we measure the position of an electron (or, as it is sometimes said, “look” at it), what we get is one definite location, not a superposition. One might say that the [wavefunction appears then to “collapse”](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-the-collapse-of-the-wave-function-auid-2120) to a single definite outcome. This marks a discontinuity: a shift from the smooth, probabilistic evolution of the wavefunction to a single definite outcome, an abrupt jump. Why this happens, or how, is one of the riddles of quantum mechanics.

**2. The strawman game**

There is a game with simple rules and an almost guaranteed victory. It is called the strawman game. The game is played by conjuring two reference figures, two clear and distinct images, neatly polarized, and then setting them against each other in an eternal duel: good versus evil, right versus wrong, black versus white—forever blind to the shades of grey.

It is a game we know all too well, one we have learned to recognize, stigmatize, even attack. And yet, even as we recognize it, stigmatize it, and attack it, we keep on playing. July 2025, [Nature survey on the interpretations of quantum mechanics](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-02342-y): once again, the so-called

Copenhagen interpretation “still reigns supreme.” Yet the poll comes with a footnote: “the Copenhagen interpretation is not a self-consistent interpretation, but has become a popular description for physicists to say they adhere to.” One cannot help but ask: what exactly were the 399 respondents voting for when they picked “Copenhagen interpretation?”

**2.1. Playing the game again**

Let us, then, play the game ourselves. Let us rebuild the strawman called “The Copenhagen Interpretation.” This time, let us shape it from the answers of those very researchers who chose it in the survey, in the hope of grasping the lingering shadow this phantom still casts. What emerges is a familiar silhouette. For many, Copenhagen still wears the face of Niels Bohr. At times it sprouts extra heads—Heisenberg, Born—yet this Lernaean Hydra remains one and the same. Its canonical features traced from the poll’s responses are the following:

1. Measurement collapse. The discontinuity arises because the act of measurement involves a change from a fundamentally probabilistic quantum description to a classical one with definite outcomes and real observables.  
2. Observer dependence. A measurement requires an observer, though not necessarily a conscious one—an observer may be any macroscopic environment.  
3. The quantum–classical cut. There exists a boundary between quantum and classical objects, though not a sharp one.

These are the canonical traits, the textbook caricature we have inherited: the Copenhagen interpretation employs wave-packet collapse, grants the observer a privileged role, and invokes the movability of the cut between quantum and classical: the dividing line between the observed object and the measuring apparatus—between what is treated quantum mechanically and what is treated classically—is not fixed by nature but can be shifted according to convenience.

If taken literally, this fact would bring science itself—the very possibility of giving a naturalistic, objectively given description of the world—to an end

\_\_\_

The only problem? As many scholars have argued for more than twenty years, this was not Bohr’s view at all. As [Don Howard has maintained](https://doi.org/10.1086/425941), what we call the “Copenhagen interpretation” seems to be, in fact, a hybrid, largely constructed by Heisenberg, reflecting his own perspective [rather than Bohr’s](http://iai.tv/articles/einstein-vs-bohr-quantum-reality-is-still-up-for-grabs-auid-3131).

What was truly novel for Bohr about measurement—what he called the “quantum postulate”—was the idea that, because of the finiteness of Planck’s “quantum of action” (ℎ), which sets limits to arbitrary divisibility, every act of measurement involves the subject (the measuring apparatus) and the object (the system under study) forming an indivisible whole, an *in-dividuum*. Their descriptions cannot be separated. If taken literally, this fact would bring science itself—the very possibility of giving a naturalistic, objectively given description of the world—to an end: we could no longer speak of properties of “things”—position, momentum, and so on—as existing independently of measurement. And yet we do, and indeed we must. We do have what Bohr called a “classical” description of the world, in which object and apparatus are assumed to be separable, and in which definite properties—“classical concepts” such as position and momentum—can be attributed to individual objects. Objectivity is preserved, but at a cost: we must acknowledge the limitations of these concepts, of these “forms of perception” (*Anschauungsformen*, with its loosely Kantian undertone). Such properties are not absolute; they are contextual. They can be defined only within specific experimental arrangements, and the very existence of mutually incompatible contexts—such as those for measuring conjugate variables like position and momentum—marks the limitation of use of classical concepts. This limit takes the form of Bohr’s famous, but often misunderstood, [principle of complementarity](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1876-0503(08)70329-3).

This is something entirely different from the three folkloristic points listed above. Once again, a polarized conflict seems to emerge; yet another duel. On one side stands Heisenberg, who, despite conceiving the act of measurement as the transition from a fundamentally probabilistic quantum description to a classical one with definite outcomes, still seems to hold a naturalistic view of sorts, at least at this stage (around 1927). On the other sidestands Bohr: for whom [quantum mechanics](http://iai-academy/courses/info?course=the-philosophy-of-quantum-theory) appears to contain a meta-theoretical component—absent from Heisenberg’s 1927 view—as a theory about theory, reflecting on how science itself works and where its limits lie, limits that the very phenomena of quantum physics compel us to confront.

But here too, we are still playing the strawman game. We have only changed the protagonists: Heisenberg versus Bohr.

**2.2. Stepping out of the game**

There is a wiser way to play: not by building taller strawmen, but by delving deeper into the problems themselves as they were conceived by the historical actors, by confronting them anew, and reconsidering the different solutions proposed to address them, in all their complexity.

Let us give an example of this new way of playing, a case study: the uncertainty relations between position and momentum, prototypical expression of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, and of the non-commutativity of the position and momentum operators. In quantum mechanics, a precise measurement of position *x* entails losing the possibility of defining momentum *p**x* with equal precision, and thus losing the ability to predict with certainty the future trajectory of the particle. What remains is a range of possibilities, each with its own probability.

With this in mind, let us take two steps. First, a return to the origins: looking back at how the problem was first introduced, for instance, through the famous γ-ray microscope. Second, an analysis and rediscovery of Bohr’s and Heisenberg’s different approaches.

Heisenberg first presented the γ-ray microscope thought experiment in 1927, precisely as a way of introducing the uncertainty relation between position and momentum. The idea was to imagine observing an electron through a microscope by illuminating it with very short-wavelength light. The shorter the wavelength, the more precisely the electron’s position could, in principle, be determined. According to the standard story, Heisenberg argued that such high-energy photons would inevitably scatter off the electron, imparting an unpredictable recoil and thereby disturbing its momentum. Thus, the more precisely one measures the position, the harder the electron is “kicked” by the scattering high-energy photon, and the more one ends up with an “uncontrollable” momentum. My research within the [ERC project BOHR21](https://www.uu.nl/en/research/history-and-philosophy-of-physics/research/bohr21), led by Guido Bacciagaluppi at Utrecht University, has shown, thanks to some letters—in particular one from Jordan to Rosenfeld (31 December 1927) and [one from Heisenberg himself to Dirac (27 April 1927](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1876-0503(08)70326-8))—that in 1927 there were in fact, two distinct versions of the microscope: Bohr’s, and Heisenberg’s own, which he described as “the one I always had in mind.” In his letter, Heisenberg even sketched both versions (see figure (1)) and derived the uncertainty relations in each case.

Figure 1: Bohr’s version of the microscope on the left and Heisenberg’s version on the right.

Despite their differences, Heisenberg presented the two situations as equivalent, two examples that, in his view, expressed the same vision and led to the same result. Here, perhaps, one can find a premonition of the Heisenberg whom Howard indicates as the true architect of the Copenhagen interpretation, driven by his desire to forge a single, unified image from his own thought and Bohr’s. And indeed, in both Bohr’s and Heisenberg’s setups, the scenario is the same: a photon, with an associated wavelength *λ*, collides with an electron (see the black dot in Fig. (2)).

![No copenhagen image 2](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzgwMCwyMTZd/No-copenhagen-image-2.webp)

Figure 2: Bohr’s version of the experiment on the left and Heisenberg’s version on the right.

Then, in Bohr’s version, the position of the electron is measured with a microscope of angular aperture *ϕ*, which “collects” the scattered photon. The uncertainty in position is *Δ**x* ∼ *λ*/*ϕ* since we are measuring with a microscope, and so [Rayleigh’s criterion](https://www.edinst.com/resource/the-rayleigh-criterion-for-microscope-resolution/) for the limit of resolution comes into play. And the uncertainty in momentum is *Δ**p* ∼ *p**ϕ*, where *ϕ*, the angular aperture of the microscope, sets the range from which the necessary information is gathered and effectively becomes the angle of “diffusion” of our photon, the spread of possible scattering directions.

In Heisenberg’s version, the measurement is carried out with a slit of width *d* which likewise, “collects” the scattered photon. Here, the uncertainty in position is simply ∆*x* ∼ d, since we are measuring the position of the electron with a slit, and so its width sets the limit of resolution. While the uncertainty in momentum is ∆*p* ∼ *p λ*/*d*, where *λ*/*d* is the finite angle of divergence of the photon due to diffraction at the slit, which again becomes the angle of “diffusion” of our photon.

\_\_\_

The Copenhagen interpretation has become a ghost we keep chasing. It is invoked to silence debates, to reassure students, and to provide a tidy narrative about quantum mechanics.

\_\_\_

In both versions, contrary to what a widespread folklore maintains, the wave-like nature of light and the related laws of optics are at work: in Bohr’s case, through Rayleigh’s resolution limit, and in Heisenberg’s, through the diffraction angle associated with the slit. And in both cases, combining the results leads directly to the uncertainty relation between position and momentum: ∆*x* ∆*p* ∼ ℎ.

Yet a closer look reveals a genuine conceptual difference that Heisenberg seems to minimize. In both cases, the wave-like aspect makes its appearance, but its role differs profoundly. In

Bohr’s case, the wave-like aspect—diffraction (the Rayleigh limit)—is a *precondition* of defining position. In Heisenberg’s case, the wave-like aspect—diffraction—is an *effect* of the measurement of position. For Bohr, it arises from the very way position is defined through the microscope; it is directly tied to the demand for a space-time description (to use Bohr’s own words), and it is this precondition that requires the uncertainty in momentum *ϕ*. For Heisenberg, diffraction arises as an effect produced by the slit, and it is this modification, the presence of a measuring slit, that produces the uncertainty in momentum *λ*/*d*. This divergence of views, subtle but essential, mirrors their broader differences in 1927: Bohr’s complementarity versus Heisenberg’s discontinuity.

This analysis does more than shed new light on the distinct ways Bohr and Heisenberg were thinking in 1927, their differences as well as their similarities. It also deepens our understanding of the very concept of the uncertainty relations by situating them in the context in which they were originally discussed and by highlighting the attempt to give them physical meaning. An attempt that, at the time, was still profoundly tied to the ongoing debates about wave-particle duality and the use of concepts inherited from classical physics, rather than confined to formalism alone.

Overall, the “case study” of the γ-ray microscope is not an end in itself—just something meant to set some historical detail straight—but rather a powerful example, showing that there is another way of doing research: one that follows half-faded paths, whose traces lie among sealed boxes and dusty papers filled with notes and scribbles. A search for what we might have overlooked while we were too busy playing the strawman game.

**3. Conclusion**

The Copenhagen interpretation has become [a ghost we keep chasing](http://iai.tv/articles/einstein-was-wrong-about-quantum-mechanics-tim-maudlin-auid-2700). It is invoked to silence debates, to reassure students, and to provide a tidy narrative about quantum mechanics. Yet what we call the Copenhagen interpretation is a retrospective construction, a hybrid label forged from fragments of different views, convenient for textbooks and surveys but ultimately misleading. This matters for more than pedantic accuracy. When we accept inherited caricatures, we also accept inherited limits on how we see the past and on how we imagine our future.

Returning to letters, drafts, notebooks, and thought experiments does not simply set the historical record straight. It offers an opportunity to re-engage with the problems on their own terms. And it makes clear that, in most cases, there is no single duel between good and evil, right and wrong, or black and white, only deeply passionate human beings confronting the same profound puzzle from different angles.

In the end, the story of the Copenhagen interpretation is a cautionary tale about how science and its history are told. Labels can be useful, but they can also harden into dogma (paradoxically, even to fight against other dogmas, or alleged ones). By recognizing the hybrid origins of the so-called Copenhagen interpretation, we regain a sense of possibility. Quantum mechanics need not be imprisoned by a single mythic narrative, nor must our thinking. Freed from the strawman, we can return to the questions that first animated Bohr, Heisenberg, and their peers, and perhaps discover new answers of our own.
