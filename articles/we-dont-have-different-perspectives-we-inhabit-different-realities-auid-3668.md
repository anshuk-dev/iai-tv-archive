# We don't have different perspectives, we inhabit different realities

*Quantum mechanics, neuroscience, and the end of reality*

**By Steve Fuller** | *2nd September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,452 words*

*We tend to think we live in a shared reality, with differences in how we experience the world attributed to differences in perspective. But according to philosopher and founder of social epistemology, **Steve Fuller**, this is a radical mistake. Breakthroughs in neuroscience, quantum mechanics, and AI show that we don't have different perspectives on the same reality. We inhabit different realities altogether.*

Our default understanding of reality is more specific than we might first suppose. Its specificity is well captured by the biases built into philosophical appeals to “realism.” For the realist, reality is discovered, not constructed. The reality of something is suspect if the knower is knowingly involved in its realization. Moreover, reality is one thing for everyone, not different things for different people—that’s “relativism.” Everything exists in this one reality, regardless of how independent these things might seem from one another. Realists tend to be vexed by talk of “multiple realities” that cannot be reduced to multiple perspectives on the same reality. Indeed, some realists expect that science will someday provide a common understanding of this shared reality.

Of course, down through the ages there have been honorable dissenters to this rather specific conception of reality, and I believe that recent developments in science and technology will give them greater voice in the future. These developments come from different directions—neuroscience, quantum mechanics, generative artificial intelligence—but they point to an emerging alternative sense of reality and the need to rethink the nature of knowledge.

Recent neuroscientific research has revealed the full extent of the variability and plasticity of the human brain. In this context, “neurodiversity,” a coinage of sociologist Judy Singer, has come into common currency. Slowly but surely, [society is abandoning the idea of a normal functioning brain](https://iai.tv/articles/the-neurodivergence-paradox-auid-2956), in terms of which conditions such as autism are deemed to be disabilities, if not pathologies. Instead, the normality of differently abled brains has gradually come to be accepted. The neuroscientist [Karl Friston](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2787) has described this in terms of [brains being differently tuned to respond to environmental inputs](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston). Eventually, this shift in perspective might even underwrite what the educationalist Howard Gardner more than forty years ago called “multiple intelligences.” Instead of training all brains to align with one shared reality, environments will be customized for these diversely tuned brains to realize their full potential.

Quantum mechanics presents a view of reality in which we inhabit many worlds at once. As the political scientist Alexander Wendt has put it in [*Quantum Mind and Social Science*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/quantum-mind-and-social-science/3D5DB273B648D0A23B49C1C4ABA5CF7A), we are “walking wave functions,” which “collapse” at decisive moments, when everyone is forced to accept the same reality, but in a way that reconfigures each person’s view of how the past was and the future will be. Niels Bohr introduced the word “incommensurability,” which [Thomas Kuhn](https://iai.tv/articles/the-next-scientific-revolution-wont-come-from-scientists-auid-3448) popularized, to characterize the kind of “coherence” involved here. For example, Galileo could imagine living in both a geocentric and a heliocentric world, until his telescopic observations convinced him of the latter. At that moment, he started living exclusively in the heliocentric world and proceeded so much in that way that he eventually faced a Papal Inquisition.

[Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)](https://iai.tv/video/ai-and-the-mysteries-of-reality) basically works by developing connections among human inputs that have been deprived of their initial human weightings. Tons of text are simply fed into big computers, which process them with minimal operating rules. In certain respects, this amounts to a rebooting of human knowledge. Humans remain the feeders, but the academic filter has been removed. After all, academic training is largely about knowing what to ignore, whereas a GenAI system like ChatGPT is much more open-minded, customizing responses to the human user, drawing on all the relevant information available. At the same time, the human prompts themselves provide more input, which serves to continually rewire the body of human knowledge, rendering it much more dynamic and fluid than anything academia would countenance—or perhaps even imagine.

Does all this mean that reality is no more than a proliferation of perspectives? Not really. I have already suggested that if we stick to the default philosophical understanding of “realism,” then “reality” and “perspective” are simply complementary terms: The latter provides one of many possible versions of the former. In these terms, academia provides a “privileged” perspective on reality. However, I am talking about a much more radical shift in perspective. Put bluntly: We live in multiple worlds simultaneously, whose differences only matter when we interact in certain ways. While unpacking this claim would take at least a book, its implications are relatively clear—and damaging—for Wikipedia, the largest and most widely consulted compendium of human knowledge in history.

At first glance, Wikipedia seems very different from traditional encyclopedias or other authoritative reference sources. Its content is determined solely by those who edit its pages. It is not dictated or even overseen by field experts. The standing of Wikipedia editors is based on what fellow editors make of their contributions. Moreover, Wikipedia content can change quite easily in response to events. (“Wiki” is Hawaiian for “quick.”) In that respect, Wikipedia is very much a “living” body of knowledge, as evidenced by its “Talk” and “History” pages. Unsurprisingly, Wikipedians see themselves as democratic insurgents against the academic elites. However, Wikipedia retains two fatal academic traits.

The most obvious one is path dependency, which Wikipedia exacerbates. Although Wikipedia studiously bans named interested parties from editing its pages, that doesn’t stop regular, typically anonymous, Wikipedia editors from being custodians of the pages that interest them. The self-selecting nature of these editors means that Wikipedia entries can become quite idiosyncratic in terms of content and length, as editors heatedly dispute matters that field experts would find marginal or settled. And whereas there are historians of science and ideas who try to rationalize the twists and turns that offline knowledge production has taken over the centuries, comparable scholars do not exist for Wikipedia’s online epistemic world. It explains why editing Wikipedia can be such a mysterious and daunting experience for newcomers. Maybe this would change if intellectual historians took Wikipedia seriously—at least the data is there to do so.

Indeed, notwithstanding its self-understanding as “open source,” Wikipedia is arguably even more biased than academia—especially in ways that academia has tried to mitigate. No one denies that for better or worse, the character of academic knowledge has been anchored in the contributions made and the decisions taken by Euro-American males. However, various external pressures on academia in recent times have significantly increased the presence of more non-Euro-American-male publishers. In contrast, even a quarter century after its launch, Wikipedians remain 85% male and preponderantly from Europe and North America. The one non-trivial countervailing pattern is that the average Wikipedian is still at least a decade younger than the average academic author. Nevertheless, the fact that the world’s leading reference work reproduces the most criticized feature of legacy sources of knowledge is telling.

However, the more profound trait that Wikipedia adopts from academic knowledge production is the presumption of a common reality, which is enshrined in Wikipedia’s “core content policies”: “Neutral point of view,” “No original research,” and “Verification.” They add up to a vision of the world consisting of what John Searle called “[institutional facts](https://iai.tv/video/seeing-how-it-is).” It is not exactly G.E. Moore’s world of commonly agreed propositions, but it’s truth on record. Thus, Wikipedia urges contributors not to argue with verifiable claims but to juxtapose opposing verifiable claims and contextualize the opposition. The policy harks back to medieval scholasticism, albeit done in a more loose-buttoned style. In effect, Wikipedia promotes the idea of common reality but one whose source is never named. At least the medieval scholastics could refer to God.

Moreover, as I have insisted, recent science and technology are rendering this very philosophical idea of reality redundant. Instead of alternative points of view on a fixed object, however defined, the alternatives always exist in a superimposed way, periodically resolving to permit the identification of distinct objects without eliminating the ongoing superimposition. [Quantum mechanics](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-is-quantum-mechanical-with-stuart-hameroff) may underwrite this vision at the physical level and neurodiversity at the biological level. At a more practical level, this shift in horizons is regularly marked by the ease with which AI-driven search engines alter their epistemic outputs in response to increasingly customized user prompts. The overall effect is likely to increase the acceptance of literally multiple realities. In any case, as the US library and information scientist Jenna Hartel said to me when ChatGPT first came onstream, “Wikipedia is toast.”
