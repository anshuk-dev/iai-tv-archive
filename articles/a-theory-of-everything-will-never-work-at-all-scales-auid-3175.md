# A theory of everything will never work at all scales

*Reality is fundamentally different at different sizes*

**By Stephan Hartmann** | *21st May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,821 words*

![A theory of everything will never work at all scales](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/A-theory-of-everything-will-never-work-at-all-scales2.webp)

---

*Physicists have long hoped to discover a single, all-encompassing theory of everything that would unlock the secrets of the universe. But, as LMU Munich philosopher of science, Stephan Hartmann, argues, a new picture is emerging—one where no single framework reigns supreme. Effective field theories are transforming how we understand science itself. Rather than reducing everything to fundamental laws, they offer a patchwork of powerful, scale-sensitive models that reflect the layered structure of reality. What, if the most reliable truths are not universal, but local, scale-dependent and context-dependent?*

For centuries, science has pursued the dream of a “Theory of Everything,” a single, elegant framework explaining the universe, from the tiniest particles to the grandest galaxies. Writing in 1980, [Stephen Hawking](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/77010/a-brief-history-of-time-by-stephen-hawking/9780553109535) predicted this theory might be found by the end of the twentieth century. But today, many physicists are charting a different course. At the heart of this shift lies an unlikely hero: effective field theories. While this article focuses on the framework of effective field theories in physics, the broader theme that knowledge often comes in scale-sensitive layers extends well beyond physics, reaching into fields such as biology and technology.

Effective field theories are remarkably modest in ambition. They describe what is relevant at a particular energy scale and set aside what is not. Their strength lies in precision and adaptability, not in claims of ultimate truth. Rather than offering a “God’s-eye” view of the universe, they offer locally valid and remarkably useful tools, much like carefully crafted maps designed for navigating specific terrains.

Physicist Steven Weinberg captures this tension better than anyone. On the one hand, he was a founding figure in the development of effective field theories and helped establish them as a central tool in modern physics. On the other hand, he remained committed to the ideal of a final, all-encompassing theory—a theme he passionately defended in his influential popular book [*Dreams of a Final Theory*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/188044/dreams-of-a-final-theory-by-steven-weinberg/). Weinberg’s legacy thus embodies a deeper philosophical question: can the patchwork success of effective theories coexist with the hope for ultimate unification? Or does the very success of effective field theories suggest that nature resists being captured by a single, all-embracing framework?

![Photograph of Steven Weinberg](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCwzMzhd/StevenWeinberg.webp)

This question opens the door to a broader reconsideration of how science works. The shift toward effective field theories challenges traditional views about reductionism, emergence, and scientific realism, inviting a humbler but arguably more honest picture of knowledge. In a world where understanding often comes in pieces, effective field theories do not aim to unify everything at once, but instead teach us how to work productively with fragments—offering tools tailored to scale, context, and purpose.

**A Tower of Theories**

Effective field theories describe how systems behave at specific energy scales, ignoring the finer details of higher energies (or, equivalently, shorter distances). They are remarkably successful in practice: chiral perturbation theory (an effective field theory that describes how protons, neutrons, and pions interact at low energies in nuclear physics), the Standard Model of particle physics, and even general relativity can be seen as effective field theories. They allow us to make predictions where more fundamental theories are either unavailable or intractable.

Instead of aiming for a single final theory, physics increasingly resembles a tower of theories: one for atoms, another for nuclei, another for quarks. Each level is largely autonomous, yet still connected to the levels above and below. This layered structure is made precise by renormalization group methods, which help us understand how a system’s behavior changes as we shift from one scale to another. You can think of these methods as a kind of theoretical zoom lens: as we zoom out, the fine-grained details blur, and broad, stable patterns come into focus.

Remarkably, very different microscopic systems—like fluids, magnets, or quantum fields—can display the same large-scale behavior. Renormalization group methods explain this using the idea of a *flow* through “theory space”: as we zoom out, the mathematical description of the system changes, gradually moving or “flowing” toward a simplified form. A *fixed point* is where this flow comes to rest, where further zooming no longer changes the system’s essential behavior. At that point, many different systems can look the same, even if their microscopic makeup is completely different. This phenomenon, known as *universality*, reveals a deep insight: the most relevant truths about a system often emerge not from its tiniest constituents, but from its collective behavior at the right scale.

This approach doesn’t diminish the importance of deeper theories. But it recognizes that no single theory can do all the explanatory work. Some theories are better suited to particular questions and scales. This is not a failure of science; it’s a recognition of how complex reality is.

**Reductionism Revisited**

The layered structure of scientific theories complicates the traditional picture of reductionism. While higher-level theories can sometimes be derived from lower-level ones, the process is rarely straightforward—and even when it succeeds, the resulting derivation may obscure more than it reveals.

Effective field theories exemplify this tension. They are often described as “reducible in principle but autonomous in practice.” Their success doesn’t depend on fully deriving them from deeper, more fundamental theories. Instead, they offer their own explanations, tailored to the scale at hand—explanations that are often more illuminating and more practically useful than appeals to inaccessible microphysics. This challenges the idea that scientific understanding must always travel from the bottom up.

\_\_\_

Effective field theories truly emerge from more fundamental ones, supporting a conditional and scale-sensitive form of reductionism. Put simply, even though new behaviors show up at low energies, they can still—under the right conditions—be traced back to deeper physical laws.

\_\_\_

At the same time, [effective field theories do not entirely undermine the reductionist project](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1355219801000053). In certain cases—especially when the underlying theory is what’s known as *renormalizable* (meaning its equations can be adjusted to avoid mathematical infinities)—the [Appelquist–Carazzone decoupling theorem](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.11.2856) shows that low-energy behavior can be systematically derived from the high-energy theory. This is done by *integrating out* high-energy degrees of freedom: that is, mathematically removing the influence of very heavy particles whose effects on low-energy processes are negligible. Because of this, such particles can be safely ignored when studying physics at lower energies (which correspond to larger length scales, while high energies correspond to smaller, more fine-grained scales). In these cases, effective field theories truly emerge from more fundamental ones, supporting a conditional and scale-sensitive form of reductionism. Put simply, even though new behaviors show up at low energies, they can still—under the right conditions—be traced back to deeper physical laws.

What effective field theories ultimately support, then, is not a wholesale rejection of reductionism, but a more pragmatic and flexible picture of scientific explanation. Understanding emerges from models that are tractable, context-sensitive, and responsive to the structures that matter at a given scale.

In this sense, emergence becomes central to how science makes sense of the world. Phenomena such as fluid flow, superconductivity, or magnetism don’t fully reveal themselves at the micro-level. They become intelligible only through higher-level models that capture patterns irreducible to the underlying mechanics.

Crucially, many of these emergent patterns are robust: they recur across systems and contexts, and are often stable under variations in microscopic details. For example, superconductivity—the complete loss of electrical resistance—can arise in very different materials with very different underlying structures, yet the macroscopic behavior follows the same general principles. This suggests that the phenomenon doesn’t depend on every microscopic detail, but rather on certain collective features. Such stability lends support to a form of explanatory pluralism, where no single level of theorizing has privileged access to truth, but each contributes something essential to our overall understanding.

**No Theory of Everything?**

If effective field theories are so useful, do we need a theory of everything? Many physicists now say no. As Harvard physicist [Howard Georgi](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.ns.43.120193.001233) once quipped: “Who knows? Who cares?” For him, the point is not whether a final theory exists, but whether it's useful.

\_\_\_

Nature may not conform to human ideals of simplicity and unity. Instead, the universe may be intelligible through a mosaic of effective models, each valid in its own domain.

\_\_\_

This marks a shift from metaphysical ambition to methodological humility. To many, the aim is no longer to discover a single unifying framework, but rather to develop a robust patchwork of overlapping, scale-specific theories. Still, in some quarters, the dream of unification persists. String theory, for example, continues to pursue a final answer. But it is no longer the only game in town. Indeed, some thinkers, including Stephen Hawking in his later work (e.g. in his book [*The Grand Design*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/77018/the-grand-design-by-stephen-hawking-and-leonard-mlodinow/9780553384666), written withLeonard Mlodinow), have questioned whether the very idea of a final theory even makes sense.

Moreover, the notion that one theory could explain everything may itself be a metaphysical illusion. Nature may not conform to human ideals of simplicity and unity. Instead, the universe may be intelligible through a mosaic of effective models, each valid in its own domain.

**A Theory of Theories**

Effective field theory functions not only as a physical framework but also as a meta-framework for understanding the structure of scientific theories. It teaches us how to relate different levels of description, how to isolate relevant variables, and how to accept limits without surrendering rigor.

\_\_\_

Rather than seeking a single, unified framework, this view embraces approximation, acknowledges the context-dependence of theories, and abandons the illusion that deeper explanations are always superior.

\_\_\_

In many areas of physics, especially when studying phase transitions or critical points, renormalization group methods help scientists understand how physical behavior changes as we “zoom out” to larger and larger scales. These methods track how key parameters evolve, revealing stable patterns that emerge despite microscopic differences. This kind of scale-based structure forms a powerful foundation for scientific understanding. But not all domains behave so neatly. In some areas, changes across scales are fragmented and don’t follow the smooth patterns that renormalization group methods rely on. And in certain cases, there isn’t even a clear separation between small-scale and large-scale behavior. For example, in the [cosmological constant problem in gravity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/and-the-limits-of-effective-field-theory/B420BA49A7548036DBDA3D0A13119B29), the energy of empty space—known as vacuum energy—doesn’t fade away at larger scales as expected, making it hard to isolate and ignore. Similarly, in fully developed fluid turbulence, like in a swirling river or a whirlpool, small and large eddies (circular motions) constantly interact with one another. There’s no clean boundary between scales, so you can’t focus on just one level and treat the rest as irrelevant. In such cases, the effective field theory approach breaks down.

Recognizing these limits points toward a more pluralistic and modular conception of scientific knowledge. Rather than seeking a single, unified framework, this view embraces approximation, acknowledges the context-dependence of theories, and abandons the illusion that deeper explanations are always superior.

This vision aligns with the perspective articulated by physicist [Michèle Levi](https://cerncourier.com/a/a-theory-of-theories/), who has described effective field theory as a “theory of theories.” In Levi’s account, effective field theories offer a systematic method for breaking down complex multi-scale problems into manageable pieces, allowing physicists to work within energy domains where both control and clarity are possible. Crucially, this framework does not require full knowledge of the underlying theory. Instead, it capitalizes on symmetries and relevant degrees of freedom at a given scale, thus supporting a bottom-up methodology that is both predictive and explanatory.

Such a philosophy of science values usefulness alongside truth. It treats models not simply as mirrors of reality, but as tools that capture relevant features at the appropriate level of description. Theories are assessed not solely by their proximity to some ultimate truth, but also by how effectively they help us navigate, predict, and understand phenomena within a given domain.

In this spirit, effective field theories offer both philosophical and methodological insights. They illustrate how abstraction, idealization, and scale-dependence can yield genuine understanding—without requiring a single, all-encompassing picture. Rather than undermining realism, they invite a more nuanced view: one in which scientific theories reflect reality as seen from particular vantage points.

**Epistemic Patchwork, Real Understanding**

What does all this mean for realism, the view that our best scientific theories tell us something true about the world? A common worry is that if effective field theories are valid only up to a certain energy scale, they can’t support robust realist commitments. After all, how can a theory be “true” if it breaks down beyond a certain domain?

Philosopher [Porter Williams](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1093/bjps/axx043) addresses this concern by offering a refined version of realism, grounded in how science is actually practiced. In his view, effective field theories don’t need to describe everything to be realist—they just need to reliably describe something. What matters is that the structures they identify are stable, empirically successful, and indispensable at the scale in question. Rather than demanding that a theory be globally valid, Williams advocates a “divide and conquer” approach: commit to those parts of a theory that do genuine explanatory work.

This shifts the emphasis from metaphysical ambition to explanatory performance. Effective field theories excel by isolating resilient patterns that persist across scales—patterns revealed through both experiment and renormalization group methods. These are not necessarily fundamental in the traditional sense, but they are robust enough to warrant realist commitment.

In this way, effective field theories do not undermine realism. They recalibrate it. They support a version of realism that is layered, local, and context-sensitive. On this view, truth is not a single, unified verdict on the universe, but a mosaic of reliable insights at the scales where we can test and understand the world.

\_\_\_

Scientific knowledge, on this view, is not a towering edifice but a sprawling network—a constellation of bridges, buildings, and pathways, each serving a distinct purpose and together illuminating the broader landscape.

\_\_\_

Importantly, this lesson extends beyond fundamental physics. In condensed matter theory, [Robert Laughlin and David Pines](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.97.1.28) describe “quantum protectorates”—cases like superconductivity or the quantum Hall effect—where the large-scale behavior of a system is remarkably stable and becomes effectively independent of the messy microscopic details. In biology, [Denis Noble](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsfs.2011.0067)’s concept of “biological relativity” challenges the idea that genes alone hold explanatory primacy; for instance, heart rhythms or cell behavior can’t be fully understood without considering interactions at multiple levels, from molecular to organ-scale dynamics. Across domains, effective theories help structure knowledge in a way that reflects the autonomy of levels—where higher-level patterns and regularities play an indispensable explanatory role, even if they emerge from more basic constituents.

This layered perspective also resonates with broader philosophical debates about explanation. Concepts such as agency, intentionality, and [free will](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674979581) may not feature in the language of particle physics or neurobiology, yet they play indispensable explanatory roles in the social and decision sciences. As philosopher [Christian List](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nous.12241) argues, such higher-level properties may *supervene* on underlying physical states, meaning they depend on them in a systematic way without being *reducible* to them. Their reality, like that of macroscopic objects, is grounded not in their presence at the most fundamental level, but in their *explanatory indispensability* within a *layered ontology*. This is a view of the world in which different levels of description capture different, but equally real, aspects of complex systems.

**Conclusion**

Effective field theories teach us a lesson in intellectual modesty. They remind us that science advances not by grasping everything at once, but by crafting tools that work where they are needed.

The result is a more nuanced vision of scientific knowledge: layered, local, and robust. It is a vision in which explanation and understanding are distributed across levels, and where realism, too, becomes effective.

Perhaps, then, the history of the universe cannot be captured by a single, unified narrative, but is better understood as a patchwork of overlapping accounts, each accurate within its own domain. Scientific knowledge, on this view, is not a towering edifice but a sprawling network—a constellation of bridges, buildings, and pathways, each serving a distinct purpose and together illuminating the broader landscape.

This mosaic view is not a concession to fragmentation. It is an acknowledgment of intellectual humility and a celebration of scientific creativity. And as effective field theories show us, this humility can yield some of the most powerful and predictive tools science has ever known.
