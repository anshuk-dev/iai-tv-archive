# Trump, Putin, and the return of the "great man" theory

*Individuals, not institutions, create history*

**By Rose McDermott** | *20th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,412 words*

![Trump, Putin, and the return of the "great man" theory](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/great-man-theory-2.webp)

---

*Thomas Carlyle famously declared that history is “the biography of great men,” a view long-since dismissed as we came to recognize the power of systemic economic and social conditions. But we underestimate the force of individual personalities at our peril, argues International Relations professor **Rose McDermott**. From Trump in Greenland to Putin in Ukraine, the psychologies and belief-systems of just a few individuals are driving geopolitics. To avoid catastrophic miscalculations, McDermott warns that we must stop relying on systemic models of change, and instead try to get into the heads of the leaders reshaping our world.*

If contemporary geopolitical events teach us anything, it is the power of individual leaders to exert decisive influence over the course of global history. Vladimir Putin’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine and Donald Trump’s recent attack on Venezuela represent only two of the most obvious examples of leaders who override international law and institutional norms to do what they want to do.

States are often seen as the central agents of change in international relations. Our dominant models and theories, like [realism](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Theory_of_International_Politics.html?id=j6qOAAAAMAAJ) and [liberalism](https://www.princeton.edu/~amoravcs/library/liberalism_working.pdf), suggest that state power, regime type, economic structure, and various other institutions are the primary influences on economic productivity, the balance of power, and war. These forces have indeed been powerful in helping shape interactions between states. Yet, if this century has taught us anything, it is that, contrary to the expectations of dominant theories of international relations, states can be captured by individuals whose idiosyncratic behavior exerts decisive influence on global affairs.

If this moment in time offers instruction about the future, it is simply this: individuals matter. This goes beyond the traditional idea that there are different [levels of analysis](https://doi.org/10.2307/2009557), which suggests that leaders can influence international outcomes through such mechanisms as their control over the instruments of state coercion. Individuals acting on their own and within the context of non-state actors can also prove decisive. One need only think of the role of Osama bin Laden in orchestrating the attacks on 9/11 against the United States. Furthermore, leaders who dominate their states, from Vladimir Putin in Russia to Donald Trump in the United States, demonstrate that specific features of personality and mere personal whim can impose enormous consequences on millions of people. This is particularly concerning when such leaders control nuclear weapons.

Critically, leaders who create [personalist](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055412000111) forms of power—whether through fear or by inspiring followers—fall prey to specific psychological limitations and biases such as narcissism and paranoia that have profound consequences for stability in international relations. And, in some cases, they can [manipulate their followers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2018.11.005) to achieve large-scale mass mobilization in their favor without requiring military force. Of course, some leaders use military force to support their regimes, but others can rely on followers within their constituencies to intimidate, harass and otherwise subdue domestic opponents without having to carry out the kind of military raids more closely associated with military juntas or some authoritarian regimes in the past.

The major threat posed by personalist leaders to the stability of international relations relates to those who retain singular control over nuclear weapons, such as Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin, Xi Jinping of China, or Kim Jung Un of North Korea. Russian doctrine may place some ostensible institutional safeguards on Vladimir Putin, but arguably nobody could really prevent their use if he were intent on launching such weapons. Furthermore, many of the leaders of states who have more recently acquired nuclear weapons, or states seeking such capacity, sit atop personalistic regimes. [Such leaders are less constrained](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/1bd32e5f-b6d4-49b1-8b60-00aa7129faa0/content) by institutional and state structures or public opinion than more democratic regimes. As a result, such authoritarian leaders have more freedom to allow their psychological proclivities of whatever form to take control of their decisions and behavior.

All this challenges our existing assumptions about the nature of strategic stability and nuclear deterrence. Personalist leaders fall prey to the kinds of psychological biases, including but not limited to narcissism, which have been demonstrated to lead to suboptimal decisions in numerous areas. Even if such leaders were no more likely to demonstrate such weaknesses than more democratic leaders, there would be fewer institutional or personal constraints on their behavior because of the nature of their personal control over the instruments of government. In addition, the introduction of new and potentially more dangerous weapons, including artificial intelligence and hypersonic missiles, heightens the risk of misperception, miscalculation, increased time pressure, and other factors exacerbating the degree of threat posed by the world’s nuclear powers.

Conventional wisdom around the stability of nuclear weapons rests on the notion that nations, and their leaders, are rational actors who will be deterred from the use of nuclear weapons by the assurance that such action will result in assured retaliation that will destroy their own interests and their very lives. This is the essence of the notion of Mutually Assured Destruction, upon which all our lives have depended since the dawn of the nuclear age. But this theory [rests on an enormous number of tenuous assumptions](https://iai.tv/articles/the-truth-about-nuclear-deterrence-auid-2078). For example, what if the leader is suicidal, as occurs so often with terrorists who employ suicide bombing as a technique? Would the prospect of death prove a deterrent to action? Unlikely. What if the leader did not believe the other side would be able to retaliate out of ignorance, misinformation, sheer arrogance, or narcissism? Such scenarios appear increasingly probable as the number of personalist regimes possessing or seeking weapons of mass destruction proliferates.

The [increasingly common populist form of leadership](https://iai.tv/video/the-politics-of-populism) represents another powerful new form of personalist governance that threatens the existing liberal international order. This matters because personalist leaders in general and populist ones in particular do not have random psychological characteristics drawn from population averages. If they did, they would not have been able to become such controlling leaders from the outset. Rather, they possess specific tendencies: they tend to be narcissistic, making them susceptible to manipulation by those willing to flatter them, and creating toxic work environments for those who serve them; they demonstrate paranoia, lashing out against political opponents who might offer resistance, both real and imagined; and they privilege loyalty over competence, often rendering their administrations rife with corruption and incompetence. These factors in combination make it exceedingly [difficult for such leaders to learn from their own or others’ mistakes](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/2666999?casa_token=wBakEiivmMgAAAAA:DiNf-eh1eciWuNuPFVFWmoitdDbzHi5Be4TipeF4Dh0SVgMD4w40RP0yn1hxCDAaItLIz3FNyRXK0HwFn9RWB1AEcvdNKyT43nQUHioHGczsPC1_5w), not least because they surround themselves with sycophants who are either unable or unwilling to tell them anything they do not want to hear.

This larger point gets to the heart of the deficit in current models and theories of international relations: the state is not the only major actor, or even the most important actor, in international relations. Individual actors, including suicide bombers, can throw a wrench into long-planned negotiations. Non-state actors can win victories over powerful militaries; no one can credibly claim that Russia or the United States “won” in Afghanistan, or that the United States did so in Iraq. In these cases, as in many others, large states with huge economies and powerful militaries were overcome by small bands of very committed individuals whose very lack of institutions and bureaucracies created an ability to pivot strategies quickly in a way that proved advantageous to their larger goals. The flexibility and consistency of non-state actors have repeatedly outperformed more militarily powerful but bureaucratically constrained and inconsistent leadership on the part of larger states.

To the extent that international relations theories focus on state behavior, and the attendant institutions and norms that characterize and support them, it loses sight of the currently most influential force in international relations: the ability of individuals, both leaders and followers, to challenge the constraints imposed by these states in effective ways. This means that existing dominant models in international relations remain blind to one of the most powerful forces of historical change: the individual. It is individual psychology and behavior that will define the future of international relations. International relations theorists may have been slow to recognize the true influence and agency of individual actors, privileging the more obvious variables of military and economic power, but ignoring individual influence going forward only renders us less capable of accurate description and prediction—and, more importantly, less capable of responding to the most influential forms of future threat.
