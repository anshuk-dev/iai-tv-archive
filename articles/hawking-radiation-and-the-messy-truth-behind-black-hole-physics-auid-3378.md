# Hawking radiation and the messy truth behind black hole physics

*Bringing our models closer to reality*

**By Eugene Chua** | *6th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 17 mins* | *3,492 words*

![Hawking radiation and the messy truth behind black hole physics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Hawking-radiation-and-the-messy-truth-behind-black-hole-physics.webp)

---

*Over the last century, science’s understanding of black holes has grown. What was thought to be an exotic speculation of theory is now a key test subject for modern physicists. However, as philosopher of physics Eugene Chua argues, the models we use to study black holes are shrouded in as yet untested assumptions. Most crucially, those that allow us to define energy conservation, which formed the basis of Hawking’s argument for black hole evaporation. Only as we de-idealize our perfect models, removing our misplaced assumptions to better align them with our imperfect world, can they become meaningful descriptions of a very messy reality.*

**1. Perfect black holes and spherical horses**

In his 1983 *Mathematical Theory of Black Holes*, physicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar remarked that “the black holes of nature are the most perfect macroscopic objects there are in the universe.” However, their perfection may come at the cost of *idealization*—the introduction, into our models, of assumptions often known to be false. As an old joke goes, physicists can predict the winner of any horse race, provided the horses were spherical and moved through a vacuum. But can a model relying on perfect spherical horses be used to make inferences about imperfect equine-shaped systems in the real world? Depending on context, not all idealizations are made equal, and it’s worth checking whether, or when, our idealizations have been put to use beyond their limits.

This demand for oversight was termed “de-idealization” by philosopher of science [Ernan McMullin](https://doi.org/10.1016/0039-3681(85)90003-2) in 1985. The idea is simple: to make justifiable inferences about some feature of a target system using an idealization, we must have a grasp of what happens to this feature when this idealization is removed, and how something like this feature approximately obtains thereafter—see also the work of philosopher [John Earman](https://doi.org/10.1080/0269859042000311299). For instance, to de-idealize the spherical horse is (at least) to ask what happens if horses were not exactly spherical, or if the medium of motion is not exactly a vacuum. In short, de-idealization asks the question: when we add the imperfections of reality back into our perfect models, what happens then? A simple question may be hard to answer: the very idea of de-idealization has come under fire in recent years. Some think that [it is too difficult and unrealistically assumes knowledge of ‘the real world’ in its full glory](https://philarchive.org/rec/KNUDNE), while others think that [scientific practice often do not aim at de-idealization](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/I/bo27128726.html).

**2. Idealizations and black hole physics**

Among their varied purposes, idealizations simplify computations and allow us to derive solutions to complicated equations which may otherwise be beyond analytic reach. It is therefore unsurprising that black hole physics—relying on the highly non-linear Einstein Field Equations—employs its own spherical horses.

\_\_\_

While local conservation of energy is baked into the heart of general relativity, this property does not include gravitational energy. Therefore, to talk meaningfully about the conservation of total energy (including gravitational energy), we must rely on idealizations.

\_\_\_

Consider the very first non-trivial general relativistic model (and also the first model of a black hole, though it wasn’t necessarily apparent at that time). It was found by Schwarzschild in 1916, just a month or so after Einstein published the theory. Schwarzschild employed a host of simplifying assumptions: it is a spacetime describing the gravitation of an isolated, unchanging, spherically symmetric point mass without charge or spin.

*Flamm’s parabaloid: Depiction of the spatial curvature around mass in* Schwarzschild model. (Source [Wikimedia Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_metric#Flamm's_paraboloid))

In the century since, the Schwarzschild model has been a crucial arena for a range of foundational results, such as the very existence of an event horizon and singularity, tests of general relativity like gravitational lensing and the perihelion of Mercury, as well as Hawking radiation and black hole evaporation. While the Schwarzschild model can be generalized to a much broader family of black hole models with spin and charge (so-called Kerr-Newman black holes), two key idealizations remain: *stationarity*, the time-independence of the black hole’s parameters, and *asymptotic flatness*, the requirement that the curvature of a black hole spacetime becomes flat at spatial infinity.

Stationarity and asymptotic flatness are crucial idealizations, especially if we want to make arguments resting on global conservation of energy. While *local* conservation of energy is baked into the heart of general relativity, this property does not include gravitational energy. Therefore, to talk meaningfully about the conservation of total energy (including gravitational energy), we must rely on idealizations.

One idealization is stationarity. Only a stationary spacetime admits a time-like Killing vector field—mathematically where, the spacetime geometry as a whole doesn’t change along a time-like direction. In simpler terms, a Killing field captures a symmetry of spacetime itself: nudging spacetime a tiny step in time along this Killing field preserves all local distances and time intervals leaving the system unchanged. By [Noether’s theorem](https://profoundphysics.com/noethers-theorem-a-complete-guide/), this time-translation symmetry yields a conserved quantity: a global energy associated with all of spacetime, defined relative to the Killing field. Crucially, in generic relativistic (dynamical) spacetimes where no such time-like Killing field exists, there is no analogous globally conserved energy.

\_\_\_

We expect our black hole models to apply not only to isolated spherically symmetric masses in empty space, but also black holes in our world, surrounded by accretion disks, other celestial bodies, and other objects.

\_\_\_

Another common idealization is asymptotic flatness: roughly, that space becomes flat as we go to (null or spatial) infinity. If spacetime is asymptotically flat, then, at this infinitely far, exactly flat region, the asymptotic symmetries (associated, again, with asymptotic Killing fields) here allow us to define a conserved total energy for an isolated system—captured by quantities such as the [ADM mass or Bondi mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_general_relativity).

Just as the spherical horse raises questions of de-idealization, we should also ask how spacetimes with these conditions—commonly used in modelling and defining energy—can be de-idealized. This question is particularly crucial because we expect our black hole models to apply not only to isolated spherically symmetric masses in empty space, but also black holes in our world, surrounded by accretion disks, other celestial bodies, and other objects. If these models necessarily require the use of such idealizations—and we cannot de-idealize—then an epistemic question arises as to whether we are justified in making inferences about actual black holes using these idealized models.

**3. Can unchanging things evaporate?**

[In a recent paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-025-00638-4), I considered one prominent argument which rests on global conservation of energy—and hence the aforementioned idealizations: Hawking’s original argument for black hole evaporation via Hawking radiation. Here’s a sketch of the argument. First, grant that black holes emit Hawking radiation. Then, [as Hawking explains](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.13.191), “Because this radiation carries away energy, the black holes must presumably lose mass and eventually disappear.” [Wald relies on conservation reasoning more explicitly](https://link.springer.com/article/10.12942/lrr-2001-6): “Conservation of energy requires that an isolated black hole must lose mass in order to compensate for the energy radiated to infinity by the Hawking process.” Such arguments require global conservation of energy; as [David Wallace](https://philpapers.org/rec/WALTCF-9) notes, “given that there is no robust local definition of gravitational energy and, relatedly, no robust way to understand total energy as a sum of local energies, we cannot simply appeal to a local conservation law to conclude that radiating black holes evaporate.”

\_\_\_

Removing stationarity rules out evaporation; retaining stationarity also rules out evaporation. The idealization of stationarity thus appears to resist de-idealization.

\_\_\_

As I pointed out earlier, global conservation of energy is guaranteed only with the assumptions of stationarity or asymptotic flatness. In Hawking’s original argument, he relied especially on the idealization of stationarity to motivate conservation reasoning. Of course, this leads immediately to a paradox: on the one hand, if the black hole in consideration is stationary, then it has a globally conserved energy. But this black hole, of course, cannot evaporate over time—it is time-independent. On the other hand, if the black hole in consideration is *non*-stationary (such as a Vaidya spacetime), then it is free to change over time. But this black hole lacks a time-like Killing field and does not have a conserved energy, so that the original argument for black hole evaporation cannot take off. Removing stationarity rules out evaporation; retaining stationarity also rules out evaporation. The idealization of stationarity thus appears to resist de-idealization.

Hawking himself was well aware of this tension. To adapt the Schwarzschild model for dynamical modeling and sidestep the paradox above, Hawking (1975) had to adopt a new idealization: *quasi-stationarity*. Today, this idealization continues to be adopted for the purposes of modeling, albeit often implicitly via remarks such as the black hole of interest ‘slowly evolving’, or simply using stationary metrics to model black hole dynamics. As Hawking puts it, because the mass of the black hole is large relative to the Planck mass, the black hole’s evolution is much slower than the rate of radiation across the Schwarzschild radius. Hence, “it is a reasonable approximation to describe the black hole by a sequence of stationary solutions and to calculate the rate of particle emission in each solution.”

\_\_\_

Quasi-stationarity is, itself, an idealization that requires de-idealization.

\_\_\_

That is, although a black hole is truly changing with time, we should assume that it changes so slowly that over any very short interval it can be treated *as if unchanging*. Think of a flipbook: each page is static, but the sequence shows motion. Here, each page is a stationary spacetime with some mass (think ‘page number’) with a timelike Killing field, in which there is a well-defined conserved energy. Within a page, Hawking’s emission argument goes through, motivating us to consider evaporation, i.e., energy loss, and to decrease the mass. This provides an argument for flipping to the next page. The sequence of pages flipping from one to the other, with each page a stationary spacetime, is a quasi-stationary process.

Can de-idealization now proceed? A new challenge appears to arise: these perturbations cannot happen *within* any particular stationary model in this sequence of models, since stationary models do not change over time by definition. We seem to run into the same puzzle as before: either each page is stationary and so does not change, or each page is not stationary and the argument for evaporation cannot take off. Quasi-stationarity is, itself, an idealization that requires de-idealization.

> **4. Approximately Killing is not quite Killing**

One strategy is to generalize the notion of Killing fields. Instead of demanding an exact time-like Killing field in each stage of the quasi-stationary process, i.e. requiring literal stationarity at each time, we demand only *approximate* stationarity, i.e. an approximate time-like Killing field, so that the system approximately has a conserved energy via Noether’s theorem. Then we run the argument for evaporation relying on approximate conservation of energy.

But what does it mean to say that a particular spacetime has an approximate Killing field? There is no canonical “perfect ruler” in General Relativity against which we measure deviations from symmetry. The closest thing is a global Minkowski metric, in which space is flat and nothing happens all the time. This means that there is already likely no one-size-fits-all notion of approximate symmetries represented by Killing fields.

But there are more specific worries. While there are many procedures for generating “the next best thing” to Killing fields—which are sometimes called approximate Killing fields or almost-Killing fields—these procedures generally do not provide a metric for quantifying how close the next best thing is to “being Killing,” only providing solutions that are *closest* to being Killing. But note that being the closest to something says very little about how close one is to that thing. That the closest In-N-Out is in California is little solace when I write from Singapore. But it’s a little worse here, because in these procedures there is no notion of “distance” provided between Killing fields and their next best counterpart. As [Matzner](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/jmp/article-abstract/9/10/1657/898578/Almost-Symmetric-Spaces-and-Gravitational), who provided one prominent procedure for approximating Killing fields notes, “we do not have to assume the deviation from symmetry is small”. In my paper, I suggest that similar problems persist even in more contemporary approaches. So, it seems that approximate Killing fields, as a way to de-idealize quasi-stationarity, face some formidable technical challenges.

\_\_\_

We need not be pessimistic about this situation. Rather, recognizing the conceptual limit of global symmetries is, itself, a crucial piece of knowledge that may help us locate new terrain on which further progress must be made.

\_\_\_

Now, as I mentioned earlier, asymptotic flatness can also independently motivate a globally conserved energy. Perhaps these worries with approximate Killing fields suggest we should abandon the idealization of stationarity, in favor of adopting asymptotic flatness instead. However, now we have passed the buck for de-idealization: can realistic spacetimes be shown to rigorously approximate an asymptotic flat one, as we’d hoped with approximate Killing fields?

**5. Flat enough, far enough?**

Typical asymptotically flat spacetimes are also stationary spacetimes, so the same paradox for the quasi-stationary process returns. At each stage, the system is either stationary, or not. Either way, we cannot yet proceed with the argument for evaporation. [Duerr](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-019-02503-3) furthermore points out that the general class of *non*-stationary asymptotically flat spacetimes is marred by singularities, yet another thing often taken to be unphysical.

What if we, again, took each stage of the quasi-stationary process to be only approximately asymptotically flat, rather than exactly so? Recall that each stage of the quasi-stationary process must support the argument from global conservation of energy to evaporation, and we, again, need a notion of a global energy. Since approximate Killing fields are not readily available, we must appeal to some oth.er notion. One might hope that asymptotically defined energies, such as the ADM mass, could play such a role. However, the ADM mass, as a globally conserved energy, is defined *at* spatial infinity—the integral crucially requires the symmetries of flat spacetime at spatial infinity, i.e. Killing fields defined at infinity. To talk about approximate conservation of ADM mass, again, requires approximate Killing fields whose problems I already briefly discussed above. This raises a significant challenge for de-idealizing asymptotic flatness.

Furthermore, in our best cosmological models, we live not in an asymptotically flat spacetime with a vanishing cosmological constant Λ, but in an asymptotically de Sitter spacetime with a positive Λ: one with positive spacetime curvature—not flat!—at infinite distances. So one should not expect results from asymptotically flat models to carry over wholesale—if at all—into asymptotically de Sitter models. One prominent example: unlike asymptotically flat spacetimes, asymptotically de Sitter spacetimes do not have the right behavior at infinity for defining global energies such as ADM or Bondi mass: there are no time-like killing fields at infinity.

With an eye on Λ, one might think that de-idealization would be straightforward. After all, a problem with approximate Killing fields earlier was that we lacked a precise metric for how close any particular spacetime is to having a Killing field. Here we have, prima facie, precisely one such parameter, Λ, as a surrogate for how an asymptotically de Sitter spacetime approximates asymptotically flat spacetime as Λ→0. Unfortunately, as [Ashtekar et al](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.116.051101) and [Koberinski & Smeenk](https://iai.tv/articles/cosmic-inflation-the-paradigm-without-a-theory-auid-3028) point out, the Λ→0 limit is discontinuous, crucially, in the energy, suggesting no sense in which the concept of energy in the two spacetimes can be “arbitrarily close” to each other, i.e. approximate each other. The two spacetimes are qualitatively different, so that the asymptotics of our world are simply different from those required by asymptotically flat models.

I mentioned earlier that the Schwarzschild model remains an important arena for many of general relativity’s most important results, such as gravitational lensing. But this model is both stationary and asymptotically flat, and I’ve been arguing that these idealizations do not yet have viable de-idealizations. If a lack of de-idealization suggests a lack of justification, does our argument go too far in ruling out the use of the Schwarzschild model tout court?

Not necessarily. This situation highlights a crucial feature of de-idealization: its success or failure depends on the purpose of modelling. Many classic tests of general relativity—gravitational lensing, the perihelion of Mercury, Shapiro delay—depend only on curvature in a *finite* region around the source. These phenomena occur relatively close to massive objects like the Sun, and do not essentially rely on the features of asymptotically flat spacetimes infinitely far away. The literally flat region is, in [the words of Duerr](https://philpapers.org/rec/DUETIA), an “idle posit” in these use cases since “all empirical content is garnered from the properties of a finite patch of an asymptotically flat spacetime.”

To see this, note that these features—which require only finite regions around the source—can be recovered even in asymptotically de Sitter spacetimes (such as Schwarzschild-de Sitter spacetimes)—a successful de-idealization for the use of the Schwarzschild model in modeling these features. However, the challenge for the argument for evaporation is that it relies on a globally conserved energy defined via symmetries *at* spatial *infinity*, so that the success of de-idealizations for features in this ‘finite zone’ does not necessarily carry over to the ‘infinite zone’. Similar worries may also apply to other arguments that rely on the conservation of global energy.

**6. De-idealization and progress through failure**

De-idealization appears to fail for the use of stationary or asymptotically flat metrics, for the purposes of defending black hole evaporation. For this purpose, the appeal to global conservation of energy may have hit a conceptual limit (though, of course, we *could* find a novel way of defining approximate Killing fields or global energies). This, in my view, is the power of de-idealization: it can help us discover the conceptual limits of our idealized models. We need not be pessimistic about this situation.  Rather, recognizing the conceptual limit of global symmetries is, itself, a crucial piece of knowledge that may help us locate new terrain on which further progress must be made. It can teach us when—and when not—to use such models. To put a positive spin on Samuel Beckett’s *Worstward Ho* (with emphasis on *better,* not *failed*)*,* “Ever tried. Ever failed. No matter. Try again. Fail again. Fail better.”

\_\_\_

To quote Holly Andersen, the process of “true-ing” our idealizations—the process of de-idealizing in order to bring perfect models into closer accord with the imperfect world—must be an ongoing, active enterprise.

\_\_\_

One promising route is to develop so-called “quasi-local” energies. Rather than defining energy globally or asymptotically, such approaches define a conserved energy on finite regions, allowing us to talk sensibly about conservation of energy—of matter *and* gravity—without presupposing global or asymptotic symmetries (see e.g. [Szabados for a review of quasi-local energies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.12942/lrr-2009-4)). This gets us further than the global approach sketched here.

For now, the promise is tempered by two unresolved difficulties. First, there are many alternatives, and they disagree with each other—like the case of [relativistic temperature](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/t-falls-apart-on-the-status-of-classical-temperature-in-relativity/43CE3D77A75E3BF5DE46BF37F5671AE1). Energy is a concept that appears to fragment in relativity, with no clear route to reconciliation. Second, even when a definition is fixed, consistently relating conserved quantities across adjoining regions usually demands additional structure—one such structure is precisely the Killing field. These are not insurmountable problems and may very well be resolved in the future. All this is to say that there remain many open questions in the foundations of black hole physics which may help us continue our quest for de-idealization, and [the quest continues to this day](https://youtu.be/1VsjyExcIkk?si=AP4FCACSoAoj21ki).

Ultimately, the moral here is methodological. To quote [Holly Andersen](https://philarchive.org/rec/ANDT-3), the process of “true-ing” our idealizations—the process of de-idealizing in order to bring perfect models into closer accord with the imperfect world—must be an ongoing, active enterprise. Some philosophers proclaim that we simply do not have a “full” or “fundamental” picture of reality with which to judge and fully de-idealize our best idealizations. Fair enough. Yet, in the most practical of senses, we must continually aim at checking the limits of these idealizations and how close (or far) they come to the imperfections of reality we *do* know – such as non-stationarity, a lack of asymptotic flatness, and so on. In another novel, *The Unnamable,* Samuel Beckett wrote: “… you must go on. I can’t go on. I’ll go on.” To make progress, we must go on *somehow*, even if we can’t go on in the fullest sense. Once we see that we need not de-idealize in the fullest sense to de-idealize, we may also ask whether the search for technical limiting procedures is the only way for models to come close to reality. If we relaxed our notion of closeness, by employing other less stringent tools such as [conceptual continuities, analogies, or sustained convergence of predictions to measurements](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ToUJx7ygQwI), we may be able to find new ways of de-idealizing as well. Although we may never learn precisely *all* the limits of our idealizations in most cases, the quest of de-idealization lets us discover more ways in which our idealizations may fail; in turn, we learn when and why they may succeed.

From this perspective, my discussion here simply highlights that de-idealization is an ongoing process, not something to be resolved once and for all. As the old Neurath’s boat metaphor suggests, we are at sea with an imperfect boat, constantly borrowing pieces from one part of the boat to plug holes at the other. Science is always work in progress, and it is no failure, nor is it a problem, to acknowledge failures in de-idealization; it simply means that more work needs to be done.
