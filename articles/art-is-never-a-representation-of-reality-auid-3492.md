# Art is never a representation of reality

*There is no neutral way to depict the world*

**By Derek Allan** | *10th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,458 words*

![Art is never a representation of reality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/art-can-never-represent-reality.webp)

---

*Art is often seen as a representation of reality, with style understood as a way of dressing the same neutral content in different forms. But philosopher **Derek Allan** argues that no such neutral content exists. Even photographs, silhouettes, or bare outlines already involve interpretation, and when stripped of style, images often become unintelligible rather than more “realistic.” Art, he argues, is an act of transformation that creates a rival world rather than mirroring the existing one.*

The question of whether or not art is essentially [a representation of reality](https://iai.tv/articles/the-fantasy-of-reality-auid-1284) has long been a bone of contention among philosophers of art, especially in the major branch of that discipline called the analytic philosophy of art, or analytic aesthetics. Some philosophers seem convinced that art *is* essentially representation, although they’re usually forced to resort to rather tortuous reasoning when it comes to music or non-figurative visual art, neither of which seem to represent anything in any usual sense of the word. Other philosophers, apparently struggling for a compromise, argue that while literature and visual art are representative arts, music is not—a claim that, unhappily, begs a question: if there are “representative arts” and “non-representative” arts, what common feature do they have that authorises us to call them both “art”? Representation being ruled out, we need to find a replacement essential feature.

I don’t wish to venture down the highways and byways of these debates. They’ve been going on, more or less fruitlessly, for decades now and are, in any case, among the more tedious areas of analytic aesthetics, which, at the best of times, tends to be a rather tedious affair anyway. Yet I suppose we do have to admit one thing. There is a tendency for many of us to assume, more or less unthinkingly, that art—especially visual art and literature—does, in some ill-defined way, “represent the world.” We struggle to apply the proposition to music (although some argue that so-called “[program music](https://www.britannica.com/art/program-music)” is representational) but it’s nonetheless hard to rid oneself of the belief that paintings like the *Mona Lisa*, or Titian’s *Man with a Glove*, for example, “represent” certain people, or that novels like *War and Peace* or Balzac’s *P**è**re Goriot*—“represent” life at a certain period of history. And from there, it seems only a short step to assume that representation is what art is really about.

Nevertheless, it is this belief that I plan to challenge here, building my case on an excellent analysis by André Malraux in one of his major works on the theory of art—[*The Voices of Silence*](https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.1702/mode/2up)—a work, incidentally, that philosophers of art rarely read and whose existence they often seem unaware of. Malraux does not, of course, argue that art—visual art, for example— never represents people or objects in an everyday sense of the word. Such a claim would obviously be untenable. But he does argue—and argues quite passionately—that the *essential purpose* of art is not to represent the world, whether or not, as in the case of the paintings just mentioned*,* something is plainly represented.

Let me begin in a down-to-earth way by considering three images. One is the magnificent image of [a horse in the Lascaux caves](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/lascaux/en/mediatheque/das-dritte-chinesische-pferd), painted some 19,000 years ago and often reproduced; the second is Rubens’ spectacular baroque image of *[St George and the Dragon](https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/saint-george-and-the-dragon/dea0a4cc-110e-425e-a8e2-bdb37444d26c?searchid=792c6767-2e0f-0415-d9b9-91b1a37fb074)*, with St George mounted on a rearing horse; and the third is a fairly unremarkable black and white photograph of a horse seen in profile.

![figure 1 horses](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCwzMzdd/figure-1-horses.webp)

**Figure 1: three images of horses (Lascaux, Rubens, photograph)**

Now, at one level, it seems perfectly reasonable to say that these three images all represent horses, each in a different style—the style of the unknown Palaeolithic painter, the flamboyant style of Rubens, and the style of a photograph. And if that seems a reasonable observation, one might contend that the three images provide different stylistic renderings of an underlying, *stylistically neutral* representation of a horse—that is, an underlying representation free not only ofthese three styles, but of any specific style—a basic, style-less representation, so to speak.

\_\_\_

Don’t we often tend to assume, even if vaguely, that if we were somehow to remove this something “added,” we would be left with the basic, style-free image of the object?

\_\_\_

Seen in this light, styles would, as André Malraux argues, be understood as “successive varieties of ornament added to an immutable substratum”—that is, an “added layer” which, in theory at least, could be jettisoned altogether if the artist so desired. This tempting idea is what Malraux calls “the fallacy of a neutral style.” In visual art, he writes, “it has been assumed for centuries that there exists a styleless, ‘photographic’ kind of drawing (though we know now that every photograph has its share of style) which would serve as the foundation of a work, style being something added. This is the fallacy of a neutral style.”

Yet, the fallacy seems plausible enough on the surface, does it not? Don’t we often tend to assume that the specific style in which an object is portrayed is something “added” as Malraux writes—so that Rubens, for example, adds a more colourful and dramatic rendering of his horse than the Lascaux painter and the camera? And don’t we often tend to assume, even if vaguely, that if we were somehow to remove this something “added,” we would be left with the basic, style-free image of the object—the “immutable substratum” to borrow Malraux’s phrase?  If I am ruthlessly honest, I suspect I had a tendency to think that way myself, not only about visual art but literature as well, assuming that each novelist, for example, adds his or her particular style to a kind of underlying “purely objective” version which serves as the foundation for the final product.

Malraux encourages us question these assumptions. The basis of the notion of the neutral style, he writes, is the idea that a living model can be copied “without any interpretation or expression.” But in reality, he writes,

*No such copy has ever been made. In drawing, this notion can be applied only to a small range of subjects [such as] a standing horse seen in profile… Can one imagine a drawing of a rearing horse, seen from in front, in a style that is not that of any school, or of any innovator?*

The notion of a style-less drawing, he continues,

*springs in large measure from the idea of the silhouette: the basic neutral style in drawing would be the bare outline. But any such method, if strictly followed, would not lead to any form of art, but would stand in the same relation to drawing as an art as the bureaucratic style stands to literature.*

The claim can be easily tested. I took my cue from Malraux and chose a photograph of a rearing horse seen from the front, reducing the image to a silhouette by a little photoshopping. The result borders on the unintelligible unless one has the photograph close by for reference. My image shows the horse “without any interpretation or expression” to borrow Malraux’s words, but it is not a “style-less” image of a horse; it is little more than a meaningless blob.

**Figure 2: image of a horse “without any interpretation or expression”**

This analysis demolishes the proposition that art is essentially a form of representation. Philosophers of art define the idea of representation in various ways, not all of which are helpful, but, at their core, they all imply a close and direct relationship between the work of art and what one might loosely call “the outside world” or “reality.” If art is essentially representation, the arguments run, its function relies heavily on a capacity to reflect that world—to hold a mirror up to nature, in Hamlet’s words.

\_\_\_

To the extent that it is even conceivable, a neutral style would not be a pure, unalloyed realism but a form of depiction that had abandoned all but the last vestiges of the procedures available to art.

\_\_\_

Seen in this light, the essential task of the artist is to effect a kind of transposition or transcription of the outside world, or “reality,” onto the surface of a canvas or into the pages of a novel. Not surprisingly, this thinking often goes hand in hand with the claim that a prime virtue of the true representational artist is “faithfulness to reality.” And taking the next logical step, one might even argue that a *neutral style* that would transcribe reality with maximum faithfulness to nature, and minimum “interference” by the artist’s style—or even none at all—might be a real possibility.

Malraux is arguing that thinking of this kind rests on a misunderstanding. To the extent that it is even conceivable, a neutral style would not be a pure, unalloyed realism but a form of depiction that had abandoned all but the last vestiges of the procedures available to art.  In visual art, it would be, at best, the bare outline or the silhouette. In literature, it would lead merely to the commercial or bureaucratic style where, in a similar way, language tends towards a lim­ited range of standard, “lifeless” forms. To the extent it is conceivable, in other words, a neutral style would result at best in the *sign*—that is, to those limited uses of visual forms or language that merely suggest, or “point to,” living forms—as a profile silhouette of a standing horse might be used to indicate the presence of horses—but would stop well short of *portraying* any such form, as Rubens or our Palaeolithic painter do.

Yet if art is not essentially representation, what is it? To explore Malraux’s answer, one needs to approach our topic from a slightly different angle. In another section of *The Voices of Silence*, Malraux reminds us of an obvious fact about art that we often tend to overlook. He points out that painting, for example, always involves a process of reduction—that is, a process in which the painter reduces a world of three dimensions to one of only two. This being so, the painter’s task inevitably requires a process of selection, exclusion, adding and re-ordering—in short, a process of *transformation*—a process in which, even if the aim is to create an illusion of a three-dimensional reality (as in Renaissance art, for instance), he or she is not transcribing or transposing the outside world, but *transforming* it, that is, quite literally creating another world*,* a world *different in kind* from the world in which we live and move.

One might object that this argument does not hold good for sculpture since here the artist is not obliged to reduce three dimensions to two, and can, if necessary, make an exact replica of an object. But sculpture, Malraux reminds us, also involves a process of reduction—a reduction of “movement, implicit or portrayed, to immobility.” And although “we can imagine a still life carved and painted to look exactly like its model,” he writes, “we cannot conceive of its being a work of art. Imitation apples in an imitation bowl are not a true work of sculpture.” Which is why, he adds interestingly, “colours applied to sculpture so rarely imitate those of the real world; and why everyone feels that wax figures (the only forms in our time that are completely illusionist) have nothing to do with art.” Transformation, in other words, is no less important in the case of sculpture.

\_\_\_

Art always involves the creation of another world—a *rival* world that depends for its very existence on a process of transformation of bare "reality" or “the outside world.”

\_\_\_

One can also see fairly readily how the idea of reduction applies to literature, since here again one is not dealing with transcription of real-life events—as in a tape recording or a typed court record, for example—but with a *selection* of incidents, kinds of characters, vocab­ulary and so on. And although Malraux does not explore the idea in relation to music, it is not difficult to see how it applies there as well. [Music “reduces” the world of sounds to its separate constituents](https://iai.tv/articles/the-musical-structure-behind-quantum-physics-auid-3393)—pitch, rhythm, tempo, etc.—and in doing so enables the creation of “another world” of sound.

Despite what some critics have claimed, Malraux intends none of this as an *attack* on “representational” art—a suggestion which, incidentally, would be very odd given his admiration for many works of a representational nature, ranging from medieval and Buddhist sculpture to Titian and Rembrandt, to modern painters such as Cézanne and Van Gogh. But in any case, the issue here is not about individual works and whether art that represents might somehow be better or worse than art that doesn’t—a question that Malraux would doubtless have regarded as absurd.

The issue here is the fundamental nature and purpose of art. And in this respect, Malraux fully accepts that representation, in the simple sense of including forms resembling real objects in a picture, is one of the *tools or techniques* available to art—like the varied uses of line or color—which an artist may or may not wish to use. As a form of endeavor—as a certain kind of human achievement—Malraux is, however, contending that art is never essentially representation. “It is for the non-artist, not the artist,” he writes, “that painting is only a form of represent­ation.” Art always involves the creation of another world—a *rival* world that depends for its very existence on a process of [transformation of bare “reality](https://iai.tv/video/a-field-guide-to-reality)” or “the outside world.”

A theory of this kind, it is worth adding, offers a much clearer and more coherent explanation of the notion of style than the theory that art is essentially representation. If the goal of art is simply to represent—to somehow transcribe the outside world onto a canvas or into a novel—what, after all, is the function of style? Style in that case begins, as we have seen, to look suspiciously like a potential source of *interference*—perhaps of distortion—and it is perhaps no accident that philosophers of art who claim that art is essentially representation typically skirt around the question of style, or fall back on variants of the idea that it is something “added” or a variety of “ornament.”

The proposition that art exists to create another world, by contrast, gives the idea of style a clear and robust meaning. Styles are the very fabric of art; they are the different ways in which the artist accomplishes the transformation that creates his or her rival world. In some cases, this might include representation of real objects; in others, it may not. But in all cases, style is never merely something “added”; it is the indispensable means through which the artist [createsanother world](https://iai.tv/articles/we-do-not-discover-reality-we-create-it-auid-3475), “no less necessary,” Malraux writes, “when the artist is aiming at unlikeness than when he aims at lifelikeness.” The role of representation then becomes clear. “We are beginning to under­stand,” he writes, “that representation is one of the devices of style, instead of thinking that style is a means of representation.”
