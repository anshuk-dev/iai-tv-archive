# Quantum mechanics rules out a final theory of the universe

*The idea of the universe as a whole doesn’t make sense*

**By Ali Barzegar** | *7th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,811 words*

![Quantum mechanics rules out a final theory of the universe](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/quantum-mechanics-rules-out-a-final-theory-of-the-universe.webp)

---

*Some physicists such as Sean Carroll argue that the entire universe can be described as just a quantum wave function, the basic mathematics underlying quantum mechanics. But LMU Munich philosopher of science, **Ali Barzegar**, argues that quantum mechanics supports a very different cosmological story, according to which there can be no final description of “the universe as a whole.” For, he argues, quantum mechanics shows that any description of a system is always a description of one system relative to another. But if the universe is everything, then there is no system outside the universe, and so no description of the universe as a whole is possible.*

Quantum mechanics is one of the most successful theories in the history of science. The wavefunction is the central element of its mathematical formalism. It is used to calculate the probabilities of obtaining different outcomes when measurements are performed on a physical system. In this sense, the wavefunction can be viewed as a catalogue that encodes the probabilistic predictions for all possible measurements on the system. More precisely, in general a wavefunction represents a superposition of the possible states associated with different measurement outcomes. The coefficients of this superposition determine the probabilities of obtaining those outcomes when a measurement is made.

Now, if a system is left to itself without intervention on our part, its wavefunction will evolve unitarily in a superposition until we make a measurement on the system. Upon the measurement, the old wavefunction suddenly is updated to a new wavefunction corresponding to the outcome of the measurement. This update is called “[the collapse or reduction of the wavefunction](https://iai.tv/video/the-collapse-of-the-wave-function)” upon measurement. However, these two processes are incompatible within the formalism of quantum mechanics. As long as there is unitary evolution of the wavefunction in a superposition there is no collapse and so no measurement outcome. Moreover, there is nothing in the formalism telling us exactly when and where the unitary evolution breaks down and collapse occurs. This is the famous “[measurement problem](https://iai.tv/articles/we-still-dont-understand-the-measurement-problem-auid-2144)” of quantum mechanics.

Generally, there are [two main sorts of interpretative approaches to the measurement problem](https://iai.tv/articles/100-years-of-quantum-mechanics-a-new-approach-is-needed-auid-3479). As the wavefunction is the central element of quantum mechanical formalism, these two approaches come down to two different views on the nature of the wavefunction.

**Does quantum mechanics describe reality?**

Mathematically speaking, the wavefunction lives in a higher dimensional space called the configuration space. For example, if our system of interest consists of three particles then its wavefunction lives in a nine-dimensional configuration space, as you need three variables to specify the position of each particle. Generally, the wavefunction of a system composed of n particles lives in a 3n-dimensional configuration space. Now, an important question arises: what elements of the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics (QM) correspond to reality? In particular, does the wavefunction living in a higher dimensional configuration space [correspond to something real](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-just-a-quantum-wave-function-auid-2024)? Einstein was skeptical about this: “the field in a many-dimensional coordinate space does not smell like something real.” The epistemic interpretations of QM, following Einstein, regard the wavefunction, [not as something real](https://iai.tv/articles/the-world-is-not-a-quantum-wave-function-auid-3096), but as representing our information, knowledge or beliefs. On the other hand, the ontic interpretations regard the wavefunction as part of the furniture of the world, in direct correspondence with elements of reality, thus a real physical quantity in itself. [The Many-Worlds Interpretation](https://iai.tv/video/are-there-really-many-worlds), [Bohmian Mechanics](https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.85.166), and spontaneous collapse models are popular examples of the ontic camp. Taking the wavefunction as real, commits us to regard the high-dimensional configuration space as the real fundamental physical space in which we live and the familiar three-dimensional space as an illusion or an appearance. Now, I don’t mean to say there is no hope to argue for such a radical view. But it clearly has quite bizarre conceptual consequences for our worldview and our place in the world.

However, this strange ontic picture is not forced upon us. There is another option available: [the epistemic interpretation](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10701-024-00804-3), according to which the wavefunction encodes the knowledge, information or beliefs that an “observing” system has about (its past/future interactions with) the “observed” system, and is a “tool” for predicting the results of future interactions with it. An important consequence of this epistemic reading of quantum states is that the notorious “wavefunction collapse” following a measurement is interpreted, rather straightforwardly, as the update of the knowledge of the observing system upon acquiring new information. When we make a measurement on or interact with a system, we acquire new information and thus have to update our old wavefunction into a new one in light of the evidence.

Epistemic interpretations can be divided into two categories. One can regard the epistemic character of the wavefunction as a result of our limited information about an underlying hidden ontic state of reality. The idea here is that every system at any moment has a determinate state with definite values for all of its properties represented by physical quantities in our theoretical framework. However, this determinate state is hidden from us: we do not have complete knowledge about it. Thus, the wavefunction expresses our incomplete knowledge about this underlying definite hidden state. A view like this matches standard scientific realism, according to which scientific theories discover and represent an already structured and agent-independent world. This can be brought in line with the ontic interpretations of the wavefunction like Bohmian Mechanics and the Many-Worlds Interpretation, which are realist interpretations. The only challenge of QM here is the epistemological challenge of how we could know, access and model this underlying hidden world. At the ontological level, everything is pre-structured and agent-independent. This tends towards a traditional form of realism in terms of an absolute structure of reality that physical theories should represent from a God’s-eye point of view. We are only detached observers or onlookers in such a world, witnessing how it unfolds independently of us.

**Reality consists of relations**

However, there is a second class of epistemic interpretations of QM, according to which the wavefunction is *both* a complete characterization of the physical system *and* encodes the knowledge of the observer about that system. On this interpretation, we should refrain from postulating any hidden ontic structure or an observer-independent state of affairs. QM deals only with the perspective of a particular system when it enters into a relationship with other systems. In other words, QM is about the *relation* between two pieces of the world, and this relation is an irreducible, constitutive component of reality and of any characterization of “physical systems.” This interaction is what is usually called a “measurement” in QM.

On this view, QM points at a world of interactions and relations. Since what is fundamentally real is only such relations, reality is in a specific sense created or at least continuously shaped and moulded by the interaction between the two involved physical systems. This is a “[participatory](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.04360)” and “relational” realism: we have a [reality that consists of relations](https://iai.tv/articles/the-quantum-world-reveals-reality-is-made-of-relations-not-objects-auid-3501), in which physical systems acquire properties (and thus, in a sense, become real) only when they enter into relationships with each other. Since the values of physical quantities are not pre-determined, we bring them about in the act of measurement or observation, which thus can be regarded as a creative act.

This participatory realism is more radical than perspectival realism. According to perspectival realism, there can be relations out there independent of agents and observers. Different agents just know and describe this pre-existing structure from their own perspective. However, according to participatory realism, the relations are created through [the interactions between systems](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/9609002). There are no pre-existing relations or structures out there. In this picture, there is only a world in the making and we as agents in different ways participate in making reality. This is a “participatory universe,” as the American physicist John Wheeler has it.

**“The universe as a whole” is an incoherent notion**

From the perspective of participatory or relational realism, every physical description is necessarily perspectival: it is a description of one physical system relative to another. Consequently, even in cosmology, what we call a description of “the universe” is in fact a description constructed by an epistemic agent who is physically situated within the world. The most comprehensive model that such an agent can construct is therefore not a view of the universe from nowhere, but a representation of the relation between herself and the rest of the world. Crucially, the agent cannot simultaneously occupy the standpoint from which the model is constructed and [also appear within that very model](https://iai.tv/articles/the-universe-is-unknowable-from-within-it-auid-3057) as one of its represented objects. Any attempt to model oneself already presupposes a further standpoint from which that modeling activity is carried out, leading to an unavoidable regress. In this sense, there is no coherent notion of “[the universe as a whole](live/iai-live-july-catching-sight-of-the-universe/catching-sight-of-the-universe)” conceived independently of every possible perspective.

This point also has important implications for cosmological interpretations of quantum mechanics. Consider the universal wavefunction invoked by the Many-Worlds Interpretation. If the universal wavefunction is understood as a complete description of physical reality, then it appears to require a perspective external to the universe itself—a “view from nowhere” or, metaphorically, the standpoint of an omniscient observer. However, relational realism denies that any physically situated agent can occupy such a standpoint. Since every observer is part of the universe whose state is supposedly being described, no observer can have epistemic access to the universal wavefunction as a whole. Thus, from the standpoint of relational realism, the notion of the wavefunction of the universe is not simply inaccessible in practice but conceptually problematic, because it presupposes precisely the perspective-independent description that relational realism rejects.

Thus, the wavefunction represents the complete information from the perspective of an agent about the consequences of her interactions with the world. The quantum mechanical formalism helps agents make better decisions concerning their dealings with the world. From this viewpoint, QM is an empirically-discovered theory of inference. In modeling the world according to QM, agents are using its rules to make correct inferences concerning their consequent interventions in the world. Moreover, this way QM tells the agents why and when they should expect what they are supposed to expect. This amounts to providing explanations for phenomena and thus going beyond mere prediction of the results of measurements.

As the main features of the epistemic interpretations of QM can be traced back to Niels Bohr, it’s best to conclude their lesson with his saying that, “we ourselves are both actors and spectators in the drama of existence.”
