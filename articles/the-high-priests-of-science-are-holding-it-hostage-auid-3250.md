# The high priests of science are holding it hostage

*The myth of truth is not helping*

**By Jamie Shaw** | *8th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,970 words*

![The high priests of science are holding it hostage](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-high-priests-of-science-are-holding-it-hostage.webp)

---

*Science is ruled by a small club of insiders who decide what kinds of research are getting us closer to the truth—and they’re getting it wrong. Brilliant ideas are shut out, innovation is stalled, and billions are wasted chasing safe bets. If we want real progress, from cancer research to cosmology, we should move to a radically egalitarian method of funding scientists, argues philosopher of science Jamie Shaw. We need to break the monopoly, spread the funding more widely, and start trusting scientists to be as wild, diverse and unpredictable as the world they seek to explain.*

**Science funding is broken**

In 2022, the US, China, the EU, Japan, and Russia together spent around two trillion dollars on science. While this may make one think that scientists are spoiled with riches, this is far from enough to go around. Scientists often clamor for funds, with one estimate suggesting that they [spend upwards of 60% of their time just applying for funding](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29707193/), and it is rare that more than [20% of submitted applications receive funding](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/3/5/e002800). This forces tough decisions about where funds should be prioritized within science.

\_\_\_

While a lot of innovation happens in science, most of it gets blocked by peer review.

\_\_\_

Almost universally, these decisions are made or informed by consulting scientists who act as “peer reviewers” to tell funding institutions what is worth their investments. The idea behind it is simple: scientists know the science best, so they have the authority to decide what sorts of science are getting us closer to the truth. This justifies giving them power to make funding decisions. On top of this, scientists are assumed to know where the most *important* truths lie. Asking scientists to review the project proposals of other scientists, therefore, can seem to be the best way to get the most bang for our buck. Governments and funding agency spokespeople routinely use peer review to promise publics that their tax dollars are going to the most important scientific projects that are out there.

But there are several very serious problems with peer review. One is how expensive it is. One study found that [over 100 million working hours went into peer reviewing journal articles in 2020](https://researchintegrityjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s41073-021-00118-2)—and this is an underestimate and the numbers are growing. Reviewing grant proposals is similarly time consuming. And this is just the reviewing side of things. Writing grant proposals takes much more work. That’s a lot of time that could have been spent on other things, like doing the actual science.

Another worry is that peer review tends to be biased against scientists from specific social groups. If you’re a woman, person of color, person with disabilities, non-native speaker, or a person from a “less prestigious” institution, [odds are you will have a tougher time getting funding](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/190/16/e489), regardless of how capable you are. Many scientists do not advance in their career because peer review blocks them from getting a chance. This gets called the “Matthew Effect”: to the rich go the spoils! If you don’t start off “rich” (e.g., at a prestigious university), you have worse odds of proving your capabilities. While, in theory, focusing on the science should make peer review more “objective,” peer reviewers are humans and, like all humans, have biases that they do not fully recognize.

Maybe this would be tolerable if peer review made sure that huge government expenditures on science went to the most deserving places. Research piling up since the 1970s, however, suggests that [peer review often does not do this particularly well](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030631277900900203). Scientists tend to be pretty bad at predicting which projects will succeed or fail and they disagree, more often than not, about which projects are the best or the worst. This poses a serious challenge to their claim of authority. Not only this, but peer reviewers often decide that ground-breaking, innovative research is not worth funding. The most famous recent example of this is Drew Weismann and Katalin Karikóa’s research on mRNA vaccines, the basis of the COVID vaccines that eventually won a Nobel prize, which [failed to get funding over ten times](https://ideas.repec.org/h/nbr/nberch/14573.html). Similarly, Ed Boydon’s work on expansion microscopy, which eventually won the 2015 Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences, [failed to get funding nine times](https://nintil.com/discoveries-ignored/). Brian Kobilka’s work on G-Protein Coupled Receptors, essential for understanding a diverse array of drugs and physiological processes, which won a Nobel prize in 2012, took over 15 years of persistent effort to get noteworthy funding. While a lot of innovation happens in science, most of it gets blocked by peer review. There are pockets of times when peer review works as we would hope, but it is increasingly looking like this is the exception rather than the rule.

\_\_\_

Scientists not only have no entitlement to speak with authority on where science should go next, but the scientists who do end up with such authority have extreme amounts of unjustified power.

\_\_\_

These problems are [becoming better recognized](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2643622/), and many who work at science funding institutions are trying to fix them. But in most cases, they merely tinker with peer review by changing some parts of it, in the hope of retaining the original aspirations of an objective guardian of the responsible use of public monies in science. They remain convinced that peer reviewers deserve the power often granted to them. This might include making reviewers take “implicit bias” tests, giving them slightly different instructions, or having them mark somewhat different things. These small adjustments are not unimportant. But people have underestimated how deep the problems with peer review go. Because of this, they miss out on just how radically we need to transform science-funding policy.

**The battle over power, authority and truth**

One philosopher who recognized the root problems was Paul Feyerabend. He argued that scientists do [not follow a fool-proof “method” that leads them to truth](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Against_Method.html?id=8y-FVtrKeSYC&redir_esc=y). Because of this, they have no authority to speak about what is true or which pursuits are worthwhile. Moreover, scientific progress does not involve [a gradual accumulation of more and more truths about the world](http://iai.tv/video/science-trust-and-truth). Using landmark examples from the history of science, he shows that science advances in a more unpredictable way. Science is constantly changing, and so are its methods. This means that there are no solid benchmarks in science that we can use to evaluate what research we think is going to go somewhere important or reach a dead end. Moreover, when we do use benchmarks based on what we *think* we know, we artificially limit scientific creativity. The strongest resistance Galileo faced when he tried to show that the earth rotated on its axis was from those who wished to enforce the most popular physics of the time—that of Aristotle. If we constantly imposed currently entrenched standards, we would not make progress.

What becomes upsetting is that scientists not only have no entitlement to speak with authority on where science should go next, but the scientists who do end up with such authority have extreme amounts of unjustified power. Science, says Feyerabend and many others since him, is a diverse practice with many conflicts of opinion and interests. Giving a small handful of peer reviewers power over others is not only unwarranted but actively harmful. Diversity becomes silenced because of power politics, not because some scientists are especially in touch with “the truth.”

This point is even salient for urgent areas of research, like medicine. Trillions of dollars have been spent on research on cancer but progress has been painfully slow. This is partly because so many of the resources get funnelled into the same couple of research programs (mostly chemotherapy and radiation therapy). As we now know, [alternatives like immunotherapies also show promise](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24116914/), but because cancer research has been so bottlenecked, we haven’t made as much progress as we probably could have.

**Solutions: redistributing power and redistributing funds**

So how can we reform science funding in ways that allow science to flourish to the best of its potential? There are at least two ideas that show promise. But I want to be perfectly clear: these ideas are new in practice. Ultimately, we have to experiment with different funding strategies to figure out what to do. But these ideas are definitely worth a try.

First: give all eligible scientists money! This usually means scientists with a position at a university, but sometimes may include scientists in private industry. I’ve been calling this a “universal research fund” (like a “universal basic income” for scientists to spend on research). The motivation behind this is to diversify the number of scientists who get funds and give them more freedom to research what they want, without having to please peer reviewers. Power gets redistributed throughout the community, rather than being limited to the few selected to be peer reviewers. Spreading money more evenly allows us to make sure that it isn’t always the same few scientists getting funds, mitigating the “Matthew effect,” and provides a better sense of who is capable and who is not.

Some might worry that this puts too much money in undeserving hands. But, as we have already seen, peer review isn’t doing a great job at this anyway. In many countries, competition for getting the jobs that would make scientists eligible for funding is fierce and usually involves lots of brilliant candidates. This makes the average scientist pretty talented, so we do not need to worry about systematically funding crackpots. Indeed, the line between crackpots and scientists with unorthodox ideas can be blurry and this is a part of what makes peer review intellectually conservative. Trusting scientists, at least on average, is entirely rational.

\_\_\_

A more radically egalitarian method of funding science will reward better and more diverse projects than peer review manages.

\_\_\_

Others might worry that a universal research fund would leave “big science” (science that requires extremely large amounts of money to succeed) underfunded. But scientists can share their funds and join forces. This gets called “crowdfunding” and there are reasons to think that this approach lowers the odds that we unjustly put all our eggs in one basket. In short, crowdfunding exploits what social psychologists call the “wisdom of crowds,” [where groups make better decisions than individuals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691619850561). This prevents concentration of power and authority with a small number of peer reviewers (usually between two and six). Because of this, we might think that a more radically egalitarian method of funding science will reward better and more diverse projects than peer review manages.

The second idea involves [introducing *lotteries* or elements of random chance into funding decisions](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/peer-review-innovation-and-predicting-the-future-of-science-the-scope-of-lotteries-in-science-funding-policy/780541A102BC4DF6FA2F87BDF0FAC256). Lotteries have been practiced in places like the UK, Canada, New Zealand, Switzerland, Germany, and Austria. They are pretty new so we are still figuring out how they work. The first use of lotteries in science funding was done by the Health Research Council of New Zealand in 2013 for “ideas considered to be transformative, innovative, exploratory or unconventional, and have potential for major impact.” The justification of lotteries is to effectively neutralize the bad parts of peer review listed earlier by giving peer reviewers less control over the fate of science. While some worry that this randomized approach makes a mockery of the idea of scientific merit, we have seen that peer review doesn’t promote merit either.

Now is a time of major upheaval in science-funding policy. This means that the most fundamental questions about peer review are getting asked again but with much more data and scrutiny than ever before. Science, right now, is hierarchical in terms of its power structure and this strongly influences what gets done. We should take a more egalitarian approach to science funding, one that recognizes the wide diversity of authorities in science and redistributes power more broadly. While there is a great deal of uncertainty about how to move forward, I think that we can and should experiment with significant changes that radically rethink the nature of science funding and provide new opportunities for the growth of science.
