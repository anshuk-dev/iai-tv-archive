# Bias in science can and must be exposed

*Are some truths really timeless?*

**By Jacob Stegenga** | *29th November 2024* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,662 words*

![Bias in science can and must be exposed](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/bias-vs-truth-part2.webp)

---

*When science tries to free its methods from the influence of political and ethical values, it pursues a dangerous fantasy. Or so claimed Kevin C. Elliott in yesterday’s IAI article. Today, Jacob Stegenga argues that, on the contrary, scientists should always strive to keep their research free of all values. While many areas of science, from medical research to cosmology, are full of uncertainty and controversy, scientists can use the scientific method to gradually strip away their prejudices, and thereby uncover the best models and interpretations of evidence. Far from actively deploying their values in their research, as Elliott advocates, scientists should do all they can to keep their politics and ethics out of their research.*

*This is part 2 of a 2-part series. Read part 1 [here](https://iai.tv/articles/the-dangerous-myth-of-value-free-science-auid-3011).*

**The value-free ideal for science**

A traditional view about science is that ethical, social, or political values should not influence scientific reasoning. This is the *value-free ideal* for science. What makes science objective and hence trustworthy, according to this intuitive idea, is that the results of science are based on evidence and logic rather than preferences and desires. We can call defenders of this view the Traditionalists. In the last 25 years or so philosophers have raised sophisticated arguments challenging the value-free ideal. We can call this group of philosophers the Critics. Professor Kevin Elliott, the author of the companion essay, is an esteemed Critic. I am a Traditionalist.

The debate between the Traditionalists and the Critics is not merely academic. The stakes are high. In the 19th century, opposition to historical sciences such as geology and evolution was based on religious values. In the 20th century the Russian biologist Lysenko promoted his idiosyncratic brand of genetics because it seemed to align with Communist political values, and his agricultural policies killed millions of people as a result. Now, in the 21st century, pharmaceutical companies often skew their research due to their financial values. These are infamous examples, and what went wrong in each of them, argues the Traditionalist, is that they violated the value-free ideal.

Traditionalists and Critics agree that values influence the research questions that scientists ask. They agree that values influence how scientists carry out their research. They agree that values influence what technologies are developed from scientific research. And they agree that the influence of values on science must be managed, for example by limiting the impact of conflicts of interest or by adopting standardised methods. So there appears to be much agreement in the background of this debate. The debate is about whether values must or should influence the results of scientific reasoning itself. Traditionalists hold up the value-free ideal as an aim that science should strive for, and they hold that science at its best attains this aim, while Critics claim that “value-free science is a fantasy,” or a “myth,” as [a previous contributor to IAI wrote](http://iai.tv/articles/the-myth-of-value-free-science-melanie-challenger-auid-2483). Who is right?

**Science’s “timeless truths”**

Some Traditionalists have, unfortunately, neglected the compelling arguments of the Critics. Any responsible view of science must contend with the important points raised in Prof. Elliott’s companion essay: in many contexts scientific methods are malleable, allowing a wide range of choices which influence results; scientists must decide how much confidence to convey in their assertions; errors can have practical consequences and scientists have a responsibility to consider those consequences. These are among the common considerations that convince the Critics.

These timeless truths suffice to show that the value-free ideal is not an illusory dream, but rather is achievable by science at its best.

\_\_\_

Yet the conclusion of the Critics is often exaggerated. They claim that the value-free ideal is an “illusory dream” or an “impossible ideal.” When I hear young scholars in conferences preface a remark by saying that everyone knows that the value-free ideal is dead, I scratch my head. All Critics must agree that diamonds are composed of carbon, that the planets orbit the sun, that dinosaurs existed, that atoms are composed of a dense nuclear core, and, crucially, they must agree that none of these scientific conclusions are influenced by values in any controversial sense. Diamonds are composed of carbon, and nobody’s love of diamonds can change that. In a book that will be published next year, I call these “timeless truths.”

These timeless truths suffice to show that the value-free ideal is not an illusory dream, but rather is achievable by science at its best. When we ask why we should trust science, it is helpful to remember what science at its best can achieve, and what features of science allow for such achievements. The great physicist Niels Bohr claimed that science involves “the gradual removal of prejudices,” which strikes me as an apt description of the value-free ideal. The value-free ideal is possible to achieve, and when it is achieved the results are worthy of our trust. No serious person today doubts that dinosaurs existed.

**How science can gradually remove prejudices**

The arguments of the Critics get most traction in policy-relevant science which is characterised by deep uncertainty, such as the compelling example discussed by Prof. Elliott about whether mobile phones cause cancer. In such contexts scientists are not trading in timeless truths—they must make many decisions about their methods, their analyses, and their communication to the public and policy-makers.  And they are aware of the practical consequences of being wrong: if they conclude that mobile phones are harmful when in fact they are safe then they will contribute to needless regulation and constrain consumer access to valuable products, but if they conclude that mobile phones are safe when they are in fact harmful then they will contribute to exposing people to those harms. Evaluation of those downstream consequences should influence the upstream decisions about their methods and analyses and communication, claims the Critic. Hence, scientific reasoning in these limited domains is (must be? should be?) value laden.

The Traditionalist should agree with everything in that prior paragraph, up until the last couple of sentences at least. The practical crux of the debate is about what scientists should do when they are called on to advise policy and their available evidence is highly uncertain. This is where Traditionalists and Critics part ways.

Prof. Elliott describes the sorts of tactics that Critics promote: scientists should be transparent about their values, or scientists should follow standardised rules to constrain the influence of idiosyncratic values, or scientists should rely on the values of the people who will be impacted by their work. That is all fine. But being transparent about the thousand choices a scientist must make might be impossible; standardised rules might encode pernicious values or introduce their own biases; many different kinds of people can be impacted by science and they can hold opposing values. Most importantly, this approach gives up on one of the central features of science that makes it worthy of our trust, namely, its striving toward the value-free ideal.

\_\_\_

Value-laden choices – far from being swept under the rug – must be exposed

\_\_\_

A better strategy is to follow Bohr’s understanding of science and aim for the value-free ideal. Prof. Elliott claims that this strategy “shoves all these value-laden choices under the rug.” That is not so. The Traditionalist maintains that scientists should do all they can to eliminate values from scientific reasoning, and to do that, value-laden choices – far from being swept under the rug – must be exposed. To be fair, such exposure can involve some tactics recommended by the Critics, such as diversifying the range of viewpoints in science and promoting critical scrutiny. Other tactics can include routine scientific practices such as increasing the quality and quantity of evidence. An important tactic that Traditionalists recommend is that scientists should typically be upfront about their uncertainty and the limitations on their findings and should hedge their public-facing communication accordingly. If scientists improve their quality and quantity of evidence and sufficiently hedge their conclusions, those conclusions are less likely to be influenced by values, and more likely to be worthy of our trust.

The scientists in Prof. Elliott’s example of mobile phone use did exactly this, when, for example, they mentioned the “low certainty evidence” that mobile phone radiation “may not increase” the risk of cancer—so these scientists conveyed their uncertainty and hedged their conclusions. It was journalists who sensationalised the science, using terms in their headlines like ‘truth’ and ‘definitive call.’ Of course, sometimes scientists sensationalise their own work. An egregious example of this occurred when scientists involved with the huge Bangladesh Mask Study wrote an editorial in a major newspaper with the headline “We Did the Research: Masks Work,” which was a hyperbolic exaggeration of what their study had found (among the 350,000 people in that study, the group of people who wore masks had a mere twenty fewer cases of Covid compared with the control group).

A Critic might respond by claiming that my examples of timeless truths—about diamonds, dinosaurs, and atoms—are not characterised by the same degree of complexity or uncertainty as is much policy-relevant science today. Of course, learning about the nucleus of atoms or what life on Earth was like 100 million years ago was not easy. Trustworthy science is rarely easy. Marie Curie—the scientist who made some of the most important discoveries about radiation—wrote that progress in science is “neither swift nor easy.” Yet policy-relevant science sometimes must be swift. Policymakers sometimes need input from scientists and in some contexts they cannot wait for the scientists to improve the quality and quantity of their evidence, and if scientists hedge too much in such contexts then they may fail to elicit appropriate policy. So, the Critic might say that the strategies recommended by the Traditionalist to pursue the value-free ideal cannot always succeed. But that is okay. Not all climbers who attempt to summit Mount Everest succeed. Like world peace, the value-free ideal is an *ideal*. But unlike world peace (sadly), the value-free ideal is attainable. Diamonds really are composed of carbon.
