# If we lose meritocracy, we risk liberal democracy

*Why young generations may no longer believe in meritocracy*

**By Gaetano Inglese** | *8th January 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,192 words*

![If we lose meritocracy, we risk liberal democracy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/meritocracy-and-the-plight-of-graduates2.webp)

---

*The belief in meritocracy – that quality, hard work pays off with a commensurate reward – is central to Western liberal democracy; without it, people do not trust free markets, and desire a more socialist government to step in. And now, because a highly educated young population has been met with lower than expected economic rewards, belief in meritocracy is indeed in decline. Researcher at the LSE Gaetano Inglese argues this decline could mean the end of Western societies as we know them.*

**Introduction**

Meritocracy is the widely shared belief that economic fortunes and life outcomes are determined by hard work and effort put in developing skills, competencies and knowledge. This principle has served as a major cornerstone of the reigning liberal individualist ideology of contemporary capitalist societies, promoting the belief that opportunities are equally accessible, and rewards are distributed based on individual merit.

\_\_\_

In recent years, an opposing, anti-meritocratic trend is emerging as the grim reality of economic and occupational outcomes increasingly fails to align with the expectations generated by the liberal meritocratic credo.

\_\_\_

Over the post-war decades especially, the belief in meritocracy has profoundly shaped the aspirations of younger generations across socio-economic classes in the West, who have enjoyed unprecedented access to higher education and, consequently, to bright opportunities for upward social mobility. Thus, while meritocracy has worked as the pragmatic myth of liberal individualism in capitalist societies, it has also boosted the rational expectations of many people, with many individuals and their families choosing to invest in education to achieve higher social status and pursue the noble goal of upgrading their socioeconomic conditions.

However, in recent years, an opposing, *anti*-meritocratic trend is emerging as the grim reality of economic and occupational outcomes increasingly fails to align with the expectations generated by the liberal meritocratic credo. [As recent articles have claimed, many young graduates are finding themselves in jobs that do not match their qualifications](https://www.economist.com/international/2024/11/18/is-your-masters-degree-useless), with their income levels often falling below what they anticipated when making their education choices. [This mismatch has raised questions around meritocratic beliefs and the fairness of economic outcomes more generally](https://www.theguardian.com/higher-education-network/2018/jan/25/too-many-graduates-are-mismatched-to-their-jobs-whats-going-wrong), particularly during times of economic uncertainty and political turbulence.

\_\_\_

Disappointed early-life expectations may weaken the belief in the fairness of the economic system and foster socialist attitudes in support of greater government intervention in the economy.

\_\_\_

More importantly, when such a felt gap between expected and real economic outcomes occurs at a young, formative age, it may have a lasting impact on one’s behaviour, possibly leading to a *generational shift* in meritocratic beliefs. This is the focus of my article: the lasting impact of unmet income and occupational expectations on belief in meritocracy. The hypothesis flowing from the observation of current patterns in declining social mobility is that disappointed early-life expectations may weaken the belief in the fairness of the economic system and foster socialist attitudes in support of greater government intervention in the economy. Most interestingly, one could argue that these viewpoints persist throughout one’s life, influencing how individuals perceive economic security, free markets, and state regulation. The important consequences of such a line of thinking are that expectations not only shape individual beliefs but may also play a broader role in reshaping public attitudes of younger generations toward economic policy. A conjecture whose answer lies in the future.

**Meritocracy and its Discontents**

As philosopher Micheal Sandel has masterfully explained, the concept of meritocracy underpins the belief that individual effort determines economic outcomes. Education is seen as its central mechanism, enabling upward occupational mobility and higher income levels. This belief has long fuelled the aspirations of young people, who view educational attainment as the gateway to economic success. For many, the implicit promise of meritocracy is that hard work, coupled with skill and knowledge acquisition, will translate into economic reward and meaningful occupational opportunities. And that this process is intrinsically *fair*.

In recent decades, however, cracks have emerged in this narrative. Across advanced democracies, a growing number of young people with higher education qualifications find themselves in jobs that do not require their level of education. This phenomenon, already widely documented, is called “skills mismatch”, and characterizes market economies where education is relatively universally accessible, but where labour markets are still underdeveloped and/or unequipped to absorb increasing numbers of educated individuals. Relatedly, income levels often fall short of expectations formed during years of study.

Such expectations’ gaps are particularly pronounced among those who invested heavily in education. The failure to meet these expectations challenges the core tenets of meritocracy, leading many young people to question whether effort and ability truly determine success in the market economy, and most importantly, to question the core meritocracy promise, that economic and life outcomes are in the hands of individuals. The decline in opportunities for upward occupational mobility has coincided with broader economic and political challenges. Wage stagnation, rising inequality, and periods of economic downturn have compounded the sense of insecurity felt by younger generations. These experiences have created a disconnect between the ideals of meritocracy and the lived reality of many young people. As a result, the belief that individuals control their economic destiny is being undermined. More simply put, while meritocracy fosters economic optimism, the *actual* lack thereof generates economic pessimism in the system.

**Support for Market Regulation and Government Intervention**

Traditionally, capitalist economic theories have conceived the individual as self-interested, deducing that attitudes towards the state– such as taxes, regulation of labour relations, redistribution etc… - are primarily shaped by an individual’s objective socioeconomic position. As it is common wisdom since the so-called median voter theorem by economists Meltzer and Richard, individuals with higher incomes are generally unsupportive of redistribution, while those at the bottom of the income distribution pyramid are more likely to favour taxes on higher incomes.

However, subjective experiences and cognitive biases critically shape individuals’ economic attitudes. As stated above, failing to meet expectations embedded in education choices may entail significant implications for individual ideological leanings, potentially disrupting the very ideological foundations of liberal individualism. Those who share the perception that the market economy is failing to deliver on its promises to them are more likely to support government intervention, regardless of their income level or occupational status.

The role of subjectivity here is essential: we often justify ex post our current socioeconomic conditions based on whether we believe our situation is fair or unfair (deserved or undeserved). For instance, an individual who lives in relatively insecure economic conditions may not turn to socialist policies if he or she believes to *deserve* such conditions. Contrarily, an individual who enjoys an objectively secure situation may become socialist if he or she believes that effort ultimately does not determine outcomes and that economic outcomes are more due to luck than merit.

\_\_\_

This dynamic helps explain why younger generations, despite enjoying higher levels of education, and often better objective living conditions, often express pessimistic accounts of the future and less trust in the promise of free markets.

\_\_\_

For many young people today, unmet expectations may serve as *the* formative experience that ingrains the perception that market outcomes are unfair because unrelated to individual merit. Periods of economic downturn magnify the effects of unmet expectations. When economic opportunities shrink, individuals who already feel disillusioned with the market system may unsurprisingly become even more supportive of government regulation. This dynamic helps explain why younger generations, despite enjoying higher levels of education, and often better *objective* living conditions, often express pessimistic accounts of the future and less trust in the promise of free markets.

**The Role of Fairness Beliefs**

To dig more into this anti-meritocracy mechanism, it is important to shed light on the relationship between *expectation shocks* and *fairness beliefs*. When individuals underperform relative to their expectations, they are likely to subsequently perceive the entire system as unfair and not meritocratic, as well as beyond their control. These experiences can thus erode the self-perception of being able to shape one’s own outcomes, which translates into a scepticism towards market-driven approaches.

Fairness beliefs are shaped by both individual experiences and broader social norms. For example, individuals raised in environments that emphasize the meritocratic ideal may feel particularly disillusioned when their personal outcomes fail to align with these norms. Or, similarly, individuals whose outcomes contrast sharply with those of peers with similar educational backgrounds may view their experiences as evidence of systemic unfairness. Such a comparative framework often reinforces the perception that effort and ability are insufficient to guarantee success in the current economic climate.

The erosion of fairness beliefs has important implications for attitudes toward market regulation. Individuals who no longer believe in the fairness of market outcomes are more likely to support government intervention to address perceived insecurity. This shift reflects a broader reorientation of attitudes in this generation, as individuals choose to prioritize security and stability over the ideals of free-market and competition.

There is also some interesting variation in the reaction of individuals to expectations shocks depending on individuals’ social class of origin. For individuals from higher socioeconomic backgrounds, the belief in meritocracy is often deeply ingrained, as they grow up in environments where educational attainment and economic success are closely linked. When *these* individuals fail to achieve their expected outcomes, the experience is likely to be particularly jarring, leading to a pronounced sense of disillusionment and a stronger shift in attitudes toward government intervention.

By contrast, individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may have more modest expectations of upward mobility, even when they attain higher levels of education. For these individuals, unmet expectations may not have the same destabilizing effect on their fairness beliefs, as they are more likely to view their outcomes as consistent with systemic inequities rather than personal failure.

**The Lasting Impact of Unmet Expectations**

The mismatch between expectations and outcomes does not merely shape individuals’ experiences on the short term. Significantly, this pattern affects mostly young people. Early-life experiences and early life stages, particularly those occurring during the so-called "impressionable years" (ages 16–25), play a critical role in shaping long-term attitudes. Indeed, they are called formative experiences. During such an eventful life period, young people experience key life events such as entering the labour market, earning their first income, and paying taxes for the first time. These experiences are particularly influential because they occur at a time when individuals are still developing their perceptions of economic systems and social fairness, and their behaviour is particularly malleable.

When young people enter the labour market and experience a so-called skills mismatch, this also has lasting implications for their beliefs across their life course. Evidently, the gap between expectations and reality can provoke shock and deep disillusionment with the meritocratic process. In the long run, the legacy of these formative experiences may weaken individuals’ faith in the fairness of the economic system and shape attitudes in support of a greater role of government in the economy to ensure protection and security. Even if income or job status changes, this perceived subjective insecurity may remain. Individuals with unmet expectations are more likely to perceive the economy as unpredictable and beyond their control, and thus to be supportive of state economic intervention to mitigate market risks.

**Conclusion**

Meritocracy has long served as a guiding principle for our liberal capitalist societies, instilling the belief that individual effort determines economic outcomes. However, for many young, educated individuals, the reality of their economic and occupational outcomes increasingly fails to meet this ideal. The experience of unmet expectations—whether in terms of income, occupation, or social mobility—has significant and lasting implications for young peoples’ attitudes toward the roles markets and governments should play within society. At a formative age, unmet expectations challenge beliefs in the fairness and predictability of market-driven outcomes, with lasting effects. Such lack of trust in meritocratic outcomes fosters a sense of economic insecurity which persists throughout life and possibly clashes with objective socioeconomic conditions. The implications of this process are consequential, widening the gap between subjective and objective evaluations of economic security for individuals, and therefore, impacting their political demands. Individuals are not rational agents, as neoclassical economics parsimoniously assumes, but rather behave in an economically rational way only when their interest is supported by the corresponding belief in meritocracy. The poor may not support redistribution if they believe to deserve their insecurity, and the rich may support redistribution if they think not to deserve what they have.

\_\_\_

We may have reached a point in our societies where meritocracy should be re-considered, where we no longer give the blame of unsuccess to single individuals, but instead recognise collective failures to deliver on expectations.

\_\_\_

Our Western liberal democratic system has long (nominally at least) supported the meritocratic idea that success is achieved through hard work. This article suggests that this is not an iron law, and rather that meeting or not meeting meritocratic expectations may have profound consequences for individual behaviour and political demands. We may have reached a point in our societies where meritocracy should be re-considered, where we no longer give the blame of unsuccess to single individuals, but instead recognise collective failures to deliver on expectations. Another, related question is whether we will witness a generational shift in beliefs in the liberal principle of “merit leads to rewards”, given the formative experiences of unmet expectations, and whether this will boost more widespread demands for the regulation of market economies.
