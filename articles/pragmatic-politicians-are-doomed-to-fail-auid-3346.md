# Pragmatic politicians are doomed to fail

*Max Weber's warning to Carney, Starmer, and other pragmatic politicians*

**By Steven Klein** | *22nd August 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,514 words*

![Pragmatic politicians are doomed to fail](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/max-weber-our-politicians-need-to-have-visions.webp)

---

*Political leaders seem to come in two flavors. On the one hand, you have ideological figures concerned with grand narratives and broad issues. And on the other you have politicians who avoid ideology for pragmatism, analysis, and bureaucracy. KCL political theorist Steven Klein argues that to understand the second politician, we must look to Max Weber. From Starmer to Carney, competent politics may seem preferable to blind ideology, but these politicians betray a fatal flaw by abandoning conviction and relying on rules and bureaucracy. For these politicians to be successful, they need to learn from Weber that politics is as much about holding convictions as it is about accepting unintended consequences.*

It can be hard to figure out exactly what Keir Starmer stands for, but if there is anything, it’s this: sober, realistic, and responsible political leadership. In the history of political ideas, the most famous defender of sober-minded political leadership is the German sociologist Max Weber. Speaking to students at the University of Munich in the wake of Germany’s defeat in World War I, Weber’s “[Politics as a Vocation](https://archive.org/details/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation)” provided a penetrating analysis of the dilemmas of political action in modern societies, introducing the apparently opposing concepts of the “ethics of conviction” and “ethics of responsibility.” Yet Weber also shows the dangers of the bureaucratic drift that stands behind Starmer’s supposed realism. For [Weber](http://iai.tv/articles/what-they-wont-teach-you-at-university-auid-1765), realist political leadership means harnessing enthusiasm, passion, and sweeping moral visions of the sort Starmer disdains—specifically because the realist recognizes the dangers of mindless bureaucratic government.

\_\_\_

Starmer positions this ruthless pragmatism in contrast to both the ideological language of his predecessor, [Jeremy Corbyn,](http://iai.tv/articles/new-political-parties-wont-save-democracy-auid-3339) and the flimsy [populism](http://iai.tv/articles/a-new-vision-for-the-centre-left-auid-2887) of his now chief rival, Reform leader Nigel Farage. This means giving the public hard truths rather than grand fantasies. Defending the (now, of course, reversed—sometimes politics is trickier than it seems) decision to cut winter fuel allowances, Starmer said, [“I’m absolutely convinced that we will only deliver that change—I’m absolutely determined we will—if we do the difficult things now.”](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cgm7rnlz81do) As Carys Roberts of the Institute for Public Policy Research put it, [“He almost has an allergy to ideology, he wants to be the prime minister who rolls his sleeves up and gets stuff done.”](https://www.newstatesman.com/cover-story/2024/05/what-is-starmerism)

Starmer is indicative of a broader trend of [bureaucrat-politicians who prize competence](http://iai.tv/articles/obamas-chief-economist-neoliberalism-is-the-only-game-in-town-auid-3262). These are often individuals who rose up the ranks of the state-aligned professions before entering public life. Mark Carney, the new Prime Minister of Canada, was a central banker before his surprise entry into the Liberal Party leadership race. Within the European Union, men in navy blue suits, adept at navigating the intrigues of the Commission, wield massive power.

\_\_\_

Politics is about commanding, creating order, and using the appropriate means to realise your ends.

\_\_\_

Weber’s lecture, on first read, seems to be calling for exactly this ethos. In the lecture, he seeks to disabuse his audience of naïve, [romanticized views of politics](http://iai.tv/articles/we-need-a-new-political-theology-auid-3145). Weber’s goal is to impress exactly what entering political life as a career means. But his view of politics is decidedly unromantic. Politics is about commanding, creating order, and using the appropriate means to realize your ends. Indeed, it is about [violence](http://iai.tv/video/violence-vengeance-and-virtue), as in modern societies only the state, Weber argues, is authorized to deploy violence to realize its ends. The other basic fact of modern political life, for Weber, is the division between rulers and followers. He is brutally realistic about the fact that most individuals only take a passing interest in politics. This means that, for Weber, the fate of democratic life depends on the quality of leadership.

All this leads to Weber’s central distinction between an ethics of conviction and an ethics of responsibility. The ethics of [conviction](http://iai.tv/articles/why-we-live-in-alternate-political-realities-auid-3239) starts with the inherent moral correctness of certain actions. It sees the world as deeply corrupt and so thinks that if bad consequences follow from the action, “the world, not the doer,” is responsible. The focus is more on expressing deeply felt beliefs than realising outcomes. “The person who subscribes to the ethics of conviction,” Weber says, “feels ‘responsible’ only for ensuring that the flame of pure conviction (for example, the flame of protest against the injustice of the social order) is never extinguished.”

\_\_\_

Despite our best intentions, our actions will wreak havoc

\_\_\_

Against this, Max Weber defends the ethos of responsibility. This stance accepts that politics requires using “morally suspect or at least morally dangerous means” that can create unexpected [evil side-effects](http://iai.tv/video/machiavellis-rules). For Weber, political leadership means not shying away from these unintended consequences and taking seriously the ethical irrationality of the world. Despite our best intentions, our actions will wreak havoc. A responsible political leader takes responsibility for those unintended consequences and responds to them.

So far, so good for Starmer. He seems like a model Weberian politician. Indeed, Weber draws a sharp contrast between his responsible politician and the two dangers of modern politics: the mindless, power-worshipping demagogue and the morally sincere but equally disastrous conviction politician. These two extremes find their embodiments in Starmer’s foils—[Farage](http://iai.tv/articles/we-must-retire-the-word-populism-auid-2913), on the one hand, and Corbyn on the other.

\_\_\_

This vain politician revels in easy promises and says whatever it takes to achieve power without really caring about realizing any goals once in power.

\_\_\_

We have already seen Weber’s disdain for the conviction politician who wants to stand above the messy struggle for power. But there’s also a danger in pursuing power *too much*. The pursuit of power is inherent to politics. This means there is an ever-present danger that this pursuit itself “becomes a matter of purely personal self-intoxication.” The bad sort of demagogue is only interested in making an impact with his words, and so becomes a “play-actor…taking the responsibility for his actions too lightly and being concerned only with the ‘impression’ he is making.” This vain politician revels in easy promises and says whatever it takes to achieve power without really caring about realizing any goals once in power. Enter figures like Trump and Farage.

But there is also a third type unsuited to politics but endemic to modern life. That is the official. Like the conviction politician, the bureaucrat essentially lives in a world where they do not have to take personal responsibility for their actions. The goal of an official is to carry out instructions “conscientiously and precisely in the same way as if it corresponded to his own convictions.” Indeed, their job is precisely *not* to take responsibility for the overall direction of politics and the myriad unintended consequences of actions. Rather, it is to comply with the rules and implement the goal of whoever wins power. The official is instinctively averse to the natural domain of genuine political leadership. They must “not do the very thing which politicians, both leaders and their followers, always and necessarily must do, which is to *fight*. Partisanship, fighting, passion…all this is the very element in which the politician, and above all the political *leader*, thrives.”

\_\_\_

The danger of creeping officialdom is that the defence of rules and institutions becomes an end in itself, another way to evade responsibility for hard choices where there are no clear answers.

\_\_\_

The responsible politician also recognizes that winning political power means mobilising their followers and indeed pursuing a cause. They are not blind to ideals and sweeping visions. The danger of creeping officialdom is that the defence of rules and institutions becomes an end in itself, another way to evade responsibility for hard choices where there are no clear answers. Indeed, the paradox of Weberian leadership is precisely that it needs to mobilize conviction for responsibility. It needs to take people’s belief in sweeping ethical and moral visions and harness that, via political warfare, for the responsible achievement of goals.

\_\_\_

Indeed, at the end of his lecture, Weber introduces a twist. The genuine political leader, he says, combines *both* the [ethic of conviction](http://iai.tv/articles/why-we-live-in-alternate-political-realities-auid-3239) and the ethic of responsibility. They find moments when they have to take a stand, regardless of the consequences. But those moments cannot be defined ahead of time. The great temptation in our age is for politicians to try to delegate those moments, as much as possible, to rule-bound, bureaucratic bodies. George Osborne, for example, created the Office of Budget Responsibility to subordinate government decision-making to objective targets and evaluation by independent experts. Far from wresting back a politics of responsibility, the Labour government has let its entire political agenda sink or swim based on OBR forecasting.

It is hard to imagine, from Weber’s perspective, a more misplaced conviction. Starmer may be correct that he is more willing to embrace hard choices than populists like the insurgent British hard right’s Nigel Farage. But Starmer is equally at risk of becoming a bureaucrat shirking his responsibility. Guided by their faith in good governance, the bureaucrat-turned-politician risks floundering in procedural straitjackets of their own making. Genuine responsibility means taking politics for what it is—a messy domain of power, conflict, and combat—and, from that unstable mix, using leadership to create a vision for the future.
