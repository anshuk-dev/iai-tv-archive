# Aristotle, reality TV, and why fiction reveals more than fact

*Fiction uncovers something deeper than truth*

**By Karen Simecek** | *8th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,795 words*

![Aristotle, reality TV, and why fiction reveals more than fact](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Aristotle-reality-TV-and-why-fiction-reveals-mroe-than-fact.webp)

---

*Reality TV - from The Rehearsal to Love Island to The Traitors - blurs the line between fiction and reality. While often seen as lowbrow, our other modes of storytelling that blur this line, namely myth, are considered some of our deepest, most profound stories. Philosopher Karen Simecek argues that when it comes to storytelling truth is far from the most important aspect. Fiction allows us to discover what is meaningful to us, whether that thing is based in reality or not.*

“Popular reality TV show exposed as fake in shocking behind-the-scenes video ... and fans aren't happy.” Headlines like this have become commonplace since the early 2000s, when shows like *Big Brother* helped launch the global reality TV boom. Viewers often express concern over whether what they’re watching is real or fake. But this raises a deeper question: why do we care – and should we? Sometimes, of course, the concern is ethical: viewers are right to be troubled by the mistreatment of contestants. But more often, the outrage stems from feeling deceived and worries that what they thought they learned or felt in response to the show was based on a lie.

Shows like *Love Island*, *The Bachelor*, and *Pop Idol* have all faced scrutiny over their authenticity. The impulse to ‘expose’ reality TV as fake suggests we place high value on the idea of ‘realness’. But what exactly is at stake in whether something is real or fictional? Philosopher Colin Radford (1975) explores this question in his discussion of the paradox of fiction. He asks us to imagine being moved on hearing a tragic and harrowing account, only to be told it’s not true. He writes:

*“If the account had caused you to grieve, you could not continue to grieve. If ... you were told and believed that it was false this would make tears impossible, unless they were tears of rage. If you learned later that the account was false, you would feel that in being moved to tears you had been fooled, duped.”*

And so here’s the paradox: we change our emotional response upon learning that we falsely believed the story to be true but when we knowingly engage with a story we believe to be untrue we allow ourselves to respond freely and deeply to the fiction. This paradox may then be the key to understanding our experiences of reality TV: Why do we feel cheated when we discover something we believed was real is actually scripted – yet willingly engage emotionally with fiction when we know it’s invented?

[Nathan Fielder’s HBO series The Rehearsal](https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://www.hbomax.com/shows/rehearsal/609d8b4c-f0a6-4a5d-b9d3-bb0f2e207efb&ved=2ahUKEwiOqfin78GPAxV0QkEAHfnwESoQFnoECA4QAQ&usg=AOvVaw2BfARtTROIq3-KfVu9HUmL) offers a compelling case study. The show uses actors, elaborate sets, and meticulous detail to help real people prepare for difficult real-life situations. But it also interrogates our obsession with authenticity and the limits of learning from ‘real’ experiences. In the show’s first episode, Fielder helps trivia fan, Kor Skeete, rehearse revealing a long-held secret to a friend and member of his quiz team. Fielder recreates the bar where the conversation will take place and hires an actor to study and mimic the friend’s behaviour. Skeete rehearses the conversation repeatedly, adjusting for different possible outcomes. Eventually, he has the ‘real’ conversation – but even that turns out to be partially staged. The line between rehearsal and reality blurs. In season two, Fielder simulates aviation disasters with real pilots, eventually flying a real plane with real passengers. His goal is to test a theory about co-pilot hesitancy in raising safety concerns with the captain. While this seems like a move toward practical application, the show’s deeper focus remains on authenticity and emotional understanding.

*The Rehearsal* asks whether we can truly learn from observing or mimicking human behaviour. Cameras inevitably distort what’s ‘real,’ yet we continue to look to reality media to teach us about love, grief, and conflict. When that trust is broken, we feel deceived. But perhaps the real value lies not in factual truth, but in emotional resonance. This tension isn’t unique to reality TV. In spoken word poetry, audiences often demand confirmation that a poet’s emotional story is autobiographical – even when the poem’s power lies in its delivery and emotional truth, not its factual accuracy.

[Martha Nussbaum](https://iai.tv/video/a-new-theory-of-ethics-martha-nussbaum) argues that literature helps us develop imagination and emotional insight. She argues that it can cultivate moral sensitivity and empathy. Aristotle, in his *Poetics*, goes further: he claims fiction is more epistemologically valuable than history because it reveals what is possible and necessary – not just what happened. History tells us that one event followed another; fiction shows us why. Understanding *why* is crucial to feeling with and for others. It helps us identify motivations, desires, and needs – and respond emotionally to their fulfilment or frustration. Fiction, then, becomes a tool for moral and emotional education. So, does it matter if a reality show uses actors, scripts, or editing tricks? Do we gain anything by knowing that someone really said *that* to *them*? Or can we learn just as much by reflecting on the general truths that emerge from our reactions?

Believability still matters. Aristotle emphasised that a good plot must be unified and plausible; each event should follow from the last by necessity or probability. Fiction must feel coherent and meaningful to be cognitively valuable. In today’s media landscape, where the line between performance and authenticity is increasingly blurred, *The Rehearsal* invites us to reconsider what we mean by ‘truth.’ How much can we learn by inhabiting roles – or by watching others do so? Does it matter whether those roles are drawn from real life or imagined scenarios?

Returning to Nussbaum, the problem may be that we find it hard to trust our own judgment. But by worrying less about whether something is real or fake, we might open up greater possibilities to develop our own responses. As Radford writes:

*“There is no problem about being moved by historical novels or plays, documentary films, etc. For these works depict and forcibly remind us of the real plight and of the real sufferings of real people, and it is for these persons that we feel.”*

If we connect this with Aristotle’s point, we arrive at a conclusion: we can trust our emotional responses to reality TV shows, provided we recognise that we are responding to the possibility of experience – not necessarily the actual experience of someone. But this will have the upshot that feelings of connection with the particular real life participants on screen is illusory (much like is the case with actors in fictions).

This shift – from seeking factual truth to valuing emotional and philosophical insight – has implications beyond entertainment. It challenges how we think about knowledge itself. If we accept that fiction, or fictionalised reality, can offer meaningful truths, then we must also reconsider the hierarchy that places ‘real’ experiences depicted on screens above imagined ones in terms of epistemic value. This is not to say that all forms of manipulation are ethically neutral. There is a difference between artistic license and deceptive editing designed to mislead or exploit (and I’ll note that many of these ethical concerns are also a feature of our evaluation of fiction). The ethical concerns surrounding reality TV – especially regarding consent, mental health, and exploitation – remain urgent.

The discomfort we feel when reality TV is revealed to be scripted may stem less from the fiction itself and more from the breach of trust. We enter into a tacit agreement with the media we engage with: we will suspend disbelief, but only if we are told when to do so. When that boundary is crossed without warning, it feels like a violation – not because fiction is bad, but because the terms of engagement were unclear. This is where *The Rehearsal* becomes particularly instructive. Fielder’s show doesn’t just blur the line between reality and fiction – it makes that blurring the subject of inquiry. By foregrounding the artificiality of its setups, it invites viewers to reflect on their own expectations and assumptions. It doesn’t pretend to be real (especially as the scenarios he rehearses become more and more ridiculous); instead, it asks what ‘real’ even means in the context of mediated experience. In doing so, it aligns with Nussbaum’s understanding of fiction not as escapism but as a mode of inquiry that helps readers and audiences to appreciate the complexity and sensitivities of moral judgement. Reality TV, at its best, can do something similar. It can offer a window into human behaviour, not by presenting unfiltered truth, but by staging scenarios that reveal patterns, tensions, and possibilities. When we watch someone navigate a romantic dilemma, a competitive challenge, or a moral crisis, we are not just entertained – we are invited to reflect on our own values, choices, and vulnerabilities.

\_\_\_

Radford’s concern that we feel duped when we discover a story is false rests on the assumption that emotional responses require factual grounding. But as Aristotle, Nussbaum, and others have shown, emotions are not just reactions to facts; they are responses to meaning. We cry not because something happened, but because it mattered. In this light, the emotional power of reality TV – even when partially scripted – can be seen as a form of philosophical engagement. It invites us to explore what matters to us, what we fear, what we hope for, and how we relate to others. It becomes a mirror, not of the world as it is, but of the world as we experience it.

In the end, the value of reality TV (and of fiction more broadly) lies not in its relationship to fact, but in its capacity to illuminate the human condition. Instead of seeking secure, factual knowledge, [we might embrace an Aristotelian view](https://iai.tv/articles/chinas-take-on-plato-and-aristotle-shadi-bartsch-auid-2710) – one that values meaningfulness, believability, and emotional insight. In doing so, we open ourselves to deeper understanding: of ourselves, of others, and of the many ways we might relate to one another in a complex world. But this also means that the limitation of our screens is much the same as fiction; we need to be in the world to create real connection with others and understand one another.
