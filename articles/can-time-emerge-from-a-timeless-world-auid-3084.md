# Can time emerge from a timeless world?

*Quantum gravity and the problem of time*

**By Eugene Chua** | *19th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *2,974 words*

![Can time emerge from a timeless world?](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Can-time-emerge-in-a-timeless-world.webp)

---

*The concept of time is indispensable in modern physics, yet in our equations for quantum gravity it is shown to disappear. Some have accepted the fundamental timelessness of nature and others have sought a deeper theory in which time is recoverable, but other physicists and philosophers of physics have attempted a different approach – to show that time emerges from a timeless world. While some have critiqued this notion of emergence on metaphysical grounds, Eugene Chua argues that we should instead critique theories of emergent time on the merits of their physical arguments which, as Chua points out, aren’t as coherent as some of their proponents might think.*

In Jean-Baptiste Racine’s 1669 play *Britannicus*, the character Narcisse famously remarked that “il n’est point de secrets que le temps ne révèle”: there are no secrets that time does not reveal. However, as modern physics probes deeper into the nature of our world, it seems as though time itself may be the greatest secret of all.

In practice, time is utterly familiar - something counted by alarm clocks and heartbeats - while in our most promising theories of quantum gravity, time vanishes, leaving us with a frozen reality. In order to see how time may come undone at the frontiers of physics it is helpful to see how our conception of time has changed throughout history.

**Time from Aristotle to Einstein**

Earlier thinkers tended to assume the existence of a universal time which flows independent of any particular object. Aristotle, in [*the Physics*](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.html), understood time as “equally everywhere and in everything” even though it was dependent on the changes of particular objects. In the Scholium of the [*Principia*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/newton-principia/#DefAbsSpaTimMot), Isaac Newton presented a distinctively mathematical picture of time, though similar in flavor to Aristotle’s universal time: “Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external”.

\_\_\_

We can broadly distinguish approaches where the self is something that emerges out of mental processes, which we might call a “persona”, from approaches where it’s something that underlies and enables mental processes, which we might call a “substrate”.

\_\_\_

This idea, of a unique objective Now that you and I share across arbitrary distances, remained firmly entrenched in the history of thought despite parallel debates about the nature of space — until Einstein’s special theory of relativity, that is. Special relativity implored us to give up the assumption of an [objective notion of simultaneity](https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/HPS_0410/chapters/Special_relativity_rel_sim/index.html) for relatively moving objects, and the universal nature of time along with it: there is no [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariance) notion of simultaneity, and hence no notion of a global ‘instant’ with which to constitute time. Simultaneity is an observer-dependent notion, and observers in different states of motion can disagree on whether two things occur simultaneously or otherwise, something [Rietdijk (1966)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/186637) and [Putnam (1967)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2024493), among others, use to great effect in arguing that the future and past are just as real as the present, contrary to our pre-theoretical intuitions.

Despite the demise of a global Now, concepts of time persist nonetheless in special and general relativity: for instance, we can find globally well-defined times (e.g. cosmic time) when spacetimes possess certain symmetries, and we can always find a Lorentz invariant proper time interval between any two points along time-like world-lines – the time read out by clocks moving along that line. Granted, these are not quite the universal, observer-independent, notions earlier thinkers like Aristotle or Newton had in mind. However, as Craig Callender points out in [*What Makes Time Special*](https://academic.oup.com/book/10106), there remains in general relativity “sharp and important distinctions between the timelike and spacelike directions of spacetime” which allows us to find some notions of time even in the relativistic setting.

**The Problem of Time**

In recent decades, however, the existence of time itself has come under attack, due in part to the aptly named “[Problem of Time](http://iai.tv/articles/tim-maudlin-einstein-didnt-think-time-was-an-illusion-auid-2317)”. This problem arises in the context of a particular strategy for unifying our best theory of matter, quantum mechanics, and our best theory of spacetime, general relativity: the canonical approach to quantum gravity. In this approach, we obtain a fundamental equation very much reminiscent of a time-dependent Schrodinger’s equation (TDSE), called the Wheeler-DeWitt equation (WDE). It differs from the TDSE in at least one crucial way. While the usual TDSE tells us how wave-functions representing the quantum state of ordinary matter evolve over time, the WDE seems to tell us that the quantum state of the world – matter and gravity included – does not change at all over time. The world is utterly ‘frozen’ and unchanging – timeless.

\_\_\_

On the one hand, one accepts that time doesn’t exist at the fundamental level. However, one provides a physical story for how time emerges – and therefore exists – at some non-fundamental level.”.

\_\_\_

Since systems apparently undergo dynamical evolution in our world, the onus is on proponents of canonical quantum gravity to explain this empirical data, and tell a story of why and how such dynamical goings-on emerge if the world is fundamentally frozen. There are, at a very general level, three sorts of responses one might give to this problem. It is important to emphasize that there remains no consensus on ‘the’ response to the Problem of Time: each response branches into various research programs, each with their own conceptual and technical challenges. (A thoroughgoing catalog of possible responses can be found in [Anderson (2010)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.2157).)

First, one can be a fundamentalist and recover time at the fundamental level. One might try to find parameters within the apparently frozen quantum state, which can play certain (if not all) roles we expect time to play, such as the role of driving dynamical evolution in the TDSE. This is something pursued, for example, by proponents of Bohmian quantum gravity ([Struyve 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-45434-9_15) is a recent example).

Second, one can be an eliminativist and do away with the existence of time altogether, biting the bullet on the Problem of Time. Instead they propose that we reinterpret physics without appealing explicitly to time-evolution at all. The most prominent proponents of such an attitude are perhaps [Julian Barbour’s](http://iai.tv/articles/time-is-the-increase-of-order-julian-barbour-auid-2195) many works on timeless physics, and more recently [Baron, Miller, and Tallant (2022).](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/out-of-time-9780192864888?cc=us&lang=en&)

Finally, one can adhere to what I call the middle way to the Problem of Time: on the one hand, one accepts that time doesn’t exist at the fundamental level: the world is fundamentally frozen. However, one provides a physical story for how time emerges – and therefore exists – at some non-fundamental level. For instance, we have such a story for temperature: we typically think that individual electrons are fundamental relative to an electron gas. Individual electrons don’t have a temperature, but an electron gas can be assigned one with statistical mechanics, and temperature emerges at the level of an electron gas. We can understand relations of fundamentality in terms of a layer cake: the base of the cake is ‘made out of’ non-temporal entities, on top of which temporal entities are ‘supported’. Of course, what it means for X to be ‘made out of’ Y, and for Y to ‘support’ X, will differ significantly depending on the conceptual resources and mathematical tools that one employs to tell their story.

**The Middle Way**

The middle way has attracted a lot of attention from philosophers and physicists because of its intriguing claim that time (and, often, spacetime) are emergent from fundamentally non-temporal ontology. If the middle way works out, it seems like we can have our layer cake and eat it, too: we can accept the Problem of Time but recover the time we know and love.

Schematically, the middle way proceeds in three steps. One, start with the fundamentally timeless ontology. Two, apply some set of formal tools – equations, approximations, idealizations, whatever we have in our mathematical toolbox – to the ontology. Three, obtain some formal product – some (sets of) solutions, some models, some parameters, and so on – that is to be interpreted temporally. I’ll briefly mention two stories for walking the middle way: semiclassical time and thermal time. Both begin from the fundamentally timeless ontology governed by the WDE, but take very different paths in trying to find time in a timeless world.

On the one hand, the semiclassical time approach (e.g., [Banks (1985)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0550321385900203) or [Kiefer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-58339-4_19) [(2004)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-58339-4_19)) proposes a set of semiclassical approximations: formal tools borrowed from ordinary physics such as decoherence, Born-Oppenheimer, and [WKB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WKB_approximation). The approximations are highly technical in nature, but they essentially let us factorize the quantum state of the timeless world into two parts, one representing the physics of matter, and another representing the physics of gravity and space. For instance, by assuming that gravitational physics is slow relative to the physics of matter, we can take the latter to be effectively independent of gravitational physics. Holding space fixed as quantum matter evolves, we can understand the variations of matter relative to this background space: we are able to use the gravitational part as a ‘clock’ for the matter part. Remarkably, doing so we obtain a Schrodinger-like equation for the variations of matter relative to fixed background space. If it walks and quacks like a duck, it’s a duck: we call the parameter of variation in this Schrodinger-like equation the time parameter. Of course, because these are approximations, the world remains fundamentally timeless: only after we discard terms here, ignore some terms there, assume factorization elsewhere, can we obtain time’s emergence. Hence: time emerges at the semiclassical level, the level at which these approximations can be applied.

On the other hand, the thermal time approach, pioneered by [Rovelli (1993)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0264-9381/10/8/015), proposes that time emerges from thermodynamics. Everywhere in ordinary thermodynamics, we think that equilibration takes time, and equilibrium occurs over time. A coffee cup takes time to cool down to room temperature, and a thermometer takes time to heat up. Connes and Rovelli’s key insight is that *if* we started with systems in states in thermal equilibrium, they satisfy a certain condition – the [KMS condition](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/KMSCondition.html) – which lets us define a temporal parameter along which the system is in equilibrium. So, knowing that a system is in equilibrium suffices also to define a time along which the system is in equilibrium. Hence, even if the world is fundamentally timeless, if there exists a level at which systems can be described as being in thermal equilibrium, then time emerges at the thermodynamic level.

Both approaches tackle the Problem of Time from different conceptual angles, but they are both about finding clocks in a world deemed to be timeless: gravitational clocks and thermal clocks. Both approaches latch onto different roles played by time in ordinary physics – in defining the Schrodinger equation, and in defining the notion of¨ thermal equilibrium – and then work backwards. If we can find things in the timeless world that play such roles, then we also recover time.

**Is the Middle Way Coherent?**

The proof of the pudding, then, is in the eating. Baron, Miller, and Tallant (2022) has recently argued that any claim of time’s emergence is incoherent from the perspective of good metaphysics. Any claim that time can emerge from no-time requires us to say that temporal things can metaphysically depend on non-temporal things. However, by interrogating standard definitions of metaphysical dependence, such as composition, constitution, and realization, they argue that standard accounts require the existence of spatiotemporal things to begin with, or they cannot be applied. For example, very briefly, for timeless things to be a part of temporal things seems to require that these parts share the same spatiotemporal locations as the whole: otherwise, *where and for how long* are the timeless things a part of temporal things? Likewise, if something plays the functional role of time, then that something just *is* temporal – an example of fundamentalism rather than the middle way.

While I agree with the spirit of the argument, I think that claims of metaphysical incoherence are too sweeping. If emergence is impossible by the very meaning of what it takes for emergence to occur, this rules out any particular program of temporal emergence – thermal time, semiclassical time, and so on – from the armchair, without ever requiring us to inspect which particular parts of the program are incoherent. Any claim of emergence is ruled out in virtue of the fact that the very notion of temporal emergence is metaphysically impossible. Furthermore, physicists working on the middle way are unlikely to be worried by esoteric concerns about the metaphysics of emergence, e.g., mereology or the nature of composition. Charges of metaphysical incoherence can be swept away by physicists as charges resting on a specific conception of emergence used by metaphysicians. In other words, these are worries *external* to the practice of physics.

Recently, I have been considering – and putting into practice – a more contextually situated standard internal to the physics, what I call physical coherence. At the very least, a physically coherent story or explanation of the derivation of time from no-time should have a consistent interpretation for: the fundamental timeless ontology, the formal tools, and the final temporal product. Starting with a physically interpreted timeless ontology, and physically interpreting each step involved in the proposed formal tool(s), we should arrive at the temporal product of the derivation with an intended physical interpretation without contradiction or circularity. Physical incoherence, then, arises when we cannot provide such a consistent story.

In “[No Time for Time from No-Time](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14854),” Craig Callender and I argue that semiclassical time falls into physical incoherence. For instance, we called out the use of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation – a common approximation used in e.g. nuclear physics to separate the physics of a ‘heavier’ nucleus’ from that of the ‘lighter’ electron. Textbook discussions of the approximation often suggest that ‘heavier’ is a proxy for ‘slower’, and ‘lighter’ is a proxy for ‘faster’: if so, what justified the use of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation in these cases is the distinct dynamical timescales had by electrons and nuclei – the usual time it takes for electrons to undergo a variety of changes is much faster than the time it takes for nuclei to respond to said changes and adjust, allowing us to dynamically separate electrons from atomic nuclei. Accepting this sort of justification, however, makes it hard to see how that approximation can apply in the case of a timeless world: since the total quantum state is timeless by hypothesis, what sense can we make of claims that the gravity part is ‘slower’ than the matter part without already assuming time to begin with? So, we cannot apply the Born-Oppenheimer approximation consistently when starting with a timeless world, and so we cannot complete our story and obtain the emergence of time without contradiction.

\_\_\_

The story requires finding systems in thermal equilibrium in a timeless world. However, if we started with a timeless world, it is unclear how we can find systems in thermal equilibrium since the concept of thermal equilibrium is typically defined in terms of time.

\_\_\_

In “[The Time in Thermal Time](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10838-024-09691-8),” I argue the same problem applies to the thermal time program. Broadly speaking, I attack the core assumption of thermal time: the story requires finding systems in thermal equilibrium in a timeless world. However, if we started with a timeless world, it is unclear how we can find systems in thermal equilibrium since the concept of thermal equilibrium is typically defined in terms of time. To push the intuition, we can find many systems in our world which are unchanging over space which are not in thermal equilibrium, such as a flat homogeneous piece of paper over a fire (it will soon combust). In a sense, it is stationary over some interval of space, but we would not say that the paper is in thermal equilibrium over that interval. So, it doesn’t suffice for us to find systems which are merely unchanging along some dimension in the timeless world (that dimension can be spatial) – we must also ascertain that these are unchanging over time (at least for some time), in order to say that they are in thermal equilibrium. But to do so requires assuming the existence of time in a yet timeless world.

Of course, I might be wrong in the end, and these programs may turn out to be physically coherent after all, given some new interpretation of the relevant physics. This is what Karim Thebault and Nick Huggett have argued in a [recent preprint](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24161/), in´ response to our analysis of semiclassical time. Ultimately, it’s work – hard work! – to be done to settle the question of physical coherence for each proposal for the middle way. In any case, I think the assessment of physical coherence is more fruitful than claims about metaphysical incoherence: it demands explanation and interpretation for the various formal moves made by proposals for the middle way, as well as careful scrutiny of the physics and where they might go wrong. For instance, one might argue for a timeless interpretation of, say, the Born-Oppenheimer approximation (as Huggett and Thebault does). This can lead to refinement and sophistication for the original´ proposal. While I don’t fully agree with their analysis, I think the debate is worthwhile nonetheless: either semiclassical time turns out to be physically incoherent, or not. Either way, we would have gained some new insights into quantum gravity and the middle way.

Time is indispensable to standard physics, yet in quantum gravity our equations paint a strangely frozen world. The middle way promises reconciliation, but whether it can do so without sacrificing physical coherence is far from settled. Ultimately, whether time genuinely emerges or simply never was, the nature of this dimension of our world remains obscured for now, a most familiar stranger. Only time will tell if its secrets are revealed.
