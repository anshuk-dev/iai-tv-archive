# The latest neuroscience may be proving Freud right

*An interview with Mark Solms*

**By Mark Solms** | *3rd August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 16 mins* | *3,263 words*

![The latest neuroscience may be proving Freud right](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-latest-neuroscience-may-be-proving-freud-right.webp)

---

*Neuropsychologist **Mark Solms** — the man entrusted with editing Freud's complete scientific works — argues that a century of neuroscience has been unknowingly proving psychoanalysis right. In this interview with Evie Manton, he traces our current theories of the unconscious, memory systems, and drive theory to their Freudian predecessors, and makes the case that following Freud back to the brainstem may hold the key to understanding consciousness itself.*

*Mark Solms will be appearing at the HowTheLightGetsIn festival this September, alongside other leading figures including Roger Penrose, Sabine Hossenfelder, Mariana Mazzucato, John Gray, and many many more. Find out more [here](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/london/big-ideas/speakers/2026).*

**Evie Manton: Most people think neuroscience buried Freud. You argue it has resurrected some of his deepest** **ideas. What exactly has neuroscience discovered that brought you to this view?**

Mark Solms: There are many things Freud discovered that we have rediscovered in neuroscience entirely independently of him. It's not as if we were following a trail Freud left—it was truly independent rediscovery.

Let me start with the headlines. First, the very idea that there is [an unconscious portion to our minds.](https://iai.tv/video/the-unconscious-mind) You might think this is taken for granted, but when Freud spoke of the unconscious mind, it was considered an oxymoron. To the Victorian ear, "unconscious mind" sounded like unconscious consciousness—mind and consciousness were synonymous. Now, in neuroscience, it is mainstream. It's an empirically proven fact that the vast bulk of our cognitive activities go on unconsciously.

Closely allied to that is the idea that we have multiple memory systems. Freud pioneered that too. If you Google who discovered multiple memory systems, you're told it was Tolman, in the 1920s—and to add insult to injury, Tolman was a bloody behaviourist! Freud introduced the model in 1896, exactly as we believe today. He said memories are transcribed not once but many times over; that it's a dynamic process; that they're transcribed in [different systems](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/special-issue-memory-and-desire-reading-freud) functioning according to different principles. He said there must be at least three: what he called the conscious, preconscious, and unconscious systems. Nowadays we call them short-term memory, declarative long-term memory, and non-declarative long-term memory. Exactly the same.

Then there's the idea [that early childhood development](https://iai.tv/articles/freud-vs-jung-trauma-extends-beyond-the-self-auid-3076) is particularly formative for the personality—and that early adverse childhood experiences are terribly important for vulnerability to psychopathology. The idea of infantile traumatic experiences that are pathogenic, of critical periods, of what we now theorize under epigenesis—all of that Freud set out.

The embodied nature of the mind. Nowadays we speak of embodied cognition. All of that is just Freud—the idea that the mind does work on behalf of the organism as a whole, captured in his concept of drive. [Damasio](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-begins-with-feelings-hanna-damasio-auid-2462), [Panksepp](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7219686/) and others now emphasize the homeostatic nature of mental processes. [Karl Friston](https://nautil.us/a-neuroscientists-theory-of-everything-237851) too. The whole paradigm of predictive processing—what is prediction but the belief that if I wish to reattain a previous experience of satisfaction, I must do this? That's what Freud called wish fulfillment.

\_\_\_

The feeling of an emotion is every bit as real as the existence of a chair. It's just easier to measure a chair.

\_\_\_

And the functionalism attributed to Hilary Putnam in the 50s, Freud introduced in 1900. He called this method of dissecting the function rather than the organ a scientific novelty, but "I can see no harm in it as long as we don't mistake the scaffolding for the building."

I'll end with feelings—the centrality of affect. The behaviorists called it the law of effect. It's actually the law of *affect*. The law says we avoid what is punishing and repeat what is rewarding—they said "punishing" rather than "unpleasurable" because unpleasure is a feeling. That is just the pleasure principle.

But above all, Freud started with the lived experience of the mind as his data. [David Chalmers's big gripe](https://iai.tv/articles/the-three-implausible-presuppositions-of-the-hard-problem-auid-1377) against cognitivism is: where does experience fit in? You have a mechanistic account of how the system processes information, but [why is there something it's like to be such a system](https://iai.tv/articles/will-we-ever-understand-consciousness-auid-1288)? This is the hard problem. For Freud it disappears, because he started from the other end. He didn't start with the mechanism and wonder about a ghost in the machine—he started with the ghost. Behaviorists tried to airbrush the psyche out of psychology. Freud accepted that the mind is subjective and worked from there. He didn't adjust the object of psychology to suit its methods; he adjusted its methods to suit its object.

So all of those things—unconscious cognition, multiple memory systems, embodied cognition, drives, the centrality of affect, early mental development, epigenesis, the functionalist approach, predictive processing as wishful processing, and the foregrounding of the being of the mind—are mainstream cognitive neuroscience today. And the last of them is at the absolute forefront of where neuroscience is going. Sorry, a long answer to a short question.

**Why was 20th-century neuroscience so resistant to subjective experience? And why did it let it back in?**

Science aspires to objectivity. The whole modus operandi is to exclude everything subjective. But behaviorism went further—it said subjective phenomena don't exist. Skinner said, and I'm quoting, "emotions are an excellent example of the fictitious explanations that we give to behavior." Emotions, for him, were fictions. What planet was he living on? Who thinks emotions are fictions? I mean, bloody hell.

Since Galileo, the methodology of science has been to exclude the subjective—which is fine, except when it comes to that part of nature which *is* subjective. In other words, the mind. It was Freud's great contribution to bring into science the empirical fact that part of nature is subjective. Incorporating it into science was bloody hard, and there was great resistance because it was a massive novelty—so it was considered not science. But it may be hard, and it's still there. Your most immediate experience tells you subjectivity exists. Somehow we have to incorporate it into the fabric of science.

Why have things changed? Ironically, because we've made such progress in objective neuroscience that we can now monitor the physiological correlates of subjective states, which tethers them to objective observables—optogenetics, fMRI, PET, deep brain stimulation.  But—and I need to emphasize this—that doesn't mean reducing subjective phenomena to their objective correlates. They are only correlates. The subjective phenomena are every bit as real as the objective ones. The feeling of an emotion is every bit as real as the existence of a chair. It's just easier to measure a chair.

I truly believe you can't study the brain only the way you study the liver—because there is something it's like to be a brain, and there isn't something it's like to be a liver. You'll never understand how the brain works if you don't take account of the causal consequences of its subjective states. What I do has everything to do with what I feel.

**Will that necessarily subjective element always place a ceiling on the degree to which we can say we've truly understood the mind?**

I don't know why it would. I follow Freud's lead: we shouldn't be daunted by the study of subjectivity, we should embrace it.

Let me be clear. When I said Freud was a functionalist, he called it [metapsychology](https://archive.org/details/freudsothertheor0000faye). You start with the phenomenal experiencing of being, and by studying it systematically you seek the underlying organizing principles behind subjective experience. You're no longer just describing conscious states; you're discerning the functional organization of the apparatus that produces them. And that apparatus is exactly what we're studying in the cognitive neurosciences—memory systems, executive systems, perceptual systems, language systems. We don't want to just describe the brain's anatomy; we want to discern its functional organization. That is the common ground between mental science and neuroscience. Those organizing laws are the explanators of both physiology and psychology. How the brain works and how the mind works must be reducible to a common set of laws.

\_\_\_

Subjective states have causal powers. If you leave out the feeling, you won't understand why we do what we do. Look at drugs of abuse—what are they for? Feelings.

\_\_\_

So I don't think we'll always be groping in the dark—far from it. There are viewpoint-dependent errors that get corrected. You'll never understand the brain if you study it only objectively, but you'll also never understand it if you study it only subjectively. By having access to both observational perspectives, we're going to be on much more secure ground.

**You use Karl Friston's Free Energy Principle to translate Freud into contemporary neuroscience. For those who haven't encountered it, what is it, and why do you think it maps so well onto Freud?**

That's easy to answer. Friston's work starts in the thermodynamics of Helmholtz—that's the ground zero on which predictive processing and the free energy principle are based. Freud's work was likewise based on Helmholtzian thermodynamics, so they share a common intellectual heritage, and Friston acknowledges this. In 2010 he [published a paper](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/133/4/1265/307446) in the neurological journal *Brain* saying exactly that: the Freudian metapsychology is entirely consistent with the free energy principle, and the reason is not hard to find—a common origin in Helmholtzian thermodynamics. So when you say I use Friston's work to update Freud, that's absolutely true—but I'm pleased to say I have him alongside me doing it. He's absolutely on board with that project.

**Freud's drive theory has often been dismissed as mythology. What does modern affective neuroscience tell us about the brain's motivational systems—how right was Freud, and where was he wrong?**

First, I think you cannot have a mental science without a drive theory. Every mind comes with a body, and it's doing work on behalf of the body. The connection between mind and body is drive. And there isn't only one drive energy—there are multiple categories, which is why they're qualitatively distinctive. Hunger feels different from thirst, which feels different from sleepiness, because these are different categories of demand, each of which must be met in its own right.

Freud's drive theory was pretty much the first. In fact he had two: one formulated in 1915, then a revised version in 1920. When he formulated the final theory, he said we are groping in the dark, because we need biology to guide us. He fully expected to be corrected, especially regarding the classification of drives. And he has been. Freud thought there were two major motivating forces. If we follow Jaak Panksepp's taxonomy, there are [seven emotional drives](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/affective-neuroscience-jaak-panksepps-rat-tickling-theory-of-emotion/BF3A3A3C18BCC5CB750130D17B6F32FC)—plus many bodily drives like hunger and thirst which aren't emotions. That's a huge adjustment—but one Freud fully expected.

The fundamental mistake Freud made is that he thought the pursuit of pleasure for its own sake was synonymous with sexuality. His view was: if the motivation is the attainment of pleasure, let's call it sexual. So a baby suckling at the breast who is no longer hungry—suckling because it feels nice—Freud calls that sexual. To most people that's weird. To say a baby suckling is having sexual relations with its mother makes people say, "This guy's crazy."

He wasn't crazy. He had a point: what motivates us is indeed the feeling, even in actual sex. Why do we have sex? Not to procreate—most of the time we're hoping *not* to, hence contraception. Heterosexual sex is for pleasure, let alone homosexual sex, which can't produce babies, let alone masturbation, let alone all the other interesting things people get up to. What motivates us in sexual life is the pleasure. But Freud then generalized and said kids squealing with delight on the playground is therefore sexual—and that's a mistake. The mistake is this: there are many different types of pleasure in the brain. That baby suckling non-nutritively is suckling for attachment—a drive all its own, the drive to have your caregiver close at hand. There's a warm, fuzzy pleasure in being with mummy, which isn't sexual; it's a need in its own right. In fact, juvenile mammals literally can't survive without caregivers. So we need to attach, and there are many such needs, each with its own category of pleasure.

So Freud was right—and it's not a small point—to observe that what motivates us is the feeling. Subjective states have causal powers. If you leave out the feeling, you won't understand why we do what we do. Look at drugs of abuse—what are they for? Feelings. This is the division between biology and psychology. The biologist says sex is for procreation; the psychologist says sex is for pleasure. From the point of view of the subject, it's the feeling that drives us. Freud was simply wrong to think there's only one type of pleasure. Bottom line. End of story.

**Let's move to repression, which we tend to think of as metaphorical, or as an act you do deliberately. What is actually happening in the brain when something is repressed?**

Let me start from what I said earlier: the unconscious, as Freud called it, we call in cognitive neuroscience the non-declarative long-term memory system. Non-declarative memories are things we've learnt that influence what we do without our being aware of them. In Fristonian terms, these are predictions—memories are about the past but for the future. The whole point of learning from past experience is to better predict what to do next. These predictions run unconsciously: we execute them without any awareness that we're doing so, or where they come from.

The non-declarative memory systems are a big domain. A subset of them is the repressed. The repressed are predictions that have been automatized—consolidated into the non-declarative systems where they run automatically—but automatized illegitimately or prematurely. Even though they don't hit the mark—don't satisfy the drive or meet the need—they were the best the child could come up with. The child concludes "it works like this" and puts it out of mind.

Normally, when a prediction is not upheld, it returns to consciousness, to a state of uncertainty, and gets updated. But once a prediction is deeply consolidated into the non-declarative systems, it's no longer subject to updating. The repressed are precisely those which *should* come back for updating, because there's prediction error. That's what distinguishes repressed non-declarative predictions from ordinary ones.

There are many problems you can't solve, especially as a child. So rather than fretfully exercising yourself over an insoluble problem, the child cuts its losses and automatizes the least-bad prediction it can come up with. Freud called these wishes—just another word for unrealistic predictions.

Crucially: the non-repressed unconscious prediction hits the mark, so it doesn't arouse feeling. The repressed ones create prediction error, homeostatic deviation. You're not meeting the need, so the feeling still reaches consciousness, but it no longer gives rise to reconsolidation. So the person experiences unpleasures without knowing where they come from. They don't understand why they feel like shit—because they don't know what unconscious childhood predictions they're executing, like hitting their head against the same brick wall.

But it's not only the child putting things out of mind. In the first two years of life the declarative systems—what Freud called the preconscious memory systems—aren't matured yet. So everything you learn then, including who to attach to and how, is perforce non-declarative. That's what Freud called infantile amnesia—it's partly developmental or maturational. Those two mechanisms—maturational, and the later cutting of losses—are how we understand how the repressed comes about.

**If we can’t access these predictions, how do we go about fixing them?**

In psychoanalysis, that's called transference. The repressed predictions can't be remembered, but they can be repeated—enacted. The non-declarative memory systems are subcortical—primarily basal ganglia, but also amygdala, cerebellum. They're motor systems, action systems, not thought systems. You don't think these things; you do them. So remembering an unconscious prediction *is* to enact it.

Although the patient can't bring the original strategy to mind in verbal form, they're enacting it here and now. So you can draw their attention to it. Schematically—one doesn't actually speak like this to patients—you say: "Can you see you're doing this over and again? Can you see it's meant to have this outcome? Can you see it doesn't have that outcome? Can you see that's why you're suffering this feeling?" This connects the feeling and the failure to meet the need with the enacted prediction, and problematizes it.

An important part is that you're repeating a childhood situation that isn't here anymore—behaving as if the present were the same as the past. Transference interpretation makes the patient aware of this, creating the opportunity to find better ways of meeting those same emotional needs. It frees the patient from compulsion and gives them a choice—which is the great aim.

**What changes in the brain when psychoanalysis works?**

First, fMRI studies demonstrate the brain changes with psychoanalytic treatment—obviously, because if the mind changes, the brain changes. But because of the materialist bias of science, we're more persuaded when we can see it. And you can see the brain change, both functionally and structurally.

The brain has two kinds of decision-maker. The frontal cortex is the great conscious decision-maker, and the basal ganglia are the unconscious ones, connected by the frontostriatal loops. Treatment shifts control from the basal ganglia's automatic, stereotyped action policies to cortical ones, where you actually think and take ownership of your own volition. It's a corticalization—in imaging you see increased prefrontal activation.

And there's good evidence it works. One of the biggest fibs in modern mental science is that psychoanalysis isn't evidence-based. In medicine we speak of effect sizes: 0.8 is highly effective. Psychoanalytic psychotherapy averages around 0.8. Short-term psychoanalytic psychotherapy is no better than [CBT](https://iai.tv/video/the-search-for-wellbeing-mental-health)—but that's because we reduce it to the same number of sessions. The big difference is at follow-up. CBT gets good results which slowly wane—hence "top-up" treatments. With psychoanalytic psychotherapy, symptom improvement actually continues. It's called the sleeper effect. To me there's no better evidence that we've treated the *cause*: the therapeutic action continues after treatment has ended.

**Are there areas where neuroscience still lacks answers that we could look to Freud on?**

We're at exactly that point. I think we're in a golden age of neuroscience. First, we've moved beyond peppercorn questions to the big picture, and Freud was a big-picture man. Second, it’s widely accepted the hard problem of consciousness is the biggest unanswered question in neuroscience—and here Freud has something special to offer.

One reason we've made so little progress on the hard problem is that we've focused on higher, cortical, cognitive forms of consciousness—mainly cortical visual perception—ever since Crick made consciousness serious in the 1990s. But the crucial thing about vision, and all cortical functions, is that they don't *need* consciousness. You can recognize a face unconsciously; you can read with comprehension unconsciously. So philosophers like Chalmers say: when you explain visual information processing, it's not clear why there has to be consciousness or what it adds. That's the hard problem.

I think if we'd started lower down—in the brainstem, the ancient part of the brain that was Freud's stomping ground, and in particular two structures, the periaqueductal grey and the reticular activating system—the problem wouldn't have arisen. These are the arousal systems; they switch on the lights. Cortex only becomes conscious because it's aroused by the reticular activating system. And these brainstem arousal systems are the [homeostatic affective systems](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-begins-with-feelings-hanna-damasio-auid-2462) we discussed. So affect—feeling—is an intrinsically conscious function. You can't have a feeling without feeling it; *that* truly is an oxymoron. Raw affective arousal is the foundation of consciousness.

The hard problem rests on the assumption that explaining the mechanism doesn't explain the experience. But if you explain the mechanism of feeling, you *have* to explain the experience—because that's what feeling is. Feeling is *for* feeling.

So, can Freud lead the way for contemporary neuroscience? Yes—by redirecting us away from all this cognitive, intellectual complexity down to the affective feeling bedrock: back to the drives, the pleasure principle, the affect at the core of what makes us do everything we do. That, I think, is the way to solve the hard problem.
