In recent years, a new paradigm in gravitational physics research emerged from information theory and information physics developments. Using the second law of information dynamics and the mass–energy–information equivalence principle, gravitational attraction can be interpreted as a data compression process that minimizes the information entropy associated with matter distributions in space. In this framework, matter evolves toward configurations that reduce informational complexity, producing an entropic force that manifests macroscopically as gravity. Remarkably, this information-theoretic approach naturally recovers Newton’s law of gravitation, suggesting that gravity may arise from fundamental information dynamics and may represent a computational optimization process occurring within the physical universe.

Gravity research has entered a period of renewed conceptual exploration driven by persistent empirical puzzles that challenge the completeness of both Newtonian gravity and General Relativity (GR). While GR has achieved extraordinary success across a vast range of scales, from solar-system dynamics to gravitational waves, it relies on the existence of dark matter and dark energy to explain observations at galactic and cosmological scales. Although these components together constitute roughly 95% of the inferred energy density of the universe, neither of them has been directly detected yet. Modified gravity models arise from the possibility that the apparent need for these unseen components reflects an incomplete understanding of gravitational physics itself. There are also additional key empirical motivations driving further developments of new gravity models.

The first major empirical motivation for modified gravity emerged from galactic rotation curves. Stars in spiral galaxies orbit at almost constant speeds far beyond the visible matter distribution, contradicting the Newtonian expectation that orbital velocity should decline with distance from the galactic center. Within GR, this discrepancy is resolved by postulating massive halos of non-luminous dark matter, while Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), addresses this by proposing [a modification to Newton’s gravitational force law](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...270..365M/abstract) at accelerations below a characteristic scale (~10^-10 m s^-2).

A second empirical motivation arises from the dynamics of galaxy clusters and large-scale structures. While dark matter models explain cluster masses inferred from gravitational lensing and velocity dispersions, modified gravity approaches [attempt to account for these phenomena through changes to the gravitational field equations](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.70.083509).

A third motivation is the accelerated expansion of the universe. Within GR, this requires dark energy, often modelled as a cosmological constant whose observed value is extraordinarily small compared to theoretical expectations from quantum field theory, creating the cosmological constant problem. [Modified gravity models](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2476) instead attribute cosmic acceleration to large-scale modifications of gravitational dynamics, [such as *f(R)* gravity](https://journals.aps.org/rmp/abstract/10.1103/RevModPhys.82.451). Moreover, tensions in the Hubble constant measurements, most notably the discrepancy between local and early-universe determinations, have prompted investigations into whether [modified gravitational dynamics](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021CQGra..38o3001D/abstract) could alleviate these inconsistencies.

Additionally, a fundamental gap in modern physics is the incompatibility between GR and quantum mechanics, with current theoretical efforts concentrated on a possible [quantum gravity theory](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/quantum-gravity-9780198842132?cc=gb&lang=en&), [string theory](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/string-theory/30409AF2BDE27D53E275FDA395AB667A), and [Loop Quantum Gravity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/covariant-loop-quantum-gravity/2DF4474CBF7845C261FA78904270F226) (LQG). Finally, a new field of research involves [emergent gravity scenarios](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP04(2011)029), in which spacetime and gravity arise from more fundamental information-theoretic structures. Across many of these programs, a common theme has emerged: the possibility that space-time is not fundamental but emergent, and information may play a central role in its structure.

In a study published in April 2025, I proposed an [information-theoretical approach to gravitational physics](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/15/4/045035/3345217/Is-gravity-evidence-of-a-computational-universe). At the core of this approach is the assumption that spacetime geometry becomes discrete at the Planck scale. This discrete view of space is supported by evidence from quantum mechanics, the discovery of Planck quanta, and subsequently the Planck scales. Essentially, spacetime gives meaning to physical properties of material objects such as position and momentum, and for this reason space could be regarded as an information storage medium of matter properties in the universe. In this approximation, a quantum of space, or an elementary cell, would have the function to register properties of matter in space, including position and velocity, acting as storage of information. The dynamics of objects in space would involve changes in this space-time information registry. [Richard Feynman proposed a similar idea](https://www.ling.upenn.edu/~kroch/courses/lx550/readings/feynman1-4.pdf) more than 30 years ago:

*There is a number at every point in space. The force on an object at a point in space will be in the direction that the register in space changes as quickly as it can. The force is proportional to how fast it changes as it moves.*

It is natural to assume that Feynman’s “point in space,” or the elementary space cell proposed by myself, is equal to a Planck area, storing one digital bit state in its surface area. This implies that information is stored in 2Dspace surfaces, which is consistent with the Holographic Principle and [Beckenstein’s Black Hole entropy studies](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.7.2333). The 3Dspace is then constructed from stacked 2D space surfaces, like a foliation of space in surfaces like an onion structure.

In this approach, each cell can register information as binary data. A simplified model assumes that if a cell is empty, it registers a digital *0*, and if matter is present, then a cell registers a digital *1*. If each elementary cell of space is seen as an event in [Shannon’s](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf) [Information Theory framework](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf), then an empty space surface has a 100% probability of finding a digital *0*, so it contains zero bits of information. If matter objects are present in a region of space, then the information content registered in that region of space is no longer zero bits. Because the system is informational in nature, [the second law of infodynamics governs its dynamics](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/12/7/075310/2819368/Second-law-of-information-dynamics). This law states that the entropy associated with informational states must decrease or remain constant as the system evolves toward equilibrium, and was introduced as [a cosmological necessity](https://ipipublishing.org/index.php/ipil/RR), being demonstrated for digital and biological [information states](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/13/10/105308/2915332/The-second-law-of-infodynamics-and-its), ground-state electronic arrangements on atomic orbitals, as well as successfully explaining the abundance of [symmetries in the universe](https://ipipublishing.org/index.php/ipil/article/view/137). Consequently, the spatial distribution of matter tends to evolve toward configurations that minimize informational entropy, or the total information content in space. This is in total contrast to the second law of thermodynamics, which requires the entropy of physical states to remain constant or increase over time. Assuming again the point mass approximation, which implies that a cell can accommodate more than one particle, then the many-particle system will evolve itself by moving the particles in space to join them together into a single larger particle inside a single cell. The final state will therefore consist of a single cell occupied by a particle of mass equal to the sum of all the other particles, located in the 2Dspace at the coordinates corresponding to the center of mass of all the particles.

This is the lowest possible Shannon information entropy and, by extension, the lowest entropy of the information states. The total mass in this system is conserved, but merging the particles together into a combined particle minimized the entropy of information, and the system will remain perpetually in this state at equilibrium.

As dictated by the second law of infodynamics, the requirement to reduce the entropy of information generated an attractive entropic force between the particles. One immediately notices that the resulting entropic attractive force possesses all the defining characteristics of gravitational interaction. Matter objects attract one another even in the absence of other known forces, and the direction of motion corresponds to configurations that minimize the informational description of the system.

Newton’s classical law of gravity and Einstein’s GR theory of gravity, despite their absolute success in describing classical and relativistic gravitational phenomena, do not explain why matter objects attract each other gravitationally.

In this informational approach, the gravitational attractive force is entropic, and it is triggered to fulfil the requirements of the second law of infodynamics, minimizing the information content, and, by extension, reducing the computational power requirements. To put it simply, it is far more computationally effective to track and compute the location and momentum of a single object in space than *n* objects.

Hence, gravitational attraction is an entropic force that emerges as an optimization mechanism in a computational process that has the role of reducing the computational power and of compressing information. Remarkably, [using only information dynamics](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/9/9/095206/1076232/The-mass-energy-information-equivalence-principle), I demonstrated mathematically [that this entropic force is exactly equal to Newton’s classical law of gravitational attraction](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/15/4/045035/3345217/Is-gravity-evidence-of-a-computational-universe).

The idea of gravity being an entropic force is not new. Verlinde [first proposed this idea in 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP04(2011)029). Following Verlinde’s work, [other studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP10(2011)140) [demonstrated](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP10(2011)140) [the entropic nature of gravity](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10701-012-9660-x), including [testing](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/466/3/2547/2661916) [the theory](https://iopscience.iop.org/journal/1475-7516) [on empirical data](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.102.084007). In fact, [Einstein’s field equations emerge naturally](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.75.1260) from the proportionality of entropy and horizon area, or information-entropic principles.

However, the 2025 study arrives at a similar conclusion by taking a distinct approach, with key differences to Verlinde’s work. While Verlinde’s approach derives gravitational attraction from entropic force considerations linked to holographic screens, my study emphasizes the [second law of infodynamics as the primary driver, combined with the M\E\I equivalence principle](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/9/9/095206/1076232/The-mass-energy-information-equivalence-principle), both concepts being absent in Verlinde’s formulation. Hence, the gravitational force emerges as an optimization process where matter self-organizes in space to reduce information entropy. This is in direct contrast to Verlinde’s work, where his entropic force points in the direction of increasing entropy. Verlinde claims that the “dynamics of information stored on each screen is given by some unknown rules, which can be thought of as a way of processing the information that is stored on it.” I show that the second law of infodynamics governs the dynamics of information, without the need for the [emergent space-time](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-emergence) concept and arbitrary introduction of holographic screens. The broader implications of this work extend to fundamental physics, including [black-hole thermodynamics](https://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-white-holes-and-the-relational-world-auid-3085), [dark matter](https://iai.tv/video/the-dark-universe) and [dark energy](https://iai.tv/video/dark-energy-and-the-universe), as well as potential connections between gravity and [quantum information theory](https://iai.tv/video/ivette-fuentes). One common theme that emerges is the overlooked role of information in a possibly computational universe.

Feynman touched on the informational nature of physics later in his life (notably in his work on cellular automata and “Simulating Physics with Computers”), when he famously said, “It’s not just that the world is a computer, but that the laws of physics are the software.”

If information is indeed a fundamental physical quantity, then gravitational attraction may represent the universe’s mechanism for minimizing informational complexity. From this perspective, gravity may ultimately be viewed as the universe performing data compression.
