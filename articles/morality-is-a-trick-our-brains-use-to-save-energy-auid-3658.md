# Our brains use morality to save energy

*Moral obligations help brains bring order to a chaotic world*

**By Jordan Theriault-Brown** | *19th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,488 words*

![Our brains use morality to save energy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/morality-is-a-trick-our-brains-use-to-save-energy3.webp)

---

*We are often guided by a gut sense of good and evil. But where does that feeling come from? Psychologist **Jordan Theriault-Brown** argues it has its roots in how a brain manages the uncertainty of the world around it, including the metabolic costs of that uncertainty. Moral feelings enforce rules that make our behavior predictable to ourselves and others. The social and psychological dynamics that follow from this energy-saving trick can motivate violence as well as bravery. This idea can explain behavior that evolutionary accounts struggle with, such as extreme self-sacrifice. When someone dies for a cause, Theriault-Brown suggests, it’s because abandoning their most foundational moral principles would force their brain to rebuild its entire model of who they are.*

Why would someone feel a duty to die for a cause? Think of Joan of Arc on the pyre, soldiers charging into machine gun fire, or even terrorists in a suicide attack. Morality is often thought to be the angel on our shoulder, motivating us to do good, but in reality, morality is much more complicated. It can motivate great acts of bravery, but it can also motivate horrendous acts of violence, all in the name of what is felt to be “right.”

Where does this feeling of duty come from, and how might it even motivate [self-sacrifice](https://iai.tv/video/loving-oneself-and-loving-others)? This question is particularly puzzling from an evolutionary perspective: if our behaviors are [“programmed” into us by our genes](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.134.3489.1501), and if those genes were selected to promote survival, then how could someone find themselves feeling bound by duty to die for a cause? The usual evolutionary answers of kin selection and reciprocal altruism aren’t helpful here for obvious reasons (those charging soldiers aren’t related, and Joan of Arc won’t be around to receive favors in turn). Likewise, [reputation-based](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S0022-5193(01)92406-3) [explanations](https://www.nature.com/articles/31225) aren’t fully satisfying either, and generally assume you’ll be around to reap the benefits of your actions. One could invoke belief in God and rewards in the afterlife to explain it, but [nonreligious people also engage in moral self-sacrifice](https://www.plough.com/en/topics/faith/a-nonbeliever-grapples-with-self-sacrifice)—for example, protestors have risked their lives to do what they feel is right, and [doctors have died to give aid in war zones](https://www.msf.org/remembering-our-colleagues-killed-gaza). Was *every* protestor and doctor compelled by rewards from God, or by some reputational or evolutionary self-interest? Or, [as Kant proposed](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/), might moral duty be something recognizable only when it runs counter to our preservation, welfare, and happiness? If we’re willing to grant the psychological fact that we can, at times, feel compelled by duty (deontology means “the science of duty”), then how could that feeling of duty emerge from the meat of a brain?

To answer this, we need to first take a step back from morality and consider the broader category of norms.

While morality concerns all the more dramatic stuff—[good, evil](https://iai.tv/articles/hobbes-vs-rousseau-are-we-inherently-evil-or-good-auid-1221), life, death, and so on—norms refer to the more common kinds of behaviors expected of us in the day to day. Social pressure pushes us to conform to these norms, which are really just the collective expectations of others. This normative social pressure has a certain feeling to it: you don’t just *want* to conform, or *choose* to conform, but rather, you feel like you really *should* conform. This isn’t the full-blown duty of moral obligation yet, but violating norms does feel bad in a particular way—for example, think about the feeling of showing up to a Halloween party as the only one without a costume ([or as the only one wearing one](https://x.com/katiedippold/status/748582543583121408)).

\_\_\_

It’s not others’ threats of punishment or ostracism that guide norm compliance; rather, it’s the simple avoidance of metabolic and affective discomfort.

\_\_\_

We call this feeling a [*sense of should*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S157106452030004X), and if you, right now, mentally simulate jumping onto a table and screaming at the top of your lungs in a crowded cafe, then you may be able to make yourself feel it too: a kind of anticipatory anxiety about violating the expectations of the people around you. Essentially, an anxiety about disrupting your local social niche, breaking the spell that lets you fade into the background of most social situations and rise to the attention of others only when you are prepared to. In other words, conforming to norms (which are the collective expectations of others) allows you to exercise a special kind of social control—control over others’ reactions (or more accurately, their nonreactions) to your behavior. A *sense of should* is the affective experience that drives you to exercise this kind of social control: you anticipate that violating norms will feel bad, and so you conform to them. You are controlled by social pressure from others, but [you also control others](https://iai.tv/articles/the-anxiety-of-trying-to-control-everything-auid-2840) (indirectly, by controlling their reactions) by acceding to that social pressure.

Why would you feel an anxious *sense of should* at the prospect of violating others’ expectations? To dip a toe into the neural explanation: brains process sensory input, but they must do so efficiently, as neural signaling [eats up the lion’s share of energy used by the brain](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1097/00004647-200110000-00001). To [encode signals efficiently](https://www.cnbc.cmu.edu/~tai/microns_papers/Barlow-SensoryCommunication-1961.pdf), the brain can do what is done in digital image compression (e.g., jpeg file formats): it can [filter predictable signals](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/whatever-next-predictive-brains-situated-agents-and-the-future-of-cognitive-science/33542C736E17E3D1D44E8D03BE5F4CD9) and limit itself to encoding prediction error (in classic information theory approaches, information is equivalent to prediction error). The claim, then, is that novel or unexpected input to the brain likely has a metabolic cost above and beyond familiar or predictable input. By this same reasoning, learning likely has a surplus metabolic cost, as learning, by definition, describes how the brain adjusts itself to deal with novel and unfamiliar circumstances.

It has been suggested that [all living things must be motivated to minimize prediction error](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston), but what we propose sidesteps this debate, relying only on the simpler assumption that processing novelty and uncertainty has a metabolic cost. Our other assumption is that metabolic regulation [sits at the heart](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-94-017-9837-2) of [what a living system must control](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262551328/a-drive-to-survive/), and so the metabolic consequences of action have likely evolved to act as a key constraint on behavior.

If this logic is correct, then violating norms has metabolic consequences. When you violate norms, you force others to react and change in response, making their behavior become (even more of) a moving target for you to predict. Most of the time, your social niche is at least somewhat predictable—people walk past, continue their conversations, and generally go about their business independent of you—meaning that your brain can filter the predictable parts of what you sense, lowering the metabolic costs of encoding. But your actions can make that same social niche unpredictable, and metabolic costs follow. If you violate a norm, like if you were to scream and jump on the table, then people will stop, notice you, and start reacting to your presence in ways that are less easily predicted than the status quo you just disturbed. Your social niche was once predictably proceeding independently of you; now it’s yoked to your behavior, straying far from well-learned predictable patterns that prior experience encoded into your brain’s model of the world. This generates [prediction error](iai-academy/courses/info?course=predicting-ourselves), which again, in a brain using efficient strategies for signal encoding, should carry a metabolic cost above and beyond a predictable status quo.

The metabolic details of prediction error encoding are an area of open research ([although we have some leads and hypotheses](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105373)). But notably, large unanticipated errors drive [a sympathetic response, along with norepinephrine (aka noradrenaline) release](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/10.1080/09548980601004024?url_ver=Z39.88-2003&rfr_id=ori:rid:crossref.org&rfr_dat=cr_pub%20%200pubmed). Psychologically, this sympathetic response corresponds with an [affective state](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10353204/) of “high arousal”—a jittery or alert feeling. So, by conforming to norms, you control the metabolic costs of your social niche, your sympathetic response, and the affective arousal you experience. Conforming to norms dials these down, and violating norms dials them up—a learnable cause-effect relationship. We learn to control the metabolic and affective consequences of our social niche, and we do so by controlling how our behavior aligns to others’ expectations.

Of course, [if you just want to get a thrill out of shocking people](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-news/flashback-tom-greens-single-most-surreal-sketch-94771/), then you can break social norms and stick out intentionally (high arousal can feel good, under the right circumstances). Violating expectations in a controlled way can also help you learn, by exploring people’s reactions (like children testing boundaries). There are many exceptions, but as a baseline strategy following the rules has concrete benefits: if we didn’t have a steady predictable social baseline to return to, then we’d be under constant metabolic and affective stress. On our account, social pressure acts through a subtle force. It’s not others’ threats of punishment or ostracism that guide norm compliance; rather, it’s the simple avoidance of metabolic and affective discomfort, both of which we can bring upon ourselves by breaking norms. But this subtle force creates a dynamic with powerful emergent properties. When we conform to norms, and when we inadvertently police these norms for others through our surprise at norm violations, we create a baseline of collective social stability. This is a social scaffold that makes society possible.

What if you can’t help but stick out? What if you have a disability, [wear a mask when most do not](https://whn.global/youve-got-a-friend-in-me-tom-hanks-shows-subway-riders-how-to-protect-their-health/) ([or vice versa](https://slate.com/technology/2021/06/masks-shopping-psychology-social-norms.html)), can’t speak fluently in the dominant language, or happen to be the only woman or person of color in the room? A *sense of should* can give insight into why being different can be so uncomfortable. It’s not that [evolutionary mechanisms for group formation](https://iai.tv/articles/humanitys-ancient-tribalism-is-causing-its-modern-problems-auid-3252) *make* in-group majorities exclude outgroup minorities. Rather, group formation may emerge from the social dynamics of discomfort and its subtle motivational force. If you are different, you create prediction error: people notice and treat you as an exception, as something unfamiliar that they still need to learn about, rather than as something that fits squarely within the well-worn paths of their prior experience. Much like how a tennis swing feels off if you pay too much attention to it, over-attending to a social interaction (because it is seen as exceptional) may foster a kind of [social dysfluency](https://academic.oup.com/book/4847/chapter-abstract/147179840). Others may experience your social identity as a source of social friction—not necessarily out of any ill will toward you, but rather, out of simpler mechanisms of over-attending to a dysfluent source of prediction error, along with the metabolic costs and affective discomfort such prediction error brings with it.

This is not to excuse such behavior. Quite the opposite: these simple mechanisms can snowball to catastrophic effect. Majority members may feel they are just subtly reacting to a small discomfort; yet, their aggregate behavior is magnified in the experience of the minority they’re reacting to. From this perspective, in-group/out-group formation may not be innately embedded by evolution into our psychology. Rather, it may be an emergent consequence of how we stabilize our social niche and seek comfort within it. [Groupiness](https://www.aspeninstitute.org/blog-posts/the-science-of-groupiness/) is an emergent property, rather than primitive human nature. Optimistically, this means that the formation of out-groups may be surmountable by interventions that foster learning and familiarity. If people could be familiarized with these interpersonal differences, then we might reduce the “strangeness” of others, unburdening minorities from having to [code switch](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/code-switching) to comfort the majority.

What does all of this have to do with duty-bound self-sacrifice? A *sense of should* compels norm compliance. It describes the affective experience of normative social pressure. This may already be enough to explain some of our initial examples: after spending months or years with your fellow soldiers, do you really want to be the only one refusing to go [over the top](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5tadlNbkN2o)? Likewise, many terrorist attacks can be traced to [small group dynamics](https://www.amazon.com/Talking-Enemy-Brotherhood-Making-Terrorists-ebook/dp/B003VIWNNA) and the [feelings of obligation within them](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/virtuous-violence/0BC95A5426105D04AD30DF7EFA1C0739). Norm compliance and a *sense of should* act to stabilize a social niche, but that niche can be a small, local network, even if that network is hostile to the society that surrounds it. This social dynamic is a double-edged sword: it can foster information bubbles, [conspiracy](https://iai.tv/articles/the-truth-about-conspiracy-theories-auid-1624), and extremism, but it can also foster solidarity and community. Terrorists and activists may both feel compelled by communal expectations and social pressure from their local social niche.

But what about Joan of Arc? Her dutiful self-sacrifice involved refusing to bend to expectations or social pressure—Joan of Arc was not a norm follower, to say the least. To answer this, [we’ve suggested that both social pressure and moral duty may flow from similar](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/42juc_v1) underlying pressures, while producing very different dynamics. The difference has to do with what social niche is being stabilized. Conforming to social pressure stabilizes the local social niche, but sometimes this conformity might violate more deeply held principles or maxims. If those maxims are foundational to how your brain models your social world and your place within it, then you’ll be loath to abandon them. Such maxims are deeply learned and reinforced across your entire life, and might include, for example, faith in God, political/ideological commitments, or a [generalized prohibition against physical harm](https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2012.651387)—for example, if you’ve never physically attacked anyone, then your understanding of the world (i.e., your predictions) may be premised on an assumption that you’ll continue to avoid such intentional physical harm, leading you to construct a maxim against interpersonal violence. Violating such maxims may [loosen core predictions](https://archive.org/details/webofbelief0000quin/page/n9/mode/2up) that place bounds on your understanding of who you are, what you value, and what you are capable of. The content of these maxims is not necessarily universal, and their development is not inevitable, but, in your mind, so much might be premised on them that they become load-bearing for your personal identity. You could violate them, but you may struggle to imagine what life would be like as the person you would be after. Becoming this new person would mean relearning your place in the world, and paying all the metabolic costs that such relearning would entail. This might be a prospect so unpalatable that you might choose death to maintain your principles.

Duty, then, could be defined as social pressure applied to the self. If that’s right, then what’s tricky is that an external compulsion to conform to norms *feels the same* as an internal compulsion to adhere to a moral duty: they are both a species of social pressure, and they both are experienced as a *sense of should*—i.e., a gut feeling that you *should or shouldn’t* do something. If both norm compliance and duty feel the same—and if both motivate the same behaviors—then arguably, in those circumstances, we *cannot* know which actually motivates us. We gain this insight only whenthese two forces pull apart, making moral tests enlightening. Again, to the Kantian point we began with: moral duty may only be recognizable in others when it conflicts with their self-interested preservation, welfare, or happiness. We add that the same may be true of ourselves: because norm compliance and duty are both species of social pressure, our most fundamental principles may only become salient in the rare circumstances where they are challenged—i.e., when principles come into conflict with social expectations, and we are forced to decide whether those principles are worth defending. So to understand what you truly value, you might ask yourself: what would you walk into the fire for?
