# Nuclear waste heat can turn deserts into farmland

*Climate policy needs a dose of realism*

**By Jan Emblemsvåg** | *18th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,409 words*

![Nuclear waste heat can turn deserts into farmland](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/nuclear-farming-3.webp)

---

*Current climate policies will take almost 200 years to reach the 2050 emissions targets and require almost $100 trillion in investments. By attempting to meet unrealistic targets we’ll leave future generations with failed policy and the debt to go with it. **Jan Emblemsvåg**, Professor in the Department of Ocean Operations and Civil Engineering at Norwegian University of Science and Technology, argues that nuclear energy is the only viable solution to our climate crisis, and that the excess heat nuclear energy generates can create cheap, plentiful water that can be used to convert carbon into agricultural land and forests.*

The world has decarbonized by 10.2% over the last 20 years. Most people believe that this is the result of current climate policies; however, as shown in a [new scientific and peer-reviewed article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2025.2586924), it is not.  Only 5.2% of decarbonization is attributable to current climate policies. The rest comes from cleaner fossil fuels, cleaner fossil power, and the expansion of hydroelectric power. The stagnation of nuclear power during this time, which makes up less of the energy we use than it did 20 years ago, has been a major mistake.

The [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/the-climate-action-monitor-2025.html) also identified a large gap between policies and results, and that the implementation of climate policies by governments is slowing, widening the gap between policy action and climate commitments. In fact, global greenhouse gas emissions rose by 1.1% from 34.75 billion tonnes in 2022 to 35.13 billion tonnes in 2023, leaving the world 13.4% behind the linear progression line towards the Net Zero targets in 2050. Worse, the gap is increasing.

So far, global expenditure on solar and wind power and the associated grid expansion has ramped up to approximately $7.3 trillion, suggesting that each percentage point of improvement costs historically almost $1500 billion. Using simple arithmetic, the meager improvement in greenhouse gas emissions and the large expenditures indicate that current climate policies will take almost 200 years to reach the 2050 targets and require almost $100 trillion in investments without accounting for population growth or a rise in standard of living.

Moreover, the amount of mining and its impact on biodiversity will be staggering. The amount of waste will be almost equally large due to the low degree of recycling and reuse. The only answer the proponents of the current policy paradigm have offered is that humanity will innovate itself out of these challenges. In this case, it is certainly time to start because the current policy path cannot be pursued in the long term from a resource constraint perspective for two reasons.

First, a [thorough bottom-up analysis](https://unece.org/sites/default/files/2023-02/1.%20Simon%20Michaus-Challenges%20and%20Bottlenecks%20for%20the%20Green%20Transition.pdf) of what decarbonization using today’s policies will imply shows that a basic shortfall of materials is expected within a relatively short time. In fact, by 2040, the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/mineral-requirements-for-clean-energy-transitions) forecasts that the current extraction of materials from the Earth must quadruple, with some demand for some at 30-60 times today’s extraction rate. These facts are so dramatic that innovation is basically insufficient—nothing short of revolutions on multiple material segments will do.

To make matters worse, the world is exceedingly poor at recycling these precious materials. The world is generating electronic waste (e-waste) [five times](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/) faster than documented e-waste recycling, and a record 62 million tonnes of e-waste was produced in 2022, up 82% from 2010, and on track to rise another 32%, to 82 million tonnes, in 2030. Thus, not only are we engaging in an unprecedented amount of extraction activity, but we are, at the same time, generating an unprecedented amount of waste due to the low degree of recycling. This cannot go on.

It is time to take a new approach. What if carbon dioxide can be turned into an asset, and not a villain like today?  The fact is that the world is [greening](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/carbon-dioxide-fertilization-greening-earth-study-finds/). NASA reports more green cover today than decades ago, and this is the result of an increase in carbon dioxide in the air. The IPCC also reports on greening in a [special report](https://www.ipcc.ch/srccl/) published in 2022.

Turning carbon dioxide into an asset can only be achieved by using an energy source that produces both heat and electricity, with very low emissions, and that has enough raw material to make it possible. Nuclear is the only energy source that fits the bill since it uses commonly available materials such as concrete and steel. Also, there is enough uranium on the planet to power humanity for at least [four billion years](http://large.stanford.edu/publications/coal/references/docs/pad11983cohen.pdf) with the current resource base, completely dwarfing anything else when it comes to scale and supply over eons.

Should the political mumbo-jumbo around nuclear be solved and [a scientific path actually pursued](https://iai.tv/video/tim-palmer-the-global-climate-future), the results are staggering. According to the article, not only will nuclear require three times less investment, but it will only take half the time (about 90 years). Furthermore, the analysis presented in the article finds that by using the waste heat from nuclear reactors, more than 3 million square kilometers of land can be turned into agricultural land, and another 10 million square kilometers can be turned into forests and other habitats. The increase in agricultural land will increase the total area for producing wheat and rice by almost 80%. This will enable us to handle population growth better, which is a substantial achievement in itself since the world population grows almost at the rate of one Germany per year.

The simple fact is that nuclear reactors generate heat, but only a third of it is utilized in generating power. The waste heat can be used for other purposes, such as desalination. The process is very basic—by heating up seawater, the steam, salt, and other minerals in the seawater separate from one another. Upon condensation, i.e., returning the steam to its state as water, the water has become fresh water. This fresh water can be used to irrigate large swathes of land, as noted above, or can provide drinking water to cities. The process is very well established, as shown by the [International Atomic Energy Agency,](https://www.iaea.org/topics/non-electric-applications/nuclear-desalination) which also offers toolkits for desalination.

The mix of forest and agricultural land that would become available would be dictated by the funds available—irrigating agricultural land is more expensive due to the need for equipment and electric power, while supplying water to natural rivers and dams for afforestation requires far fewer resources.

There are [many different technologies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029549312000519) available to turn seawater into freshwater, and the one chosen is the oldest and simplest, which relies on heat alone. When this simple process is industrialized, enormous quantities of freshwater can be made very fast and cheaply, and this is an aspect of nuclear reactor technologies that is entirely ignored in the debate about climate polices and what technologies to pursue. With enough water, there are many problems that can be solved or alleviated around the world. Growing more wheat and rice is just one option. In temperate countries, it might be used for drinking water or industrial processes instead. Importantly, with [an increasingly volatile climate](https://iai.tv/articles/we-still-dont-understand-climate-change-auid-2843), it is difficult to know exactly how best to use water, but it is obviously the case that, in order to use water, we must first have water available, and nuclear power can solve this.

It is therefore time to realize that the efficacy of the current climate policies is largely overrated and will not stand the test of time. Nuclear can help us turn carbon emissions into food, and in the process produce all the power we need twice as fast at one-third of the cost. No other clean energy source can do this, and it would be a crime of ignorance towards future generations if we followed the current policy path without seriously amending it.

We need to start the energy transition rather than keep on adding to our energy supply, but the Net Zero 2050 target should be abandoned because it makes no sense. Impossible targets drive bad policy options at the expense of sensible policy options because impossible options divert resources and time. We are currently leaving [future generations](https://iai.tv/articles/its-time-to-give-up-the-phrase-climate-change-auid-2903) not only a failed policy but also the debt to go with it. As a Chinese proverb goes: “Those in a hurry do not arrive.”
