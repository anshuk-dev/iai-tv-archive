# Navigating reality in the misinformation age

*Postmodernism has been misused by authoritarians*

**By Lee McIntyre** | *30th May 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,769 words*

*Philosophy has a long tradition of rejecting those who claim to know the objective truth. But in an age of disinformation, philosopher Lee McIntyre argues that understanding truth and reality is more critical than ever. In this interview, with Omari Edwards contributing editor of IAI News, they delve into the complex nature of truth, the misuse of doubt, and the vital role philosophers must play in public discourse to combat propaganda and promote clarity.*

“If everybody always lies to you, the consequence is not that you believe the lies, but rather that nobody believes anything any longer.” – Hannah Arendt

In a world where the boundaries between truth and falsehood blur under the weight of disinformation, the quest for reality has never been more critical — or more contentious. Philosophers, however, have been caught with their pants down. Whilst many argued vehemently against the postmodernists and tried to save truth from the critiques of perspectivalism and bias, our tradition was unable to show that objective truth wasn’t a fantasy. As Lee McIntyre puts it, “We have left our weapons on the battlefield, we should not be surprised our enemies have now turned them on us”. In my interview with Lee on his latest award-winning books, [Post Truth](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262535045/post-truth/) [and On Disinformation](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262546300/on-disinformation/), we debate the role of truth, philosophy and the public sphere.

**The most important philosophical question I want to ask you is the one philosophers have struggled with since Socrates: what is truth and how do we know when we have it?**

Starting off with an easy one. I think that the lay public and even many philosophers often get mixed up between questions of [ontology](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ontology-metaphysics) and [epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology/). Is there a way that the world is vs can we know how the world is. There are lots of interesting philosophical detours on the question of whether things really exist when we aren’t looking at them. We could have a lot of fun questioning the merits of [Berkelian idealism](http://articles/the-universe-is-made-of-experiences-not-things-auid-2844), and challenging the commonly held assumption that there is a way that the world just is. But the question which matters, and the one I try and address in my work, is showing how we can know it. Especially in a time when truth is being [attacked in bad faith](https://medium.com/@visionarydoc/the-truth-is-under-attack-why-critical-thinking-matters-more-than-ever-before-a833a895b256). That is not by other philosophers, or other humanists or critical theorists or whoever they might be, but rather those pursuing it for some political means. In that sort of era, I am a very strong defender of the notion of truth. I’ve not abandoned all the lessons I learnt in my epistemology seminars but we are disserved if we abandon the search for truth.

**In *Post Truth*, you put a lot of the blame for the current political conversation currently being had on the shoulders of Critical Schools, English Literature departments and postmodern tradition. But the lack of certainty in the sciences and the notion of objective truth also comes from empiricism and pragmatist traditions, that we will always be left with the problems of language and mind which cannot be overcome?**

It's such a great question and you put it so well, because I'm a philosopher and I love to have the debates that you're talking about, even with postmodernists who may not like me for the things that I said in that book. Because, look, I think that for the most part, their heart was in the right place. They're pointing out that objective knowledge is hard to have and sometimes people are biased, and sometimes people really are making claims that are not an assertion of truth but an assertion of their political power. Those are really important things for scientists and others to reflect on. Any knowledge seeker, whether it's empirical knowledge or not, is well served by the idea that they could be wrong. That is the thing that they think is true and knowable. But it may not be. Questioning authority was the whole point of Socrates. The first lesson philosophy should teach us is to avoid hubris.

**But given the role of more mainstream academic philosophy in a critique of objective truth, can we really go back to using truth without contradiction? Should we not acknowledge that the postmodernists were right to some extent?**

The problem is that kind of unravelling that happens over the next millennia of philosophy that starts with ‘we could be wrong,’ to ‘all we know is red patch now,’ and ends up giving aid and comfort to the fascists, the autocrats, and the authoritarians who want to claim that everything is narrative, and there is no truth. Because if there's no truth then there's no blame and there's no accountability for their actions. That is a very different thing than Foucault, Derrida, or even Latour.

Let’s focus on Latour for a second, he said all of the nasty things that he said about science during [the science wars](https://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/harrycollins/the-science-wars/), and you know, a lot of scientists and more than half of philosophers hated him for it. But then, even [he later said](https://www.science.org/content/article/bruno-latour-veteran-science-wars-has-new-mission), “Oh, my God, what have I done? I've given aid and comfort to the global warming design deniers.” You know, why does it now burn my tongue? And I think the reason it burned his tongue is because he did not intend to create a weapon that was left on the battlefield that will be picked up by the enemies of truth. Because as critical as all of the post-modernists and critical theorists are, I don't think they ever meant for the world to implode around the idea that anything goes, nothing matters, and it's all just completely irrelevant.

I think that serious damage was done by bad faith. Poorly intentioned people picked up the claim for post-modernism from the battlefield and said, 'Oh, my God, look at this tool.' We can use this to incinerate any empirical statement that we don't like and use it as part of a strategic denialist campaign to say that everybody is a liar. Because of this, some people tend to resist the subtlety of what Trump is doing, for example. Trump's strategy is not simply to say, 'Oh, that's false.' And I'm telling you the truth is to say that there is no truth and everybody's a liar. Everybody's biased, and if everybody's biased what do you do? You go to your corner, with people that you'd like to believe are telling the truth, or your tribe and ideology. That's when you get someone like Trump, just a stone cold idiot. But he is a master of this technique of misdirection, and propaganda. Which says that sometimes the most effective thing to say is 'Who really knows what the truth is'? Who checks the fact-checkers? How do we know that that's the case?

Tabacco companies did it for years. The tobacco companies in the 50's claimed that there wasn't a definitive study which proved that cigarette smoking caused lung cancer. They were right. They were right because, no, there's never been a definitive study that has ever proven that anything follows from anything because of David Hume. Now, were they [radical empiricists](https://www.britannica.com/topic/radical-empiricism) who followed David Hume to the edge of the earth and then looked over? No, they were greedy bastards who wanted to make money and exploit the idea of doubt for their own purposes. That's what I'm fighting against.

**Finally, what should we be doing now? Philosophers have left the weapon of doubt on the battlefield, how do we fight back? Is it our responsibility to fight disinformation?**

I think that philosophy is strengthened when we engage with the important issues of our time. And when we engage in a dialogue with people who might care about it, if they could cut through the jargon and understand the sorts of distinctions that we're making. What do the philosophers mean? We now  live in a time in which serious conversations are had on CNN and MSNBC and in New York Times about truth, facts, knowledge and reality. And I almost never see a philosopher quoted. I see lawyers, priests, and pundits but not philosophers having a voice. How did we get to this point where we became experts on these topics, and our moment arrived, and we weren't ready? That's why I do what I do. That's why I do public philosophy, because I think that philosophers have a responsibility, whether they created the problem or not, to elucidate the public. So that they know what these distinctions are.

I'll give you a dumb example. It's maybe not so dumb, but it drives me insane. You remember when Muller, Robert Muller, that careful, careful person gave his report and said, if we could have exonerated Trump, we would have. There's an implication there that anybody who takes deductive logic understands the [contrapositive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/contrapositive), and understands that what he was saying is that we could not exonerate Trump, because we had evidence that he was guilty. But then Trump was out there, he says, totally exonerated and a bazillion people believe him because they don't understand that distinction. But what if there had been a philosopher on MSNBC to explain that? What might have changed?

I don't agree with the idea that public philosophy is just a popularisation or that it can't be rigorous, because it can't just end it nor can it just slide into activism, where, you know, we're not even asking the hard questions of ourselves. Public philosophy is, you know, kind of the inheritance of what Socrates was doing, and I think that's terrific. But when you asked me about our responsibilities, yes, I think we have some, and I think we're not meeting them. Because philosophers, by and large, don't have a public voice. And it's easy not to if you don't try, you know. Every now and then a philosopher is moved to try to send something off in the New York Times. And it gets rejected. Why? Because a thousand others may have a platform, and they never understood that in order to have a voice in the public, you have to build a platform and try hundres of other things before one of them works. There are terrific other philosophers out there like Jason Stanley and he is wonderful because he is an acclaimed academic and Epistemologist, but he has a public voice and has led us to understand the dangers of propaganda and fascism. That's the kind of work I really love.
