# A New Existentialism for the Anthropocene

*How the Climate Crisis has changed being*

**By Nikolaj Schultz** | *12th June 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,064 words*

![A New Existentialism for the Anthropocene](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/a-new-existentialism-for-the-antropocene.webp)

In the quest for new theoretic answers in response to the climate crisis, Nikolaj Schultz proposes a new existentialism in for the Anthropocene age. In this in-depth interview, on his latest book [Land Sickness](https://www.wiley.com/en-ae/Land+Sickness-p-9781509556137), Schultz pushes back against the notion of the “individual” from the Lockean liberal tradition and shows that this idea of the individual as autonomous, indivisible, and impenetrable is but a bad description. The individual is always a relational being and this is all the more true in the age of a climate crisis.

**Why did you write the** [**book**](https://www.wiley.com/en-ae/Land+Sickness-p-9781509556137)**? And why did you choose this auto-fictive/'ethnografictive' style?**

It’s true, the book is written in a quite weird genre, far from how I normally write books, between the personal and the theoretical. The reason was that I wanted to capture the affects, the experience, the emotions of living on a climate damaged planet – and for this, I needed another genre, and a more subjective or personal style. As you indicate, I call it an “auto-etnografictive essay”, understood as an essay based on own observations, thoughts, and experiences over a few days, that I then reconstructed on a narrative level. Now, if you read it as a novel, it is awful, and if you read it as a theory book, it is probably not great as well – but if you accept that it’s a sort of hybrid genre in between, then it might work. This said, I do consider it more book of theory than of literature: I would say that it is probably more of a “literary existentialism” than it is a “existential literature”.

**Was there some way in which you thought this first-person encounter is more personal and thus a more intimate, humanization of the climate crisis?**

Exactly: By starting from my own experiences, I believe it allowed me to unlock some of the more intimate, sensitive, and existential aspects of the climate crisis, that I believe have been missing. We have a lot of hard facts and data on these questions, and a lot of very important analyses of the situation “from a distance”. And these are obviously crucial terms to tackle the situation, but we also need investigations into what it means and how it feels to be living in a time of global climate change, and to be human on a planet that one’s own species is destroying in habitability day by day. This is what I am trying to find a narrative and conceptual language to describe and understand. We have a lot of such books on the topics of class, gender, sexuality etc., but for some reason not really on the topics of ecology and the climate, and I believe this is a problem. Why? Well, because as many scholars have noted, then the climate crisis is not only a political or scientific crisis – it is also, and perhaps first and foremost, a crisis of sensibilities. And what I try to render sensible with the book is the existential transformation of the human being in a time of the [Anthropocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene).

**How does the concept of 'Landsickness' as portrayed in your book reflect the broader existential and philosophical themes explored?**

The concept of land sickness is one I stole from nautical language, and I use it to describe this “double shaking”, this simultaneous trembling of the Earth and the Human being. [Bruno Latour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Latour) would have called it a “geopathical” term, a term that describes a suffering related to the “geo”, that is, to the Earth. To be more specific, I use it as a sort of umbrella term for some of all the troubling, frightening and sometimes painful affects that climate change induces in us and that I try to analyse and depict in the book – including the fear, the grief, the anguish, anxiety and anger that comes from seeing the Earth disappear beneath your feet, as well the nausea, guilt, confusion and loss of horizons that arises when you realize that it is disappearing due to your own being’s freedom and actions. The book is generally about how the moral-existential landscape of the human being fragments in a time of global climate change, when realizing every step you take is contributing a little to the decaying of the Earth, and Land Sickness is a term I use to describe some of the affects that follows from it.

**Your book explores the existential weight of the climate crisis. Indeed, in your book you note, 'I mix and interfere, therefore I am and continue to be.' How does the existential weight of the climate crisis serve as a crucible for philosophical inquiry into the nature of the self, challenging us to confront the illusion of separateness and cultivate a more holistic conception of identity?**

This is a good question. I would approach it a bit differently. As I said above, I believe the human being and its existential conditions simply are being transformed in a time of global climate change. The Earth is mutating, and the human figure is mutating as well. Why? Because the human is the very case of this violent, planetary transformation. This is the whole point of the concept of the Anthropocene. And indeed, yes, this forces us to reconsider the human figure, its ontology, and – again – its existential conditions. Today, the human being has simply transformed into another being than we thought it was, another existence, another species: An existence that is dragging behind it a set of destructive traces that are threatening the planets conditions of habitability, and thus its own survival as a species. I was just in Vienna where I was reminded [Egon Schiele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egon_Schiele)’s motto: “My being, my … decaying”. But this was supposed to be on an individual level, not on the level of a species! And that is certainly something the old existentialists would not have imagined – and it’s a drama that I believe will be weighing more and more heavily on our life terrains and moral landscapes in the years to come.

**Do you think the climate crisis leads to the death of individualist understandings of being? Or is there a way in which this might prompt an attempt to re-evaluate the ethical foundations of individualism, inviting a deeper exploration of the relationship dimensions of moral agency and responsibility?**

That, on the other hand, is a difficult and nuanced question, because its true that my book goes back to the individual, but of course it’s a completely different “individual figure” than in the Lockean liberal tradition, which the book is one big critique of ; the whole point of the book is going back to this figure, and then through a narrative and a conceptual discussion show it as dissolving. As [Dipesh Chakrabarty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipesh_Chakrabarty) describes in the postface to the book, then a main point of the book is about showing how this idea of the individual as autonomous, indivisible, and impenetrable is but a bad description – and how the ontology of human is relational, how the human always is penetrated by and penetrates itself a multitude of other beings that it both depends on and destroys at the same time.  And perhaps, as Chakrabarty notes, such redescriptions might in fact help “recovering the self” in the Anthropocene age.

I have never understood that distinction or the having to chose between acting individually or acting collectively. Obviously, they go hand in hand, and we must act both alone and together.

\_\_\_

Under any circumstances, based on such a re-account of the human, it does pose the question of individual responsibility in the Anthropocene – and I must admit that some of the critique I have received for doing so, instead of solely focusing on “collective action and “structural change”, have been surprising, to say the least. Surprising on the one hand, because I wrote a book with Bruno Latour on the need of collective action and making emerge a strong ecological class that can fight for the habitability of the planet, and because *Land Sickness* takes up again these questions of “geo-social class struggles”. Surprising on the other hand, because I have never understood that distinction or the having to chose between acting individually or acting collectively. Obviously, they go hand in hand, and we must act both alone and together. To say “We can’t direct attention to individual actions and responsibilities because ecology is a structural problem” is a bit like saying that because racism is structural, then it doesn’t matter if individually keep on using the n-word or other racial slurs. It makes no sense. Again, we must fight on all fronts, and as my friend [Katrine Høghøj](https://www.techne.ac.uk/our-students/techne-students-list/2022/katrine-hoeghoej/) recently reminded me, then we can still learn from the surrealists, who knew that [Rimbaud’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rimbaud) call to change your life and Marx’s to change the world go hand in hand.

**In the book you state 'I barely recognize myself. The image of the human species seems to have been transformed. ... I am here, there and everywhere.' Do you think the climate crisis necessitates a shift in our philosophical focus? Do we need a new philosophical paradigm to  respond to the mutability and flexibility of our condition in light of the climate crisis?**

I believe it does, and this is what I advocate for what I call a new existentialism, understood as a novel tradition of thought that – in the wake of the Anthropocene – poses the questions of being and existence differently. How so? To simplify things, then we can say that the old existentialism of the 19th and 20th century focused on the meaning, purpose, or conditions of human existence, whereas the new existentialism focus more on the questions of what beings that allows or disallows other beings to continue existing, at a moment in history where the human being is destroying the other life forms that conditions its own existence. One important set of contributions has here been the non-human turn, following not least Bruno Latour’s 40-year-old attempt to include non-humans into social theory.

\_\_\_

We also need portrayals and descriptions that brings us closer to understanding how the shape of the human figure itself has changed shape, how it is undergoing a existential metamorphosis, as it is transforming into a being that is destroying its own species conditions of subsistence

\_\_\_

 Think here about e.g. [Baptiste’s Morizot](https://www.luma.org/en/live/people/Baptiste-Morizot-d62668f7-24e0-4e9f-b6e6-a2a681be5202.html?lang=en), [Vinciane Despret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinciane_Despret), [Emanuele Coccia](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/program/festival-evening/moviment/emanuele-coccia), [Anna Tsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Tsing) to mention a few, and their crucial, brilliant works that focus on making us sensible to other life forms than humans, by describing the plurality of living beings that humans co-exist with and depend on. Yet, like the old existentialist tradition, a new existentialism will necessarily be a movement of thought with many different branches and divisions, and here the grain of salt that I propose to add is that we also need portrayals and descriptions that brings us closer to understanding how the shape of the human figure itself has changed shape, how it is undergoing a existential metamorphosis, as it is transforming into a being that is destroying its own species conditions of subsistence. Facing our new terrestrial condition, not only demands that we become sensible to other living beings – it also involves a sensibility to our own human figure having transformed into another sort of existence.

Again: What are the affects of this human metamorphosis? How does the psycho-emotional landscape of the human fragment in a time of global ecological mutations? What are the moral and existential dead-ends or dilemmas that the human being face, as it tries to navigate in a climate damaged world? Or simply: What does it mean to be human, when the habitability conditions of the Earth are disappearing, due to your own being? These are the questions that I am interested in, which of course also means that – despite what I said above – then it is not simply that the existential question today changes from the question of meaning or conditions of the human existence, to the question of what beings allows or disallows other beings to exist – instead, a new existentialism should be posing these two questions in the same breathe, precisely because they today go hand in hand. And what’s what I tried to do tentatively in *Land Sickness*.
