# Wittgenstein, Heidegger, and the language of silence

*What happens when silence speaks*

**By Steven Bindeman** | *11th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,158 words*

![Wittgenstein, Heidegger, and the language of silence](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/CTT-wittgenstein-2.webp)

*We think we communicate and understand most deeply using language. But how can we say what words cannot express? Silence. Drawing on Wittgenstein and Heidegger, philosopher Steven Bindeman argues that language often keeps us separated from the world and is fundamentally incapable of describing it. Only silence can bridge the gap between the self and world.*

If it is possible to mean more than we can say, then we will need to make use of silence. We can all recognize times when we were unable to articulate something important. Such moments place us in a situation of uncanniness, facing the disconnect between ourselves and the world around us. The common reaction to this disorienting experience is to fill it up with words, since it’s not the sort of thing we want to endure for long. When we bring silence to language, however, both the nature of language and the constructed world it encompasses undergo significant changes. As Kierkegaard once wrote, “Bring silence to people who listen and watch them take themselves to heart.”

It’s no accident that the two most renowned philosophers of the 20th century, Heidegger and Wittgenstein, placed the phenomenon of silence at the center of their philosophies. When Heidegger designated nihilism as the inner logic of Western history, he was pointing to the disconnect between language and world; when Wittgenstein insisted at the end of his *[Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/#TracLogiPhil)* that all discussions about ethics, values, and the meaning of life are meaningless because they have no clear referents outside language, he too was pointing to the same disconnect between word and thing.

\_\_\_

As Kierkegaard once wrote, “Bring silence to people who listen and watch them take themselves to heart.”.

\_\_\_

Like Heidegger, Wittgenstein thought deeply about the relationship between language and world, or words and things. Also like Heidegger, [his ideas on this subject underwent drastic changes](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-vs-wittgenstein-lee-braver-auid-2615) as he distanced himself from his original position. In his early work, the *Tractatus*, written in the trenches in the First World War, he sincerely believed that he had solved all of the significant problems that existed in the world of philosophy. In his preface to that work, he insisted that “the reason why these problems are posed is that the logic of our language is misunderstood.” He went on to say that “the whole sense of the work can be summed up in the following words: what can be said at all can be said clearly and what we cannot talk about we must pass over in silence.”

This did not mean that these issues were of no interest to him, however. It merely indicated that a diﬀerent way of using language, perhaps under the ordinance of silence, must be invented. Most importantly, the essential dignity of “the unsayable” must be respected. Wittgenstein’s own thinking here can be extended by referring to what he wrote to his friend Paul Engelmann concerning a poem by the German poet Ludwig Uhland: “And this is how it is: if only you do not try to utter what is unutterable then nothing gets lost. But the unutterable will be—unutterably—contained in what has been uttered.”

Wittgenstein vs Wittgenstein
By Lee Braver](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-vs-wittgenstein-lee-braver-auid-2615)**

In his *Tractatus* Wittgenstein developed the idea, which later had an enormous influence on the logical positivists, that a formal theory of language could be developed, capable of showing how propositions can successfully represent real states of aﬀairs. Language, he said, is like a picture that is laid against reality like a measuring rod and reaches right out to it. But Wittgenstein soon saw that relating propositions in the form of language to facts concerning the world could not in itself be proved. Without proof, his house of cards collapses. Although propositions are indeed capable of modeling and describing the world with a rigor not unlike that of mathematical representations of physical phenomena, they cannot describe how they describe this world without becoming self-referential. Propositions are consequently recognized essentially as [meaningless](https://iai.tv/video/wittgenstein-s-collection-of-nonsense?ts=1606792711), since their meaning consisted only in their ability to connect with the world outside of language.

\_\_\_

We learn here how to let language itself speak through us.

\_\_\_

With his new ideas about language, put forth in his [*Philosophical Investigations*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/#PhilInve), Wittgenstein now saw language as a dynamic system, made up of what he called “language games.” Wittgenstein's main concern in this later work was with the misunderstandings that arise in our use of language. “Language is a labyrinth of paths,” he wrote. “You approach from one side and know your way about; you approach the same place from another side and no longer know your way about.” “A philosophical problem has the form: I don't know my way about.” We turn to philosophy, Wittgenstein believed, when we’re confused, when we don’t understand something. This happens when we bump our heads against the linguistic knots we encounter in our daily lives when the meaning of something isn’t clear to us.

In order to explore the meaning of things, Wittgenstein had initially insisted in the *Tractatus* that we must proceed logically. Yet in certain situations, logic may not be the most exact language we have. In the *Investigations* he therefore explored the intricacies of language usage from a diﬀerent perspective. He realized that he had in his *Tractatus* misunderstood the role of the ideal in language, recognizing now that a mere word can never denote a real thing. “We feel as if we had to repair a torn spider web with our fingers.” With this web, he was referring to the connection between language and reality. The tear means that “we must do away with all explanation, and description alone must take its place.” We thus pass from logical explanation in the *Tractatus* to phenomenological description in the *Investigations*.

\_\_\_

Heidegger soon learned to “let go” of his dependency on clarity.

\_\_\_

Heidegger, in turn, like Wittgenstein, also wrote about [how and when the limits of language are reached](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-and-the-paradoxes-at-the-limits-of-language-auid-3146). He spoke of his concern that Dasein was in danger of evaporating into the world of trivial daily work activity and thus losing sight of its “real” self altogether. (Heidegger used the term Dasein, which literally means “Being-there,” to refer in a non-objective way to the situated nature of human experience.) Heidegger realized that if we want to determine the essence of ourselves, we will need to approach our subject from a neutral point of view—and this will entail avoiding our historical attraction to and dependency on abstract reasoning. In short, he wanted to approach Dasein as a mystery, as something not already pre-determined. He wanted to move past our uncritical acceptance of Aristotle’s definition of man as the animal who thinks. While Heidegger acknowledged that the articulation of who one is can take place only in language, he also recognized that he needed to step outside the traditional metaphysical form of subject-object dualism if he wanted to discover the authentic nature of Dasein.

Heidegger's route to reality
By Simon Glendinning](https://iai.tv/articles/escaping-from-distraction-simon-glendinning-auid-2743)**

Heidegger soon learned to “let go” of his dependency on clarity. He referred to this process as “Gelassenheit,” and it enabled him to disconnect from his dependency on ordinary logic as the appropriate vehicle for authentic discourse. Not only did Gelassenheit become a central theme in the second half of his career—it became a necessary state in the process of self-transformation for Dasein, and it also became the vehicle for discovering authenticity through the gift of the poetic life.

Heidegger now believed that “authentic exploration” into the “true” nature of Dasein could only take place in dialogue with great works of art. For this [authentic exploration](https://iai.tv/articles/escaping-from-distraction-simon-glendinning-auid-2743) ever to happen, however, several transformations in the nature of self would be required: the experience of time passing, the experience of others speaking, and the experience of one's inner voice. In *[Being and Time](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/#BeinTime)*, Heidegger had written about the transformation of one’s inner voice in terms of his discussion concerning the development of one’s conscience. This, in turn, involved transformations in time-consciousness and hearing. His ideas about hearing and keeping silent occur in the context of his discussion of the possibility of his completing the analytic of Dasein.

\_\_\_

An awareness of one’s own impending death leads one specifically to the question of what it means to be alive.

\_\_\_

Both hearing and keeping silent require a full understanding of the nature of Dasein: “Dasein hears, because it understands.” To be able to keep silent, Dasein must have something to say—that is, it must have at its disposal an authentic and rich sense of itself. This authentic form of discourse requires a breaking down of ordinary time in order to take place: “Its time is a diﬀerent time, an essentially slower time than that of idle talk, which ‘lives at a faster rate.’” Speaking in this context must somehow involve a deeper kind of listening, not merely to one’s own voice or even to the person to whom one is speaking. We learn here how to let language itself speak through us. That this takes place within a dialogue between thinkers and poets is Heidegger’s main thesis in his later works. In *Being and Time*, however, he believed that authentic discourse belonged to those who had confronted death.

The role of keeping silent in the overall analytic of Dasein becomes more concrete when it is seen in its connection to what Heidegger calls conscience. Dasein’s understanding is related to its state-of-mind, or mood. In some moods, Dasein is very open to the opinions of others. In these situations, it is threatened with the possibility of loss of self: “Losing itself in the publicness and the idle talk of the ‘they,’ it fails to hear its own Self in listening to the they-self.’” Another kind of hearing is necessary to break away from this influence. The call which returns Dasein to itself is the conscience. This conscience has a voice that Dasein is capable of hearing; yet it is not a call that can be put into words: “Conscience discourses solely and constantly in the mode of keeping silent. In this way it not only loses none of its perceptibility, but forces the Dasein which has been appealed to and summoned, into the reticence of itself.” The content of this call is indefinite, but its direction, toward a self-aware reticence, is certain:

Only in keeping silent does the conscience call; that is to say, the call comes from the soundlessness of uncanniness, and the Dasein which it summons is called back into the stillness of itself, and called back as something that is to become still. Only in reticence, therefore, is this silent discourse understood appropriately in wanting to have a conscience. It takes the words away from the common-sense idle talk of the they.

In *Being and Time*, Heidegger related his concept of conscience to thinking about death: “Authentic thinking about death is a ‘wanting-to-have-a-conscience.’” This thinking about death places us in a state of mind of anxiety, which in turn presents us authentically with ourselves. The idle talk of our they-self leads us to treat death as an event to be feared. But anxiety is not a fear of death, for this fear is an inauthentic mood. To dread death is to be uncannily aware of one’s own eventual dying. “Anticipatory resoluteness” is Heidegger’s term for Dasein’s authentic way of treating death as a meaningful possibility, in which an awareness of one’s own impending death leads one specifically to the question of what it means to be alive. Within the mood of anxiety, we do not take existence lightly, as something to be wasted; we treat life as a gift, as something precious to be savored at every moment, whether high or low. Anxiety, then, is the mood that is attuned to the possibility of death. Conscience calls Dasein back to this mood, and thus back to the stillness of itself. This stillness connotes Dasein’s freedom, as a form of life which is in harmony with its own nature.

We think we can’t know silence because we can’t describe what it is. But we *can* describe the eﬀects of silence. Indeed, silence has meaning because it aﬀects our lives. Even if silence is another language, it cannot speak to us directly, for it contains nothing in itself but only reflects what surrounds it. Silence, then, is an example of indirect discourse, analogous to Wittgenstein’s “showing” or Heidegger's “poetic dwelling.” Silence can thus be experienced in the process of breakdown, as a disruption in the connection of self and world, and it can also be felt as part of the healing experience which takes place when self and world are brought back together. Both experiences exist beyond words.
