# Overcoming hostile architecture

*The buildings designed to hurt you*

**By Karl Mendelson** | *21st February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,668 words*

![Overcoming hostile architecture](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/hostile-architecture-thumbnail2.webp)

---

*The way a building is designed is not a value-free choice. Architecture is not neutral. All buildings have a philosophy that underpins them. Most cities in the world today include 'hostile architecture' - buildings designed to exclude certain behaviours and marginalised people, such as spikes outside shops to prevent rough sleeping. Rather than fostering freedom and community, modern buildings prioritise control and surveillance. Karl Mendelson here argues urban design should be for all individuals and promote participation in society, not simply serve the interests of property owners or the powerful.*

Walk through any major city, and you’ll see them: benches with armrests dividing every seat, spikes lining storefronts, sloped ledges that look like places to rest—until you try. Architecture has long shaped the way we experience the world around us. Public spaces throughout our recent history served as venues for meetings, leisure, and rest. Yet, in recent years, the favoured fashions of urban designers have sought to limit, rather than encourage, the uses of these spaces.

This is where hostile architecture comes in: a design philosophy that explicitly seeks to restrict certain behaviours, from sleeping to skateboarding, through uncomfortable or impractical features. While this might seem like a pragmatic way to manage public space, it hides a deeper ethical issue. Hostile architecture isn’t just bad design; it’s a philosophical approach that values control and exclusion over community and inclusivity. When we design spaces to prevent certain people from using them, what message does that send about who belongs in the city?

This shift in design philosophy represents a broader political change, one that has moved from openness and engagement to control and exclusion. Philosophers like Hannah Arendt, Michel Foucault, and David Harvey provide us with a lens through which to critically examine this shift and the deeper ethical implications of hostile architecture. Public spaces should be for everyone, but hostile architecture rewrites that rule. As we examine the consequences of these design choices, we need to ask: What kind of cities do we want to live in? Are we shaping them for control or for the community?

\_\_\_

These designs are not neutral; they are choices about who belongs in the city and who is not.

\_\_\_

One of the most troubling aspects of defensive design – the more acceptable face of hostile architecture - is its lack of formal acknowledgement by the very individuals and firms who implement it. Many architects, urban planners, and property developers who incorporate hostile or exclusionary design elements into public spaces rarely, if ever, make a clear, public commitment to these practices. Instead, these decisions are often framed in terms of efficiency, order, or security—virtues that, on the surface, seem entirely neutral and uncontroversial. The use of hostile features is rarely justified as a moral choice; rather, they are typically rationalised as necessary for managing behaviour and maintaining public safety. This lack of transparent intention surrounding defensive design allows it to be implemented without the scrutiny it deserves, leaving little room for public debate about the ethical ramifications of such choices.

It obscures the fact that these designs are not neutral; they are choices about who belongs in the city and who is not. Their justifications sidestep the reality that such measures disproportionately affect marginalised populations, particularly the unhoused. Furthermore, by not engaging with the ethical considerations of their designs, architects and urban planners avoid the accountability necessary for making decisions that serve the common good rather than the interests of a specific group. They have forgotten that all of our choices about our society are ethical ones. Ones which would benefit from a better philosophical analysis.

So, at the heart of defensive design lies a deeper issue: a failure to incorporate philosophy into the process of urban design. When architects, planners, and developers prioritise order, security, and efficiency without considering their broader ethical implications, they essentially abdicate the responsibility of interrogating the human cost of their work. Philosophy, particularly political philosophy and ethics, offers a crucial lens through which we can challenge the dominant urban design paradigms that underpin hostile architecture. Without this critical perspective, we risk losing sight of the fundamental question: What kind of world are we building, and for whom?

\_\_\_

The way spaces are designed—what we make accessible, what we make uncomfortable, and who we decide to include or exclude—can either empower or marginalise.

\_\_\_

Philosophers like Michel Foucault have long warned about the insidious ways that power operates in seemingly neutral spaces. In his seminal work *Discipline and Punish*, Foucault argued that power is not just enforced through laws or force but is deeply embedded in the built environment. "Space is fundamental in any exercise of power."

The way spaces are designed—what we make accessible, what we make uncomfortable, and who we decide to include or exclude—can either empower or marginalise. In the case of hostile architecture, this logic of control over human behaviour often results in public spaces that prioritise compliance over belonging. Spaces become battlegrounds of power, where the rights of the individual to simply exist are compromised in favour of maintaining a sanitised, controlled public order. In Foucault's terms, the city becomes a mechanism of surveillance, shaping not just physical movement but also the social interactions that take place within it.

At its core, this is a question of ethics, a revealing case study of our values. So, we must ask whether these design choices truly reflect the common good or the values we wish to promote as a society. Hannah Arendt, with her work on public space and human dignity, reminds us that democracy thrives when spaces are designed to foster interaction, difference, and collective engagement. Public spaces, in her view, are not just sites for utility—they are foundational to the political life of a community.

But what happens when those spaces are designed to push certain people away? When a park bench is deliberately made impossible to lie down on, it stops being a public good and becomes a tool of forced displacement. Who is architecture really for? When spaces are designed with the intent to exclude, they undermine the social contract that allows people to live together in a shared, diverse society. To reclaim the public realm, we must ground our designs in egalitarian principles and recognise that true freedom comes not from exclusion but from inclusion.

\_\_\_

Urban spaces should be sites where democratic ideals are tested and lived, not places where the most vulnerable are invisibly pushed aside.

\_\_\_

The lack of philosophical reflection in design allows dangerous assumptions to persist—that some people are more deserving of space than others, and that the comfort of a select few should take precedence over the rights of the marginalised. To truly challenge the entrenched thinking that fuels hostile architecture, we must inject philosophical rigour into the design process. Urban spaces should be sites where democratic ideals are tested and lived, not places where the most vulnerable are invisibly pushed aside. In this sense, architecture must be more than a technical discipline—it must be an ethical one. It must be a discipline capable of asking the difficult questions: What do our spaces say about us? How do our built environments shape not just behaviour but also identity and belonging? If architecture shapes the world we inhabit, then it is high time we ensure that it serves not just the privileged few, but the public good in all its messy, diverse glory.

Urban design should empower, not exclude. In *Rebel Cities*, David Harvey reminds us that "The right to the city is not merely a right of access to what already exists, but a right to change it." That means rejecting an architecture of control and embracing one of community. Instead of hostile design, we need participatory, human-centred spaces. Spaces that don’t just serve the interests of property owners but reflect the needs of all who live there.

Defenders of these designs insist they’re necessary. They argue that limiting how public space is used prevents crime, protects property values, and ensures that cities remain orderly and functional. A bus stop that’s impossible to sleep on, they say, isn’t an attack on unhoused people—it’s a way to keep transit infrastructure clean and accessible. A bench divided by armrests isn’t about exclusion—it’s about making sure seating is used as intended. These arguments dress up exclusion as efficiency, but they miss the bigger point: a city built to keep people from resting isn’t a city built for people at all.

\_\_\_

If a park bench shouldn’t be used for sleeping, should a sidewalk be designed to prevent loitering?

\_\_\_

And where does this logic end? If a park bench shouldn’t be used for sleeping, should a sidewalk be designed to prevent loitering? If a plaza shouldn’t allow skateboarding, should it also discourage gathering? The more we accept hostile design as normal, the more we allow the city to be shaped not around people’s needs but around a narrow vision of order that serves property owners above all else. As great theorist of urban design Jane Jacobs argued, great cities aren’t controlled from the top down—they emerge from the life that happens within them. When we overregulate and overdesign public space, we don’t just make it less accessible. We make it less alive.

There are alternatives. Cities like Vienna and Amsterdam have experimented with urban design that invites rather than excludes integrating all citizens into public life. These alternatives offer an inspiring vision for urban spaces. Rather than limiting access or creating discomfort, these cities embrace flexible, adaptable designs that cater to a broad range of users and uses. Public seating in Vienna, for example, is designed to accommodate both brief stops and longer rest periods, with benches that allow people to sit or lie comfortably. Amsterdam has integrated skateboard-friendly areas that enhance urban life without sacrificing safety or accessibility. These aren’t impossible changes. Cities could invest in public spaces designed for flexibility rather than control, recognising that a truly public space is one that serves everyone, not just those who fit a narrow definition of acceptable behaviour. Cities where philosophers and ethics get involved in their design might just be better off.
