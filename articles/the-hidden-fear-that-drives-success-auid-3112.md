# The hidden fear that drives success

*Psychoanalysis, conscientiousness, and the death drive*

**By Jeff Greenberg** | *17th March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,303 words*

![The hidden fear that drives success](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-hidden-fear-that-drives-success3.webp)

---

*We all want to succeed. But it is clear that some have more drive than others. What makes an individual more careful, hard-working and responsible than other people — known as trait conscientiousness — at a fundamental level, largely remains a mystery. There is no one brain region we can point to, and two people brought up in the same environment can possess different levels of the trait. Social and existential psychologist, Jeff Greenberg, outlines the origins of conscientiousness, arguing psychoanalysis and fear of death may be key to explaining this mystery.*

If you think about your friends, some of them are probably very careful, responsible, and hard-working. Others may not be. These characteristics comprise a trait known as “conscientiousness,” one of the five basic dimensions of personality. And research confirms that its prominence varies considerably from person to person. Studies also show that conscientiousness helps people achieve their goals, [strongly correlates with academic grades](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10381607/#:~:text=Conscientiousness%20has%20a%20positive%20effect,for%20performance%20(Meyer%20et%20al.), [career success](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0092656619300753#:~:text=A%20general%20conclusion%20from%20this,FFM%20traits%20for%20most%20jobs.), and even [health and longevity.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228336988_Conscientiousness_and_Health_Across_the_Life_Course)

But mysteries regarding this trait have puzzled many for decades. If conscientiousness is so valuable, why isn’t everyone high in it? Why are some more conscientious than others? And if having low conscientiousness is so counterproductive, why was this not selected out by evolution? The answers are somewhat unexpected.

To tackle these questions, we must first consider its origins. Genes promoting this trait were likely selected over the course of evolution because conscientiousness contributes to success in achieving goals, serving one's survival, and the capacity to procreate and care for offspring. Indeed, [studies](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-29881-x) of identical twins reared in separate homes suggest that about 50% of one’s level of conscientiousness is determined by one’s genes.  Specifically, identical twins reared in separate homes develop very similar levels of conscientiousness, while non-identical ones reared in separate homes do not.

But conscientiousness is only one of a number of traits that can affect evolutionary fitness. Humans get their genes passed on to future generations in various ways. For example, over the course of human history, whilst some males have passed traits on through conscientious pursuit of resources, seeking of mates, and parenting; others have had their genes passed down through boldness, physical strength, and promiscuity. So people low in conscientiousness can get by as well. But it remains unclear whether being low in conscientiousness has been advantageous for some individuals or simply not an impediment because they have other attributes that make up for it.

Just as genes affect conscientiousness, so does socialization. Children are motivated to stay in the good graces of their parents to sustain their love, protection, and approval. They try to learn how to be good boys and girls as they are taught what their parents view as good vs. bad behavior and worthy goals to pursue in life.

They internalize these conscientious values and goals into what Freud called the superego and George Herbert Mead called the generalized other, so they can be sure they are living up to parental expectations. The superego is an internal judge, based largely on one’s parents, that assesses how well one is meeting learned morals and progressing toward desired goals.

The generalized other is a similar internalized audience, but Mead emphasized more that it is based on both parents and other societal influences. This inner self-critic develops because as infants become toddlers, parents begin responding to them with rewards when they meet their expectations, but with punishments when they do not. By internalizing and then trying to meet these expectations, the child can improve their chances of being treated favorably and minimize their chances of being treated unfavorably.

As a consequence, we all come to associate good feelings and security with believing we are living up to these internalized values and goals, but anxious and guilty when not. Parents, teachers and other influential people in our early lives vary in how much they prioritize the value of being conscientious, both in teaching this and in how they behave. And our wider culture will shape the values and goals they encourage in us, and that we are later likely to internalize as our inner moral norms.

Although genes and the socialization process determine a person’s general level of conscientiousness, this is not the whole story. Situations temporarily discourage or encourage conscientiousness in any of us. Although we tend to think that stress and pressure motivate us to be more conscientious, they often have the opposite effect. For example, stressful periods in our lives can make us feel overwhelmed and arouse anger, anxiety, frustration, impatience, or panic. These emotions tend to impede long-term thinking and encourage more impulsive actions. Someone generally could be a good worker or friend but become neglectful in fulfilling these roles under such cognitive or emotional strains.

In addition, traumatic events such as a near death experience, a cancer diagnosis, or loss of a loved one remind us that life is short, and so may shift a goal-oriented person more toward seeking as much enjoyment as possible on a daily basis. The Kurosawa film Ikiru illustrates this reaction as the protagonist Takashi initially responds to a terminal prognosis this way.

\_\_\_

When people are reminded of their mortality, they will strive harder to live up to their worldview and its values.

\_\_\_

Alternately, cues in situations may also encourage more conscientious. Friends may remind us of the value of carefully focusing focused on our long-term goals. One [study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/109258720000500101?icid=int.sj-full-text.similar-articles.2) showed that even a simple sign asking park visitors not to remove petrified wood from the Petrified Forest reduced such thefts.

Crucially, [psychological research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory) also shows that when people are reminded of their mortality, they will strive harder to live up to their worldview and its values, to feel more like significant contributors to the world who will leave a positive legacy into the future. For example, reminders of mortality have been shown to increase contributions to valued charities and causes like environmentalism. These findings were anticipated in Ikiru when the dying Takashi, after briefly becoming hedonistic, devotes the rest of his life to transforming a swamp into a children’s playground.

This would lead us to the surprising conclusion that becoming acutely aware of our mortality and the end of life often makes people more conscientious. As Ernst Becker wrote in his influential book, *The Denial of Death*, "The idea of death, the fear of it, haunts the human animal like nothing else; it is a mainspring of human activity."

Conscientious people generally enhance our lives as reliable, helpful friends and relatives. In addition, they have contributed many impressive accomplishments. Imagine the focused planning and hard work it took back in 1501 for Michelangelo to have taken a 10-ton block of marble and in three years transformed it into a six-ton statue of David that has awed millions of people for half a millennium. Conscientiousness, in allowing people to get things done well, is necessary to nearly all significant achievement.

\_\_\_

We are left with the unsettling admission that people high in conscientiousness have contributed substantially to the many horrors that have marred history.

\_\_\_

However, it’s important to recognize that conscientiousness can be overemphasized, which can make a parent or friend overbearing. Of greatest concern, a conscientious person raised as a neo-Nazi or an ISIS devotee can cause immeasurable harm to others who are portrayed in their own worldview as evil. When the Nazis built their death camps, organized, goal-oriented people undoubtedly contributed to the efficient killing of millions.

Because people’s worldviews determine their life goals, we are left with the unsettling admission that people high in conscientiousness have contributed substantially to the many horrors that have marred history. In contrast, conscientious people with life-promoting goals have contributed innumerable admirable actions and accomplishments in the arts, sciences, and helping professions. Conscientiousness will only enhance human experience primarily when people have also embraced constructive goals and values.
