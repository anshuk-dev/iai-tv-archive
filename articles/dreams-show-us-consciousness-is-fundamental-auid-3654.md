# Dreams show us consciousness is fundamental

*The structure of dreams reveals reality's observer problem*

**By Andrew T. Jaffe** | *13th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,051 words*

![Dreams show us consciousness is fundamental](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/dreams-show-us-consciousness-is-fundamental.webp)

---

*Every scientific experiment requires an observer; every measurement is made by one. But the observer itself remains elusive. **Andrew T. Jaffe** uses the example of a scientist within a dream who can discover the physical laws of the dream world but can't use the scientific method to discover they're dreaming. This isn’t a gap science will eventually fill because the observer isn’t just another object. Consciousness is fundamental; it is prior to the world that science describes.*

Last night, you undoubtedly became someone else. You were back in some random school frantically rushing to class to take a test you had forgotten about, or back living in a house you had not visited in decades. You may have been conversing without any shock or alarm with long-deceased friends or family or facing challenges that bore little resemblance to your waking life. While the dream lasted, nothing seemed unusual. Your dream identity felt totally normal. Then you woke up!

Many people dismiss dreams as just an odd byproduct of sleep. Others label dreaming as an evolutionary development to allow mammals to practice activities or take on challenges they may encounter during waking life so they are better prepared. I think dreams reveal something profound that most of us rarely stop to consider. The person you believe yourself to be is surprisingly unstable. Your age, memories, relationships, and even personality can shift instantly in dreams. The [autobiographical self](https://iai.tv/video/the-self-and-the-world) that seems so solid during the day can disappear completely without your noticing. But throughout the dream, something remains remarkably constant with waking life: there is still an observer having an experience. Whatever that observer is, it appears less attached to the identity it inhabits than we typically assume.

Every experience you have ever had, whether waking or dreaming, shares one remarkable feature. There has always been someone there to have it. Interestingly, this phenomenon is rarely investigated. Science has become extraordinarily successful at explaining the world before us. It has traced the origin of galaxies, explained the evolution of species, mapped the circuitry of the brain and the DNA of the body. Physics has uncovered laws governing matter and the movement of light with astonishing precision. But every scientific explanation begins with an assumption so obvious that it is rarely acknowledged: there is an observer.

All experiments require one, each measurement is made by one, and every theory is conceived by one. The observer itself remains elusive. Philosophers call one aspect of this mystery the hard problem of consciousness: why should physical processes be accompanied by subjective experience at all? Neuroscience confronts another challenge in the binding problem: how do countless disparate neural processes become the single unified experience each of us enjoys every moment? Beneath both questions lies an even simpler one: who, or what, is the observer?

Neuroscience has taught us through brain imaging which regions become active during various activities like perception, memory, language, emotion, and decision-making. Researchers are adept at identifying neural activity that consistently accompanies conscious experience. But, as of now, there is no place they can point to and confidently say this is where the one having that conscious experience can be found.

If, however, awareness is primary, and the brain secondary, we may be looking in the wrong place for the seat of awareness. It would be akin to looking for the dreamer by carefully examining only the dream. We already know from direct experience that in dreams, we often readily accept completely different lives without hesitation. But who is the “I” who accepts the dream world as real and observes this new character? Imagine spending an entire lifetime inside one of those dreams.

Take for example the dream experience of a young scientist who grows up asking the same questions scientists have always asked. Why does gravity work? How did life begin? Why does the brain produce thought? Why do human beings experience themselves as individuals? Her life’s work becomes dedicated to answering those questions. She slowly but surely transforms her field, mapping neural circuits, reconstructing evolution, and developing elegant theories that explain why brains construct such [convincing models of self](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-exists-and-it-is-an-illusion-auid-3347). Her predictions and experiments are confirmed by the scientific community, and her colleagues celebrate the groundbreaking discoveries that so consistently describe the world around her. She has no reason to consider a possibility where reality extends beyond the world she studies. Each successful experiment only reinforces the same conclusion. Any question must have an explanation somewhere inside the universe she can observe.

Then consider that this highly decorated scientist takes up the practice of meditation. She has heard about wonderful, non-dualistic, contemplative experiences and wishes to investigate them further. Sitting in her lab, she downloads the Waking Up app and takes a guided meditation. [Sam Harris](https://iai.tv/video/the-divided-self-sam-harris-roger-penrose) gives her the pointer to look for the looker, to seek to find the source that is observing. She is initially taken aback by the absurd nature of the directive; she can still observe her thoughts, memories, emotions, and sensations. But when she refocuses on the question and attempts to find this seat of awareness, she finds nothing. No point of self to refer to. Her first instinct is to explain the experience in familiar terms. She reasons that meditation has temporarily disrupted the Default Mode Network, producing the feeling that there is no enduring self.

Then, within the dream, she suddenly becomes lucid. Nothing in the dream itself changes. Her lab remains unchanged, the equipment continues making measurements, but she has had an instant and profound shift in terms of understanding who, in fact, she really is. The scientist she has spent a lifetime defending is only a character through which the dream is being experienced. The laboratory belongs to the dream. The brain she studies belongs to the dream. Even the scientific laws she has uncovered belong to the dream. And in considering her meditative experiment, she realizes that the observer she had been trying to locate was never something that existed as an object within the dream, but rather, it belonged to a different level of reality altogether that existed outside of the dream. Her dream remains completely coherent. What changes is the realization that the dreamer, not the dream character, is the one experiencing it. Her epiphany does not erase her dream character’s scientific discoveries. It places it in context. Her theories still explain why the dream behaves as it does. They still explain dream matter, dream biology, and dream brains. They were never capable of explaining the dreamer; only direct experience could.

The reason I find this thought experiment compelling is that we all have experienced being a dream character and convinced a dream was real. We have all awakened and recognized that the character we had become was only temporary. The dreamed experience was not destroyed by waking; it was just understood differently.

This naturally raises the question: could waking life contain a similar misunderstanding about identity? People who describe profound contemplative or nondual experiences often say something interesting. They rarely claim to have learned new scientific facts. Instead, they describe a change in perspective. The ordinary sense of being a separate individual becomes less convincing. Awareness feels more fundamental than the biography through which it is expressed. Different traditions explain those experiences in different ways, and they should not be mistaken for scientific evidence. They do suggest that changing the observer can reveal something that collecting more observations cannot. Most of us assume awareness itself is unique to each person. That feels obvious because our memories, personalities, beliefs, and emotions are all different. Strip those away, though, and the distinction becomes less clear. Dreams remind us that even our autobiography can change overnight. So the question becomes: does awareness change in the same way, or is it the constant feature behind every experience we have ever had?

[Cognitive Dream Interface Theory (CDIT)](https://www.amazon.com/Living-Dream-Philosophical-Scientific-Consciousness/dp/1917991622/ref=sr_1_1?crid=1TFFNQDFJSLBI&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.Q-BxdwtdFhtQ5vJGvu6AoIPU8MwLd86jzNK11Lc-79rPKgoC85EXnOmOubNwm5Vu.S4TYTGK5tQzko6QbdCyu5H7JdJm1ctC-uSFXAkBrZ3o&dib_tag=se&keywords=living+the+dream+cognitive+interface+theory&qid=1786108938&sprefix=dream+interface+%2Caps%2C175&sr=8-1) attempts to answer this question. It suggests that awareness is not something produced late in the story by sufficiently complicated matter. It may very well be the starting point. Our individual lives differ because the experiences unfolding through each of us differ, but the awareness observing through each unique window may be the same. Dreams offer a clue. In our dreams, a variety of characters and scenarios are both created and observed by one mind, one awareness. All explanations must end somewhere. Physics begins with laws and constants that are accepted as part of a framework from which everything else is explained. CDIT makes a similar move. It treats [awareness as a timeless, natural primitive](https://iai.tv/articles/annaka-harris-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3136), not as something supernatural or outside nature, but as the one thing that does not require another explanation beneath it. The question then becomes which starting point explains more while assuming less.

One reason I find this approach compelling is that awareness is the only phenomenon we know directly. Everything else, including the brain, the body, and the physical world, arrives through experience. Dreams demonstrate to us that awareness can present complete and convincing worlds populated by people, places, memories, and physical laws. That observation alone does not prove that waking reality is generated in the same way. It shows, though, that awareness is capable of organizing experience into a coherent world. Taking awareness as fundamental does not diminish science. Physics still explains why the universe behaves with such remarkable consistency. Chemistry explains the interactions that give rise to matter as we know it. Biology and evolution explain the extraordinary diversity of living organisms, and neuroscience continues to uncover how [the brain constructs the interface through which we navigate the world](https://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real). CDIT does not replace those explanations. It asks whether they are explaining the world itself, or the structure of the experience through which the world is encountered.

If awareness is fundamental, does that mean the external world is merely an illusion? I do not think so. CDIT is not arguing that each of us creates our own private universe. The remarkable consistency of science, and the fact that we all appear to inhabit the same lawful reality, argues against that. The proposal is different. Reality is shared, stable, and governed by consistent rules. The world itself does not disappear under CDIT. What changes is our understanding of what it is. Instead of viewing matter as the foundation of reality, CDIT treats awareness as the natural primitive from which our shared experience of matter, space, and time arises.

For many, this is hard to fathom, and few people are drawn to idealism. Waking life does not feel dreamlike. It feels solid, persistent, and independent of us. We cannot alter mountains by wishing them away, or suspend gravity by changing our beliefs. If CDIT is correct, that is exactly what we should expect. A convincing interface has to present itself as objectively real. Dreams usually feel completely real until we wake from them. A dream would lose its persuasive power the moment we recognized it as a dream. So our dreaming mind keeps us immersed until we awaken or rarely, become lucid within the dream. Waking life is vastly more coherent and stable than any ordinary dream, making the impression of an external material world even more compelling. From an evolutionary perspective, organisms that treat the environment as real survive far better than organisms that do not. Nothing about that observation conflicts with CDIT. It is exactly what the theory predicts.

Tonight you will almost certainly become someone else again. You will inherit another history, another body, and another world. While the dream lasts, that identity will feel as real as this one does now. Tomorrow morning you will wake and immediately recognize that the character you had become was never the whole of you. I cannot prove that waking life stands in the same relationship to awareness that dreams do. I just think dreams point toward something we rarely stop to consider. The one constant throughout every experience we have ever had has never been the body, the personality, or the autobiography. It has been awareness. Science will continue to more precisely reveal the extraordinary structure of the universe. But if dreams teach us anything, it may be that the observer will not be found by science as another object in the world. [It may only be recognized through the experience of being aware](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-a-dream-not-a-simulation-auid-3560).
