# How transhumanism dangerously ignores human nature

*The new religion of the mind*

**By Omari Edwards** | *14th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,173 words*

![How transhumanism dangerously ignores human nature](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/How-transhumanism-dangerously-ignores-human-nature.webp)

---

*At this year’s [HowTheLightGetsIn Festival](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay), transhumanism faced its reckoning. Once hailed as the next step in human evolution, the idea of transcending biology through technology now divides its own believers. In a fiery exchange between Zoltan Istvan, Àlex Gómez-Marín, Susan Schneider, and Adam Goldstein, IAI Contributing Editor [Omari Edwards](https://x.com/edwardsomari1?s=21) captures a movement in crisis, where the dream of digital immortality collides with the fear of losing what makes us human.*

Few ideas have divided thinkers more sharply than transhumanism. For some, it represents humanity’s next evolutionary step, the promise of overcoming disease, aging, and even death. For others, it marks the point where human ambition turns self-destructive, mistaking technological power for moral progress. As advances in AI and biotechnology blur the line between human and machine, the question is no longer science fiction but a pressing philosophical dilemma: should we seek to transcend our nature, or to understand it?

At this year’s [HowTheLightGetsIn Festival](https://howthelightgetsin.org/), a tent filled with early risers gathered to debate what has been called “the world’s most dangerous idea.” The atmosphere was expectant but uneasy. This was not a discussion about gadgets or engineering, but a confrontation over [the meaning of being human](iai-academy/courses/info?course=the-future-of-being-human) in an age determined to reinvent it.

\_\_\_

“Transhumanism is a death cult,” said Gómez-Marín

\_\_\_

Zoltan Istvan spoke first, and he didn’t hesitate. “Death,” he said evenly, “is a technical problem.” Transhumanism, to him, was the inevitable next step in evolution: eradicating aging, sleep, even hunger. “We don’t want to die because we starve,” he said. “We want to overcome suffering with science and technology.” He gestured at the audience. “Who here has lost someone they love? Transhumanism is a humanitarian project to make sure that doesn’t happen again.” Then, with a flourish of a man once again running for office, he added: “Of course it’s the world’s most dangerous idea, but a lot of dangerous ideas have been good for humanity.” The case for transhumanism thus made, the line in the sand drawn, if more tentatively than a few years ago. Transhumanism was bolstered by the success and take-up of [LLM technologies](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-can-uncover-humanitys-unknown-unknowns-auid-3083), but rather than strengthening opinions, it has shaken the true believers.

Àlex Gómez-Marín, the physicist and neuroscientist, smiled grimly. “I absolutely abhor transhumanism,” he began, provoking laughter and applause. He called the movement “an algorithmic invasion, fascinating, dangerous bullshit.” His metaphor of a traffic light was crisp: green for the wonders of technology, amber for the warnings, red for the catastrophe. “Transhumanism is a death cult,” he said, “a [pseudo-religion dressed in techno-scientific language](https://iai.tv/articles/the-false-religion-of-transhumanism-auid-3006) whose goal is to extinguish the human condition and ask us to clap as it happens.” The applause grew quieter.

\_\_\_

The claim that digital immortality awaits us, Schneider said, was “marketing masquerading as metaphysics.”

\_\_\_

Gómez-Marín’s point landed because it pierced the fantasy. “They think we are machines,” he said later, “and it’s a weird dualism, materialist and spiritual at once. They tell us we’ll upload our souls to the cloud. They don’t know what a human is, but they want to build its replacement.”

[Susan Schneider](iai-academy/courses/info?course=exploring-intelligence) followed, dry and lucid. “Oh boy,” she began, “what an amazing start.” She described herself as a “sober-minded transhumanist turned critic.” At eighteen, she had “drunk the Kool-Aid.” Now, she said, the dream of uploading minds was a “philosophical error.” “You wouldn’t survive the upload,” she argued. “You’d kill yourself and produce a copy.” The claim that digital immortality awaits us, she said, was “marketing masquerading as metaphysics.”

Schneider’s concern was not only metaphysical but political. “Big tech has taken these [philosophical ideas](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-in-the-machine),” she warned, “and used them for propaganda purposes, for regulatory capture. They distract us from digital privacy, mental health, and human flourishing.” It was a reminder that transhumanism’s loudest preachers, from Musk to Altman, tend to speak in the language of eternity while selling subscriptions to the present.

Adam Goldstein, the [Softmax](https://www.softmax.com/) AI researcher, entered as the moderate. “We’ve been transhuman since we were human,” he said. “We have more bacterial cells in our bodies than human cells. The question isn’t whether we’ll merge with technology: it’s whether the symbiosis will be healthy or toxic.” His tone was pragmatic: less prophecy, more engineering. “We need a science of symbiosis,” he insisted, “to measure whether humans and AIs are actually aligned. That’s how we build a good future.”

But as the debate unfolded, the temperature rose. Schneider challenged the dream of instant augmentation. “Imagine walking into a shop and coming out fluent in every language,” she said. “You might leave feeling good, but you may no longer be you.” Gómez-Marín nodded. “Progress isn’t always forward,” he said. “Suffering is part of what makes us human. Get over it.” His warning was sharp: “The opposite of life is not death, it’s the machine.”

Istvan countered with moral urgency. “We’ve all accepted death as natural,” he said, “but why should we? The miracle of life shouldn’t just end. We can fix that. We can save people.” His compassion was real. Yet it is exactly that appeal, salvation through engineering, that critics like Evgeny Morozov and Byung-Chul Han describe as the ideological core of Silicon Valley: a [religion of innovation](https://iai.tv/articles/silicon-valleys-quest-for-immortality-is-a-mistake-auid-2945) that sells deliverance while ignoring domination.

By the time Goldstein answered a question on regulation—“Regulation is the least elegant solution,” he said, “What we need is a consciousness that recognises we’re all in this together”—it was clear that no one on stage believed the future could be left to code.

\_\_\_

The transhumanist dream, like so many Western dreams before it, mistakes power for wisdom and progress for purpose.

\_\_\_

What struck me most was not the disagreement but the exhaustion beneath it. Transhumanism’s optimism has curdled into confusion. The fantasy of uploading our minds or transmuting our bodies, once a promise of freedom, now feels like an evasion of self-knowledge. Gómez-Marín called it “intellectual dialysis.” Schneider called it “pseudo-science.” And Istvan, who still believes, admitted, “We don’t have the ethical framework yet.”

That’s the point. We have the tools, the funding, the rhetoric, but not the philosophy. The faith that technology can solve [the problem of being human](https://iai.tv/articles/the-delusion-behind-upgrading-humanity-or-ending-it-auid-3195) has collapsed, leaving only the slogans. “It’s inevitable,” they say, which is the perfect marketing strategy for those who profit when it is.

As I left the tent, I thought of Gómez-Marín’s warning: “They think they’re gods, but gods are terrible with toys.” The world’s most dangerous idea, it seems, is not that we will become machines but that we have started to think like them.

Perhaps the real question is not whether technology can transcend our biology, but whether we can transcend the fantasies that built it. The transhumanist dream, like so many Western dreams before it, mistakes power for wisdom and progress for purpose. It promises freedom from the limits that define us, but in doing so risks erasing the very conditions under which freedom has meaning. We may yet build the machines that outthink us, but until we recover a philosophy of what it means to live and to die. No amount of code will save us from the confusion of our own creation.
