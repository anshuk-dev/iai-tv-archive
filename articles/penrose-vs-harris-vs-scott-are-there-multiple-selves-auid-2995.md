# Penrose vs Harris vs Scott: Are there multiple selves?

*Debating the divided self*

**By Nina Lyon** | *11th November 2024* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,107 words*

![Penrose vs Harris vs Scott: Are there multiple selves?](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/divided-self-write-up.webp)

---

*We can feel and even act like different people in different situations and at different times. Is a single self enough to hold together our messy, often contradictory collection of experiences and drives? At HowTheLightGetsIn 2024 in London, Sam Harris, Roger Penrose and Sophie Scott met to debate The Divided Self, hosted by Jack Symes. The bestselling philosopher, quantum physicist and leading neuroscientist investigated whether we need to update our model of the self, and how to do it.*

We take the existence of a self for granted: that there’s an *I* writing this review, a *you* reading it. But what is the nature of this *I* that’s carrying out the actions, and can it hold the strange multiplicity of experiences and behaviours that happen in our lives as a coherent whole? *The Divided Self* at HTLGI London 2024 brought together a stellar panel to tackle this question: philosopher, writer and meditation guru Sam Harris, physicist Roger Penrose, and neuroscientist Sophie Scott.

Host and [New Generation Thinker](https://www.durham.ac.uk/news-events/latest-news/2024/05/durham-philosopher-selected-as-bbc-new-generation-thinker/) Jack Symes asked the panel if can we assume a single self is where conscious experience takes place, and whether that self bears responsibility for our actions. More provocatively, could multiple selves offer a better way to explain the wildly divergent thoughts and contradictory behaviours that humans can display?

Sam Harris began with a familiar view for users of his excellent meditation app, [Waking Up](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/can-you-experience-enlightenment-through-sam-harris-waking-up-meditation-app). When we talk about the self, it refers to the subjective sense of being a subject separate from experience—the feeling that you’re “having” an experience. Most of our experience carries this subject/object duality. But, Harris argued, this is an illusion. First, neurologically, there’s no place in the brain hiding your ego. Second, if you search for this “I” within experience, you fail to find it, sometimes conclusively. Meditation and psychedelics help reveal this, but even without them, people regularly lose their sense of self by staying busy or focusing elsewhere. Selfhood is mostly introspective and easily interrupted. Therefore, we don’t need it, and practices like mindfulness can free us from it.

Roger Penrose’s view was that the self is tightly linked to consciousness and, perhaps, memory. The conversation brought up the philosophical challenges posed by the practice of [cutting the corpus callosum](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/11546-corpus-callosotomy) in epilepsy patients. While it stopped seizures, it also could also create two separate fields of experience in one human, as Harris later pointed out. The same person might try to embrace their spouse with one hand while trying to strangle them with the other. For Penrose, the question became one of continuity: consciousness forms a chain from early childhood until death. If conscious experience is like a string of beads, is the self its own entity, distinct from the beads of individual experiences shaped by the changing conditions around them?

Sophie Scott argued that in addressing problems posed by a divided brain or multiple experiences, we first need to define “self.” Is it Harris’s subjective sense of a personal subject or Penrose’s continuity of memories feeding into identity? Memory, Scott noted, is biologically unique: other body parts regenerate over time, but [neurons generally don’t](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/neuroregeneration).

Damage to brain function can dramatically affect identity because memories and world knowledge are neuron-bound, stored in a constantly remodelled network. Our social interactions shape this network, allowing us to adapt to different social settings. Yet, Scott explained, people can retain a self despite impaired memory – a musician devastated by [encephalitis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/encephalitis/) still knew his name, who his wife was, and how to conduct music. If memory is key to our experience, it may be less in its necessity in forming the self and more about its ability to make sense of the world around it so that it isn’t constantly unfamiliar and distressing. The subjective sense of sameness over time is illusory: as Scott reflected, reading her teenage diary at fifty showed she’s not the same person now. Experience reshapes us.

Is it, therefore, mistaken to view this experience as selfhood? Subject/object dualism makes us feel like a subject aware of a separate object-body, Harris said, like a video game’s player-view. But we’re not in touch with the world as it exists outside our brain. Even so, no matter how unsure we are of external reality, consciousness – the experiencing that we’re doing right now – is something that we can be sure exists.

For Penrose, whether or not consciousness is fundamental, it’s [connected with the wave function collapse](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-the-collapse-of-the-wave-function-auid-2120) in physics. But, he clarified, consciousness doesn’t cause collapse. Physics needs to find more answers – there is a physical law out there that we don’t yet understand that may make sense of consciousness. Learning more about computers and [Gödel’s incompleteness theorem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/goedel-incompleteness/) taught Penrose that a certain sentence can be true, and you can see it’s true even though it’s not derivable from the rules you put in. That quality of understanding, Penrose said, transcends what computing can do.

For Harris, abandoning selfhood isn’t just philosophically accurate: it enhances experience, as literature on transcendent experiences shows. But if Harris is right, this poses a problem for moral responsibility: without an *I* owning will, can we have free will? Free will, for Harris, is a conceptual error downstream of the error of the self: our behaviour is determined by our genes and our environment, and while we may need to lock up dangerous criminals for their and others’ safety, to blame them would be mistaken: they didn’t intentionally make themselves.

Both Scott and Penrose identified elements of action that cannot be conscious: the constant bodily adjustments that keep us from falling over when sitting on a chair, or the lightning reactions of tennis players. There was, however, for Penrose, something in free will which is not random and has a conscious element. There are, he added, elements of ongoing mystery to what consciousness is, which physics is trying to explore. If Penrose is right, we can’t ultimately understand the questions about experience and the continuity of experience that characterise any debate about the self without understanding [consciousness](http://iai.tv/articles/quantum-collapse-holds-the-key-to-consciousness-auid-2952) first.

Multiple selves might be having a moment with Tiktok’s current self-diagnosis trend of [dissociative identity disorder](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/abcs-child-psychiatry/202203/the-tiktok-inspired-surge-dissociative-identity-disorder), but our panel didn’t have much truck with them. And it was divided on the problem of the self. Is it an illusion created by the brain that we can opt out of and transcend? Is it a continuity of consciousness linked to memory and quantum processes? Or is it best understood as a product of our experiences and interactions in an outward-facing social world?
