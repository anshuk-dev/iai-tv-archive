# War does not need a grand narrative

*But they may still be helpful*

**By Stathis N. Kalyvas** | *14th March 2024* | *Read time: approx. 5 mins* | *1,087 words*

![War does not need a grand narrative](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/24-03-12-The-problem-with-grand-narratives-of-war-2.webp)

---

*Since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, grand narratives of war have been springing up everywhere. From Conflict by [David Petraeus 2023](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/the-big-ideas/speakers-2/david-petraeus), (appearing at the upcoming Howthelightgetsin Festival), to On Wars by Michael Mann 2023, and How States Think by John Mearsheimer and Sebastian Rosato 2023, these sweeping accounts aim to provide profound insight into conflict and human nature. Gladstone Professor of Government at the University of Oxford, Stathis Kalyvas, in this short article, explains why these accounts fail, but why people still want them anyway, and why their comfort and familiarity makes them desirable.*

There is no question that grand narratives of war are fashionable. Historical accounts, taking a broad sweep over tens or even hundreds of years, are published regularly, and they are often penned by a range of thinkers across the academic and ideological spectrum.

These narratives draw typically from a large corpus of primary research and attempt to extract useful insights both about war and human nature. But do they succeed? Are we learning something new or at least profound? The answer is likely no. But this still doesn’t get to the heart of why there’s such high demand for them, and the other purposes they serve.

One recent stab at such a grand narrative is *Conflict: The Evolution of Warfare from 1945 to Ukraine* by General David Petraeus, a military commander in the infamous *War on Terror*,  and Andrew Roberts, a British popular historian. They review the major post-Second World War conflicts to identify how the battlefield has shaped the way war is waged from military technology, leadership, and coalition building to name but a few themes. Their focus is both presentist and unabashedly US-centric and the conflicts that attract most of their attention are those that entailed US participation: Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Iraq loom large.

If there is a key insight, it is the blurring of the boundaries between war and peace, high and low-intensity war, conventional and unconventional warfare. The apparent emergence of a perpetual state of low-level conflict is masked by plausible deniability that turns war into a process less visible and intense yet much more present. The ongoing conflict in [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war) is an example, involving US troops, Wagner Group Russian mercenaries, and Iran-backed militias among a variety of other armed actors

Yet, this is an insight that manages to be both generic and questionable. It could be argued that boundaries between war and peace were equally, if not more blurred, in the past. Think of the highly violent European colonial expansion in [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa#:~:text=In%201870%2C%2010%25%20of%20the,Ethiopia%20retaining%20their%20full%20sovereignty.&text=The%201884%20Berlin%20Conference%20regulated,emblematic%20of%20the%20%22scramble%22.) which took place in the second half of the nineteenth century during a period that historians describe as the “British Peace.”

The attempt to peer into the future furthermore produces a correspondingly hazy perspective: “Trying to predict the future of warfare is notoriously difficult” the two authors point out. However, they still feel compelled to venture some observations and reflections on some of the potential developments that lie ahead, an effort that feels forced and vague.

Now take Michael Mann’s *On War* , which is equally thick but much more ambitious, ranging across a much greater swath of history, beginning with Rome and ending with the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Tellingly, one chapter bears the title “A Thousand Years of Europe.” Mann is a historical sociologist with a strong theoretical bent, best known for his monumental four-volume treatise *The Sources of Social Power*.

He is a master of extracting theoretical insights from vast quantities of historical writing. Mann manages to distill the drivers of the decision to go to war down to three key points: greed, status-honor-glory, and the enjoyment of domination.His effort to theorize and speculate about the future is unquestionably serious. But, despite his valiant efforts, Mann also ends up equally swallowed by the immensity of the material he grapples with.

Mann’s narrative on the whole is that most interstate wars were based on irrational understandings of means or ends. This is contrary to the well-known and controversial John Mearsheimer and Sebastian Rosato’s story (the two representatives of the realist school of International Relations). In *How States Think: The Rationality of Foreign Policy* they argue that many of the things that might strike us as [irrational are not](https://www.mearsheimer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Why-the-Ukraine-Crisis-Is.pdf). Theirs is less a grand narrative of war and more a single-minded argument illustrated with a rich historical palette of vignettes.

\_\_\_

They set out to critique the tendency to characterize the behavior of international leaders we disapprove of as “irrational.” The initial motivation is to show that Vladimir Putin’s decision to invade Ukraine was [not as irrational](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JrMiSQAGOS4) as typically portrayed in the media. Instead, they argue “Putin and his advisers thought in terms of straightforward balance-of-power theory.”(p. xi). In their eyes, Ukraine’s move toward the West was a power ploy that [threatened Russia](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ClitqYW8HVk&t=251s); they had no choice but to react.

Their understanding of rationality is extremely broad. States are rational if their policies are based on credible theories and result from a deliberative decision-making process. By this definition, one would think that all autocratic regimes would be irrational. This turns out not to be the case: even autocrats deliberate before making their decisions and base them on credible theories. One might ask here, why does this matter? “The consequences are profound,” they argue, since this reveals that,  “states have a sound basis for making foreign policy” (p. 224). Yet, it is unclear if such a statement helps us understand foreign policy better; granting the soundness of foreign policy does not necessarily make it any more acceptable or less objectionable to those negatively affected by it.

Despite their differences, all three books converge to remind us that war is alive and kicking and that Plato was right: only the dead have seen the end of war. However, there is little beyond this point that sticks. It is just hard to identify any profound insights. But perhaps this genre serves an altogether different purpose.

Indeed, the similarity of the grand narratives of war to children’s stories is striking. Like them, they follow familiar patterns and offer comfort and familiarity through endless repetition; they stimulate readers’ imaginations by transporting them to exotic locales in a way that challenges them just enough. It is the combination of repetition and endless variation around well-known themes rather than the supply of new insights and perspectives that make them so popular and explains why this genre continues to thrive.
