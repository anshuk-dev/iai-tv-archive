# The campaign against empathy must be resisted

*Nietzsche, Bloom, and the will to care*

**By Gigla Gonashvili** | *25th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,175 words*

![The campaign against empathy must be resisted](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-campaign-against-empathy-must-be-resisted2.webp)

---

*In recent years, empathy has gotten a bad rap. Paul Bloom's famous work* Against Empathy*argued that empathy was necessarily biased and should be thrown out. Figures like Elon Musk argue our instinct for compassion is leading to bad outcomes. Here, philosopher **Gigla Gonashvili** argues we must reclaim our empathy from those who seek to temper it. We must embrace our will to care.*

As we watch the world aflame today, we naturally wonder with whom we should empathize the most, or whether our empathy is of any use at all. Recently, there has been a growing discontent with empathy, primarily due to its partial and biased nature. And yet, empathy cannot be so easily discarded or downgraded. It is inextricably tied to the concept of the will—a fundamental concept in both philosophy and religion.

It is clear that we humans, along with other living beings, have empathy. It is a complex emotion that binds us to others and makes us think about them and their perspectives. Yet, on closer inspection, [questions begin to arise](https://iai.tv/video/the-empathy-trap-dan-zahavi). How much empathy should we have? For whom should we have it? Should we rely on it at all? Is it the true foundation of morality?

A strong case has been made against empathy—most famously by [Paul Bloom](https://iai.tv/video/human-nature-and-the-possibility-of-utopia-paul-bloom) in *[Against Empathy: The Case for Rational Compassion](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/against-empathy-paul-bloom?variant=32122194853922)* (2016). He argues that empathy is, in fact, a poor moral guide, one that might lead us blindly into other people’s shoes without providing a proper ethical reason for doing so.

\_\_\_

Unlike compassion, which has deeper, more ancient roots in both religious and moral thought, empathy is a relatively recent concept.

\_\_\_

This line of argumentation is indeed valid. For instance, a [study conducted in 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00302/full), “Empathic Neural Responses Predict Group Allegiance,” revealed a considerable group bias in us. It turned out that participants’ brains reacted more strongly to seeing hands pierced by a needle when those hands were labeled as belonging to their own religious group. Thus, empathy is not impartial: [group bias seems to be deeply ingrained in us](https://iai.tv/articles/the-dark-side-of-empathy-auid-2558).

In place of empathy, Bloom recommends what he calls “rational compassion.” He defines compassion as a genuine care for others’ well-being without becoming emotionally immersed in their feelings. This compassion can be nurtured rationally, as rationality provides the evident means to achieve it without being an end in itself (that is, the goal is not to remain simply rational and cool-headed).

This focus, elaborated by Bloom, is certainly valuable: it highlights our shortsightedness (in which we so often persist) and our sense of group-belonging, which creates the danger of tribalism—even among people who consider themselves world citizens.

Yet, to my eyes, his argumentation leaves some fundamental aspects of empathy and compassion untreated. Therefore, in what follows, I will try to tackle the thorny issue of our shared feelings.

Unlike compassion, which has deeper, more ancient roots in both religious and moral thought, empathy is a relatively recent concept. [First elaborated by Theodor Lipps at the end of the 19th century](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/empathy/#HisInt), it was initially designed to highlight the more cognitive, perspectival aspects of our experience of others. Empathy, while it has Greek roots, actually comes from the German Einfühlung (taken up most notably by Husserlian phenomenology) and can be translated as “feeling-into.” Compassion, on the other hand, is much like the Greek sympatheia: a “feeling-with” or “suffering-with” (com-patire).

The etymologies themselves do not provide much guidance. Is “feeling-with,” for example, more distanced—and therefore better—than “feeling-into”? And if we insist that it is compassion that nudges us toward a position of genuine care, for whom do we care? For how many people can we care, given our limited resources? And how does this caring differ from simply wishing somebody well and giving them well-intended advice? Furthermore, how does this solve the highly intricate reality of mutually reflected feelings—feelings that are not only brought to a conscious level but also lead to strong emotional binding, be it attachment or even the narcissistic idealization of another person?

While some useful distinctions can be made between the concepts of empathy and compassion, I would argue that the emphasis should be placed on the “pathic” root of both words (coming from the Greek pathos). It has to do with being affected. I can be affected by many things, even by my own embodiedness (auto-affection), but also by my being-with-others, which opens up the primordial dimensions of our affective (pathic) experiences. Let me point out some relevant findings from experimental science to illustrate this.

For decades, neuroscience has pointed to the existence of so-called mirror mechanisms. Overlapping neural circuits are activated both when one performs an action and when one observes another performing it (notably in the work of [Giacomo Rizzolatti and Vittorio Gallese](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8713554/)). Humans also appear remarkably predisposed to imitation: neonates, even before fully developing visual acuity, [can mimic an adult sticking out their tongue](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/897687/). From a very early age, we are hardwired to reflect and incorporate the gestures and expressions of those around us.

Therefore, from the very beginning, we are quite literally in somebody else’s shoes. And this is exactly what creates problems. Let me offer a deliberately provocative example—a passage from Friedrich Nietzsche’s [Zarathustra](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1998/1998-h/1998-h.htm) that reads as follows:

Is not pity the cross upon which he who loves humankind is nailed?

Nietzsche is talking about Jesus. He uses the word Mitleid (translated as pity by Graham Parkes), which is in fact compassion (suffering-with). Is this some kind of crude attack on Christianity, a religion of compassion? I will not ponder Nietzsche’s choice of words, nor offer an apology for it, but simply point out that he was a thinker who thought deeply about the mean-spiritedness of humans. He recognized our jealousy, our close-mindedness, our malicious desire to constantly put others down—in short, the highly distilled poison brought about by humans simply being human.

Therefore, when we talk about compassion—which cannot exist without sharing feelings, however rational they might seem—we have to recognize that it also means sharing feelings and emotions we don’t want to have, and realities we don’t want to know. Somewhat against Nietzsche, it should be pointed out that Jesus was not only someone who told us to love our enemies, but also the figure from whom phrases such as these originate:

My mother and my brothers are those who hear the word of God and do it. (Luke 8:21)

And also:

Do you think that I have come to give peace on earth? No, I tell you, but rather division… They will be divided, father against son and son against father, mother against daughter and daughter against mother… . (Luke 12:51–53)

Harsh words, to say the least. The redeeming interpretation is that Jesus warns us against natural kinship and pushes us toward a universal love. And yet, our feelings for a specific group of people cannot simply be transplanted elsewhere. After all, we were raised and nurtured by a few significant others, not by all of humanity. Doesn’t this universalism run the risk of rendering our feelings rather neutral—making us simply friendly, polite, and ultimately guilty when we fail to live up to the standard? How does one learn to genuinely care?

Obviously, we do care, right from the very beginning. And if we want to care more, we must pay attention to the moment when we learn drawing a principle of action from a passion (from a pathic experience, to put it technically). Let us now turn to another German philosopher.

In his [Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals](https://archive.org/details/immanuel-kant-groundwork-for-the-metaphysics-of-morals) (1785), Immanuel Kant proposed the famous categorical imperative, stating that our actions should be guided by a maxim (a principle of action) that we would want to become universal, valid for everyone. Yet, how does one come to desire, or will, such a universal principle?

Kant makes it clear that the only thing in the world that can be qualified as unconditionally good is the good will (guter Wille). One might possess intellectual virtues (the power of judgment) or moral virtues (courage, for instance), but without a good will, there is no proper grounding for our actions.

The will can be good despite the consequences. It is good through the mere act of willing, and not as a means to an end. Thus, Kant frames the will as something highly interior or immanent, while maintaining that this will cannot exist without a principle.

Obviously, the Kantian elaboration leans mostly toward a universal, impartial understanding of justice (akin to the [Rawlsian veil of ignorance](https://iai.tv/articles/rawls-vs-nozick-justice-or-freedom-auid-2854)) and doesn’t say much about empathy or compassion. In fact, Kant regarded them as dangerous for obscuring the true principle of action. But precisely because this will is good through the mere act of willing (as if by its auto-affection), it is a highly affective thing; that is, it is also a highly shared thing. And therein lies a somewhat hidden hope: that this will—our will—cannot exist without a regard for others, without a care for them, even if Kant himself wouldn’t use this language to elaborate his highly formal imperative.

Today, as we look around and see the world aflame, the international order crumbling, and our horizons narrowing and suffocating us, we might find it futile to ask the Kantian question of whether our principle of action could be shared by a humanity currently at war with itself.

As we are relentlessly bombarded with images and videos of fire and suffering, our first reaction is likely a helpless disbelief: “This can’t be happening.” But if we look deeper, we might hear a voice saying: “This can’t be our will; this can’t be what we humans want.” And in that voice lies hope.

Regardless of the sides we might take—indeed, quite often we are forced to choose between bad and worse—we cannot close our eyes to things that are beyond horror. We need to care, and we need to spare.

\_\_\_

We should learn what we care about, how much we care, and talk with other people who care too—tell them that it is impossible for us not to care.

\_\_\_

Even when it is explicitly clear who the attacker is and who the victim is, one might still feel a sort of empathy when something horrible befalls the former. This does not mean being blind to justice or to personal and international borders; it simply means recognizing a normal human reaction.

The alternative to this reaction, however naïve empathy might seem, is indifference. Or worse: a will to destruction. This is where the fire spreads and consumes everything. I am personally reminded of the visceral 1959 war movie [Fires on the Plain](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0053121/) by Kon Ichikawa. It portrays a group of Japanese soldiers in the final stages of the war whose social bonds disintegrate under the pressure of hunger and despair, resulting in violence, madness, and cannibalism. It reveals an utter destitution and collapse.

The enemy (the Americans) isn’t even present for most of the film. Only at one point do the Japanese catch a faraway glimpse of them and ask each other if that is their enemy. It is a poignant moment, foretelling the imminence and immanence of destruction. Looking at human societies today, we might be standing before this exact kind of danger.

Let me steer this discussion toward a conclusion. In both philosophy and religion, the will is a fundamental concept. We may not believe in the will of some omnipotent God, but it is clear that existence is not merely a collection of brute facts. Things are moved, so to say, from inside—even if the proper source of that movement remains unknown to us, the supposed movers of our bodies and subjects of our own experiences.

Schopenhauer talked about the [will to life](https://iai.tv/articles/schopenhauer-and-the-insatiable-will-to-live-auid-2262): an irrational drive leading to procreation and suffering. Nietzsche tried to make it more seductive, terming it the will to power as a homage to the self-inventiveness of life [despite all its suffering](https://iai.tv/articles/schopenhauer-vs-nietzsche-the-meaning-of-suffering-auid-1801). Before them both was Kant, who wanted to ensure that the will was necessarily endowed with an inner principle, giving us the concept of the “Categorical Imperator” (as Nietzsche jokingly remarked).

Maybe it is time to give this will another twist and term it the will to care. Basically, the universe and all living (or non-living) things care enough to exist. They care enough to give and offer themselves. Everybody knows life isn’t free; you pay the price with your own skin. But what makes it bearable and, more importantly, worthwhile is that there is a genuine, unconditional caring—free from the paralyzing friction of second-guessing. This entails a spontaneous, affective moment, an affective givenness and caring (the same way you would instinctively care if your own body were on fire). From this pathic baseline, we can elaborate our concepts of empathy and compassion without falling into the traps of bias and shortsightedness.

Furthermore, as for us, who feel like helpless spectators of a world aflame, perhaps we should care how we share and to whom we give our metaphorical two cents. Our small opinions and support matter greatly in the end. We should learn what we care about, how much we care, and talk with other people who care too—tell them that it is impossible for us not to care. We can share our care. Focusing on our will (and our caring) provides a flexible starting point for creating or repairing the connections that might just snatch human communities and societies from the consuming flames of destruction.
