# You are not the person you see in the mirror

*The mirror test doesn't prove self-awareness in the way you think*

**By Marina Zilbergerts** | *11th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,730 words*

![You are not the person you see in the mirror](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/you-are-not-the-person-you-see-in-the-mirror.webp)

---

*When we are young, parents point out our reflection in the mirror and tell us, “that’s you”. Yet something always feels uncanny and a little off. The reflection in the mirror misses something vital about our identity as human beings: our inner self, the consciousness inhabiting the body. Philosopher **Marina Zilbergerts** argues the consequence of this is that human beings and other creatures are not self-aware in the way we think they are. The mirror test as a test to judge self-awareness is a fantasy of psychology.*

What do *Snow White*, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, *The Matrix*, and *Harry Potter* have in common?

All of these archetypal stories return to the mirror as a way of exploring the different dimensions of self-awareness.

In *Snow White*, the mirror is an arbiter of beauty whose truthfulness drives the Evil Queen to rage. In *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, the portrait in the attic serves as a true mirror that reveals the protagonist’s moral condition. In *The Matrix*, Neo touches the mirror and is drawn into a liminal space where the boundary between appearance and mind is renegotiated. In *Harry Potter*, the Mirror of Erised reveals the heart’s deepest desires.

Looking into a mirror, we expect to see a correspondence between ourselves and our reflection. Yet what we see in the mirror does not always match our self-image. What lies at the root of this discrepancy?

The experience of seeing our reflection can be marked by an uncanny sense of mismatch, making us more keenly aware of what the mirror cannot reveal: our sense of self, our identity.

**1. The mirror test**

The “mirror test” has been used as a standard by psychologists assessing self-awareness in animals. We know, for instance, that chimpanzees, dolphins, and elephants, and even, [as a recent study suggests](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-25837-0), cleaner wrasse fish, can recognize their bodies in a mirror. Scientists observed these animals interacting with their own reflections by attempting to remove a mark from their bodies, or by using the mirror to inspect themselves. These behaviors were [taken as evidence of their self-awareness](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.167.3914.86).

However, this test has many limitations. For one, it does not assess self-awareness in animals that rely less on vision and more on other senses, such as smell, as in the case of dogs or cats. Other animals may simply not take the reflected image to be relevant to them, even if they do possess other forms of self-related awareness.

It seems that one could know every detail of the processes of one’s body, and still not know oneself.

The ability to recognize one’s body in a mirror may indicate a form of bodily self-recognition, but it does not necessarily capture [an awareness of oneself as a subject](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-exists-and-it-is-an-illusion-auid-3347), as a being with an inner life that unfolds in time.

In the era of AI, the question of self-awareness extends to non-living systems. What does it mean for AI to be self-aware? Cognitive scientists and AI researchers, particularly those working within a computational framework, rely on a functionalist definition: a system is considered self-aware [if it contains a model of itself within itself](https://global.oup.com/ukhe/product/the-emperors-new-mind-9780198784920?cc=gb&lang=en&) and can access and convey information about that model. Interestingly, this definition sets a higher bar than the standard mirror test because it demands a more complex form of self-modeling.

But what does this say about AI self-awareness? Does it mean that a system capable of interacting with its own body in a mirror and representing its internal parameters is thereby self-aware? And more fundamentally, what do we mean when we speak of self-awareness in the human sense? On what grounds do we recognize it in ourselves?

**2. The stranger in the mirror**

At four years old, I was fascinated by a big mirror at my grandmother’s house. Having no siblings at the time, I would play with my own reflection. I experienced the image reflected back to me as a fascinating trickster, a twin from a parallel dimension. When I wasn’t looking in the mirror, who knows what she was up to?

If an adult asked me to identify my body in the mirror, I would have responded correctly. I knew where my eyes were, where my ears were, where my teeth were. But I also remember entertaining the possibility that it wasn’t really me, or that it wasn’t *all* *that I am*. There was a strange sense of looking both at my own body and at something, or someone, other.

My selfhood, perhaps my *soul*, so tied up as it was in my inner imaginary world, was not reflected in what I saw in front of me. And I was sharply aware of that distinction.

Over the years, the act of looking in the mirror became more habitual. I checked my appearance, adjusted my hair, made sure my self-presentation struck some form of compromise between authenticity and the expectations of my surroundings. The sense of strangeness receded into the background.

But it did not disappear entirely, and in rare and unexpected moments, it returned.

Looking in the mirror after I had my first child was one such experience. My appearance remained mostly the same, but I didn’t feel the same looking at it. The mirror wasn’t able to reflect the upheavals of identity that had just transpired in me*.* *Is this still me, now, that there is this other person who is a part of me, yet no longer physically part of me*? *Does this new responsibility permanently alter who I am, or who I would become?* These thoughts and sensations faded, but the memory of that uncanniness remained.

The sense of “the stranger in the mirror” can appear at different moments in people’s lives. When we do something we are ashamed of, we may find it difficult to meet our own reflection. When we overcome an important setback and acquire new confidence, we may suddenly feel like a new version of ourselves is looking back at us from the mirror. Our moods and inner motions, too, influence what we see. In all these cases, it is our self-awareness—the sense of the self beyond the mirror—that shapes how we encounter our physical reflection.

It is precisely this kind of anti-reflection, the *strangeness* we feel in encountering our own image, that reveals the presence of self-awareness.

**3. AI and the coherence of the self**

What does the mirror test actually reveal about self-awareness?

Consider an example posed by Roger Penrose: [a video camera pointed at a mirror, forming a model of itself within itself](https://global.oup.com/ukhe/product/the-emperors-new-mind-9780198784920?cc=gb&lang=en&). Is this system self-aware?

![figure 1 you are not the person you see in the mirror copy](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzI5OSwxOTFd/figure-1-you-are-not-the-person-you-see-in-the-mirror-copy.webp)

*Fig. 1: Penrose’s example of a camera pointed at a mirror*

This example satisfies part of the functionalist definition I outlined earlier, which involves the capacity for self-reference. But would we go so far as to call it self-aware? The answer seems clearly negative. But the question is why. What are we missing?

The camera lacks integrated meta-processing, we might say. As such, it is no different from a thermostat. It has input and output, but no form of meta-cognition.

To supply that meta-cognition, let us increase the level of technological complexity. Imagine a humanoid robot running on a sophisticated AI system with billions of neural parameters, gazing into a mirror, processing visual data from its own reflection, and reporting on it:

![figure 2 you are not the person you see in the mirror copy](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzMwMCwzMDBd/figure-2-you-are-not-the-person-you-see-in-the-mirror-copy.webp)

*Fig. 2: Robot self-reflecting via a mirror*

The system can correlate the physical coordinates of its mechanical body with what appears in the mirror. But does this amount to self-awareness in any meaningful sense? Again, something seems to be missing.

Let us turn to a more human example to see what might still be lacking. Suppose a person acquired a superpower that allows them to track all the physical processes of their body. They could identify what the stomach is digesting and pinpoint the location of an infection. They could log the content of every thought, register each emotional shift, and accumulate all of it as data.

And yet, such a person might remain prone to self-deception. They might struggle to sustain meaningful relationships, unable to connect their actions, desires, and emotions into a stable way of being with others. They would possess an exhaustive account of their processes, and yet lack any understanding of their own heart.

It seems that one could know every detail of the processes of one’s body, and [still not know oneself](https://iai.tv/video/catching-sight-of-our-self).

Perhaps what we mean by self-awareness in the human sense is the experience of a self that unfolds coherently over time, accompanied by a knowledge of one’s desires and motivations—not only that they exist, but *why they matter* and how they relate to this evolving identity.

The lack of a cohesive self becomes clearly apparent when dealing with artificial systems. As Dan Lloyd observes in “[What It Is Like to Be a Bot](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1292675/full),” the system

*routinely apologizes and often repeats the governing principle from the inaugural prompt only to generate one which frequently contains a new instance of error. At every turn GPT initially claims it is confident in its answers but will immediately rescind any claim […] it seems disconnected from its own prescriptions.*

It is precisely this lack of cohesion that underlies another of Lloyd’s observations: the system’s remarkable capacity for role-playing. Because it is not anchored in any enduring sense of self, it can assume virtually any persona without contradiction:

*The LLMs can pretend to be almost anything. … The compulsive confabulations mixed with the bold pose of complete confidence are dangerous. GPT-4 can diagnose disease, propose legal strategies, recommend investments, write term papers, yet it would be foolish to regard any of its utterances as fact.*

What makes LLMs so adept at this role-playing is precisely their lack of self. Without it, LLMs cannot distinguish a true narrative from a false one; they are not attached to any narrative of their own. There is nothing that is more true or false for them *personally*.

This latter description of GPT reminded me of the psychiatric case of Mr. Thompson described by Oliver Sacks in [*The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/691748/the-man-who-mistook-his-wife-for-a-hat-by-oliver-sacks/). Mr. Thompson was upbeat, communicative, and capable of reasoning, yet he lacked a sense of self. “Deprived of continuity, of a quiet, continuous inner narrative,” Sacks observes, much as Lloyd does of GPT, “he is driven to a proliferation of pseudo-narrative, a pseudo-continuity, a pseudo-world populated by pseudo-people and phantoms.” Those around him began to wonder whether he, the human being, still possessed a soul.

To be self-aware, then, it seems that one would require a coherent self to be aware *of*. And this self, the *inner* self, [is not something that can be captured in a mirror](https://iai.tv/articles/how-to-create-a-consciousness-test-auid-3080).
