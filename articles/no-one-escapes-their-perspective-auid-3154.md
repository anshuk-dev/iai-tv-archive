# No one escapes their perspective

*Jason Blakely on Truth, Tradition, and Dialogue*

**By Jason Blakely** | *29th April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,613 words*

![No one escapes their perspective](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/no-one-escapes-their-perspective.webp)

---

*We like to believe we’re on the side of reason, that our politics follow from facts, while others are trapped in their blinkered perspective. But what if that’s the greatest illusion of all? In this article, Contributing Editor of IAI News, Omari Edwards, speaks with political philosopher Jason Blakely, who argues that no one escapes their perspective: not policy-makers, not political commentators, not even you. Our beliefs, Blakely contends, are shaped not by truth alone but by deep, often invisible forces: identity, power, fear, and historical accident. Blakely calls for a radical shift: expose ideology wherever it hides, dismantle the myths of neutrality, and build a politics that begins with the admission that everyone is navigating in the dark.​*

At the heart of Blakely’s latest book, [Lost in Ideology,](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/lost-in-ideology/9781788216630/) is a deceptively simple claim: ideology is a cultural tradition. We should think of political ideologies the way we think of language, religion, food, or music, as traditions passed down through history, giving shape and meaning to our social world. “We’ve forgotten that ideology is a cultural tradition, similar to language or food or music,” Blakely said, noting that people use *ideology* as a slur just meaning *bad or wrong ideas.* However misguided a political viewpoint may seem, [it cannot be dismissed as just stupidity or “brain science” gone awry, as some neuroscientists now argue.](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/08/science/ideology-neuroscience-politics-zmigord.html) Every ideology, Blakely insisted, is “a phenomenon, a thing in the world,” [a lived tradition that makes sense on its own terms to its adherents.​](http://iai.tv/articles/political-tribes-are-predictable-auid-2983)

To illuminate this, Blakely offered a vivid metaphor: ideologies are like maps we use to navigate the political terrain. Humans are meaning-making creatures, and an ideology provides an orienting vision of how society should be. The trouble comes when we confuse the map for the terrain; imagining that our preferred worldview is simply Reality itself, while everyone else’s is a faulty or dangerous distortion. Blakely [drew on the story by Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges, *On Exactitude in Science*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/audio/2013/jan/04/will-self-jorge-luis-borges), about an empire so obsessed with cartography that it creates a map the exact size of the territory. Similarly, we often unconsciously treat our own ideological map as if it were the whole world. “We think we don’t have an ideology… we think we just have the geography, and everyone else has ideology, just wrong ideas,” Blakely said.​

[Recognizing that everyone, including ourselves, operates from a particular ideological map is the first step to finding our way out of the fog.](http://iai.tv/articles/mexican-philosophy-vs-trumps-post-truth-world-auid-3053) “I self-consciously hold a map,” Blakely stressed, “the map is not the same as the terrain.” This doesn’t mean our political visions are false, only that they are not identical to objective reality. “It’s not common sense, it’s not science... it’s a vision, a map, an orienting view of how politics should be constructed,” he explained.​

**The Myth of Neutrality**

If ideologies are inescapable cultural traditions, what happens to the idea of political neutrality? Modern liberal thought often cherishes the notion that we can set aside ideology and agree on policies based purely on facts or universal reason. Blakely challenged this ‘illusion of neutrality’. There is ultimately no view from nowhere in politics, no purely objective, ideology-free standpoint. “I do think that neutrality in the human sciences is a myth,” he said bluntly.​

We often pretend that “the facts are the facts are the facts” and that we're simply being pragmatic or scientific. But whenever we describe human society, Blakely argued, we are always offering an interpretation infused with ethical and ideological commitments. As he put it, “the description of the terrain is never innocent of the vision” behind it.​

Blakely's critique extends to some of the giants of political theory. He took aim at the young John Rawls, famous for imagining [a neutral “veil of ignorance” behind which rational people could agree on principles of justice.](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/) Rawls, Blakely argued, fell prey to the liberal illusion that one could step outside of cultural traditions altogether. Behind Rawls’ veil lurked distinctly liberal intuitions about fairness and equality, not universal truths.​ Though clarifying the older Rawls was far more in line with his thinking.

But does rejecting neutrality mean embracing relativism? Not at all. Blakely argued that we need a different model of political objectivity, one drawn from the humanities rather than the natural sciences. He compared political argument to interpreting literature: reasonable people can debate the meaning of *Hamlet* without descending into chaos, and some interpretations are better than others even though no mathematical proof settles the matter. In politics, as in art, we must argue interpretively, weighing visions of the world against each other.​

“We want to make this fast move… but that fast move is not intellectually honest when it comes to ideologies,” Blakely said. [Real objectivity comes not from pretending we have none, but from carefully comparing interpretations](http://iai.tv/video/between-truth-lies-and-post-truth), probing their strengths and weaknesses, and acknowledging that the conversation may never fully end.​

**Phenomenology and the Lived Meaning of Ideology**

Blakely’s approach is shaped by phenomenology and hermeneutics — philosophical traditions that prioritize the experience of meaning. This, he explained, sets him apart from much of mainstream political philosophy, which tends to focus on abstract logical analysis.​

Analytic philosophers, Blakely noted, often imagine that [if you simply sharpen the concepts and propositions enough, you can clarify political disputes.](http://iai.tv/articles/truth-wont-save-democracy-auid-2954) But ideologies are not mere sets of propositions; they are lived traditions, rich with emotional resonance and ethical significance. “These traditions are far more formidable than mere logical analysis can deal with,” he said.​

\_\_\_

Given the ethical depth of ideologies, how can we ever hope to change someone’s mind?

\_\_\_

In *Lost in Ideology*, Blakely attempts a phenomenology of ideology offering not just arguments but vivid descriptions of what it feels like to inhabit different ideological worlds: nationalism, feminism, socialism, and more. His goal is to show the ethical magnetic pull these worldviews exert, and how deeply they shape our experiences of right and wrong, justice and injustice.​

This depth, Blakely suggested, explains why people rarely change their minds based on logical critique alone. Even if you could refute every point someone made, their attachment to an ideological map would remain. It speaks not just to their intellect, but to their sense of identity, community, and meaning.​

**Critiquing from Within**

Given the ethical depth of ideologies, how can we ever hope to change someone’s mind? For Blakely, the answer lies in immanent critique, arguing from within a person’s own ethical framework, rather than trying to impose an external standard.​

Suppose, he said, you are speaking with an evangelical Christian who believes all people are made in God’s image. Rather than dismissing their views as false consciousness, you might ask how they reconcile that belief with policies that dehumanize the poor or marginalized. In this way, you invite people to reflect critically on tensions within their own value systems.​

\_\_\_

We must create and protect those spaces or risk losing the democratic spirit altogether.

\_\_\_

“You're trying to exhort, inspire, point the person… toward a better vision, on their own terms,” Blakely explained. It’s slower and harder than simply declaring victory, but it respects the ethical seriousness of ideological life and it’s far more likely to succeed.​

One example we might all aspire towards is the work being done by [Matthew Sitman and Sam Adler-Bell on their podcast ‘Know Your Enemy’](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/03/07/arts/know-your-enemy-podcast.html) where they try to take the Right’s intellectual thinkers seriously. But this still could leave us trapped in a technocratic world where we rely to heavily on experts to do this work for us.

**Listening Instead of Technocracy**

One danger Blakely warned against is the temptation of technocracy, the idea that experts, armed with data and scientific methods, can simply manage society without ideological debate. Whether in free-market form (think Silicon Valley libertarians) or in state-socialist form, technocracy replaces politics with administration.​ [Blakely’s arguments hit home when we consider the extent to which so many emergencies are ignored or polarized not by science](https://harpers.org/archive/2023/08/doctors-orders-jason-blakely/) but [by political manipulation or politicians own cowardice towards accountable decision making.](http://iai.tv/articles/how-should-we-judge-politicians-auid-1236)

But the path forward is not straight forward. We cannot just expect to identify the problem of political cowardice and ideological blindness leading us down the road of technocracy. Blakely argued instead for ethnographic listening: paying careful attention to the diverse traditions and meanings people live by. Rather than treating public opinion as a set of data points, we should engage in real conversations about values, stories, and visions of the good life.​

\_\_\_

Philosophy can help us clear away illusions, like the myth of neutrality or the dream of a politics without ideology.

\_\_\_

[In a time when political discourse is often reduced to polls and algorithms,](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/article/2024/jun/23/landslide-polling-companies-election-labour) Blakely’s call for a more humanistic, deliberative politics feels urgent. “Where is the real space for encounter and dialogue about our ethical sources?” he asked. We must create and protect those spaces or risk losing the democratic spirit altogether.​

**A Call for Humility**

Our conversation, like his book, [closed on a note of epistemic humility.](https://hedgehogreview.com/issues/the-varieties-of-travel-experience/articles/ideologies-have-their-reasons) Philosophy can help us clear away illusions, like the myth of neutrality or the dream of a politics without ideology. But it cannot rescue us from the human condition: the need to argue, interpret, and sometimes even struggle over competing visions of the good.​

Blakely acknowledged that dialogue does not always succeed. Fascists, for example, often reject deliberation outright. But even in a divided world, he argued, dialogue remains our best hope. By recognizing that we all carry ideological maps, and by learning to read both our own and others' with honesty and care, we might yet find new paths toward a more humane politics.​

In an age of polarization, Blakely’s work offers a refreshing blend of realism and hope: a reminder that truth in politics is something we must always argue over, interpret anew, and seek together.​
