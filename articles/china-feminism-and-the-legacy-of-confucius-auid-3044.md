# China, feminism and the legacy of Confucius

*The feminist heart of Confucian philosophy*

**By Sor-hoon Tan** | *13th January 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,393 words*

![China, feminism and the legacy of Confucius](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/confucianism-and-feminism-thumbnail.webp)

---

*As the double burden of paid work and family demands leads Chinese women to think twice about marriage and children, we can rush to blame Confucianism’s sexism. But this is a mistake, writes Sor-hoon Tan. We need to separate Confucian philosophy from the oppressive patriarchal norms of its time. Confucian values of care and cultivation can enrich today’s feminism and support equal opportunities for all.*

Is Confucianism a tradition that views women as inferior and subjugates them to men, whether fathers, husbands or even sons? Early twentieth century New Culture movement intellectuals who considered Confucianism antithetical to modern life certainly thought so. He-Yin Zhen (何殷震,1884− ca.1920), who may well deserve the title of [the first Chinese feminist](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-birth-of-chinese-feminism/9780231533263), also placed the blame of Chinese women’s oppression on Confucian teachings. The belief that gender inequality in Chinese society is rooted in Confucianism is practically conventional wisdom except in specialized academic discussions.

Consider a well-known passage from the *Analects* (17.25): “It is only women and petty persons who are difficult to provide for. Drawing them close, they are immodest, and keeping them at a distance, they complain.” This has often been cited to demonstrate Confucian denigration of women. “Petty persons” are ethically deficient in contrast to exemplary persons (*junzi* 君子) in the *Analects*, so the passage appears to question women’s moral character and capacity.

Some commentators suggest that, within its historical context, the passage is comparing “petty persons” not with women in general but with girls, concubines, or women of low birth. Rather than concluding that women are inferior to men by nature, one could argue that the specific observed behavior was the result of women being denied the opportunity of education and personal cultivation; furthermore, such behavior was probably also an expression of their sense of insecurity due to their dependence on men. Thus interpreted, the passage demonstrates the undesirable consequences of discriminating against women, especially in limiting their opportunity to learn and develop themselves, instead of endorsing conventional prejudice against women and justifying gender inequality.

While many feminists both in and outside China continue to be hostile towards Confucianism, there is a growing stream of scholarly and philosophical works that not only defend Confucianism from feminist criticisms but also argue that there are philosophical affinities between feminism and Confucianism which could contribute to feminism in various ways. This has parallels in feminists’ [re-reading of the Western philosophical canon](https://www.psupress.org/books/series/book_SeriesReReading.html). [Chenyang Li](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1527-2001.1994.tb00110.x)’s suggestion, inspired by feminist care ethics, that the primary Confucian virtue of *ren* – usually translated *inter alia* as benevolence, humaneness, authoritative personhood – could be understood as promoting a kind of care ethics sparked a lively debate among Confucian and feminist scholars.

[Sandra Wawrytko](https://doi.org/10.1111/0301-8121.00011) argues that, even if Confucius’s own behavior reflected the sexism of his time, the universal applicability of the Confucian priority on personal cultivation is supportive of feminist projects and there are ideas in the *Analects* that attest to Confucius’ potential as a feminist. [Karyn Lai](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004332119_006)’s proposal of a Confucian epistemology based on reliability as embodied knowledge joins feminist theories in challenging the model of rationality dominating mainstream Western epistemology. [Kevin DeLapp](https://brill.com/display/book/edcoll/9789004332119/B9789004332119_007.xml)’s Confucian role epistemology goes further in employing Confucian understanding of how roles constitute a person’s identity to address criticisms against feminist standpoint epistemology for being either essentialist or arbitrary.

Lisa Rosenlee has just published [*Confucian Feminism*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/confucian-feminism-9781350426184/)*,* which seeks to contribute to transnational feminism by using Confucian concepts, methods, and concerns to address contemporary women’s issues, concerns, and experiences. While the traditional Chinese inner-outer gender segregation limited women’s personal cultivation, Confucian emphasis on education had provided them greater opportunity for education than was available to women in many other traditional societies of the same period. A growing literature showcasing Chinese women’s accomplishments in literature, education, even in government also serves to recover the agency of Chinese women – however severe the oppression of Confucian-era traditions, women were not merely victims.

Reinterpreting Confucian texts and philosophical reconstructions of Confucianism to purge it of sexism and highlighting women’s accomplishments despite oppressive traditional practices associated with Confucianism should not be misunderstood as denying the complicity of Confucianism, as a historical tradition, in the oppression of Chinese women and women in other East Asian societies that had adopted Confucianism as state and social ideology. The issue is not anachronistic judgment, let alone blame, of Confucius and Confucians of earlier times, but how to avoid perpetuating practices that we now judge to be undesirable. As [Vrinda Dalmiya](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6253.2009.01514.x) points out, conceptual possibilities of traditional ideas do not prevent historically grounded oppressions that are usually tied to conventional interpretations of those very ideas. Accommodating feminist concerns at the philosophical level is not enough to meet the feminist challenge to Confucianism. It is also important to understand how oppression resulted from particular concepts or tenets and the ways they were interpreted and put into practice, and how some of them continue to underpin current gender inequalities.

That conventional interpretations of Confucian ideas about gender and women’s role in the family and society remain mired in patriarchal assumptions is all too clear in Chinese mainland [Confucians’ arguments against same-sex marriage](https://doi.org/10.1017/hyp.2023.100). While gender essentialism is not a feature of Chinese thought, the traditional Confucian family ideal is structured by a gendered division of labor that curtails women’s scope of activities and entrenches a stereotypical binary view of gender roles underlying both heterosexism and homophobia. The traditional view of gender roles that assigns the domestic sphere as the woman’s responsibility also fuels the “[double shift](https://thediplomat.com/2023/11/gender-norms-and-womens-double-burden-in-east-asia/)” problem in contemporary China, where women holding full-time jobs nevertheless bear a disproportionately large portion of unpaid domestic and care responsibilities. Such traditional gender role expectations also contribute to various gender inequalities in the labor market.

The inequitable division of unpaid domestic work and [the gap between male and female expectations of marriage and its benefits](https://disruptiveasia.asiasociety.org/gender-inequality-and-the-ultimate-resistance-of-women-in-china) have contributed to the [falling number of marriages and births](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/marriages-china-slump-historic-low-2023-06-11/) in China. Instead of questioning the justice of traditional gender roles, mainland Chinese Confucians who tout the traditional Chinese ideal of the virtuous wife and mother as the primary if not the sole legitimate identity for women (whereas men’s identities are not similarly limited) add to the trend of reviving gender stereotypes that perpetuate gender injustices. Unless the conventional interpretation of the Confucian family ideal based on heteronormative gender roles is successfully countered, Confucianism will continue to be complicit in the oppression of women and sexual minorities.

The oppressive conventional Confucian family ideal can be countered by separating what was in fact historically contingent and should be made obsolete from that which has perennial value in Confucianism. Confucian relational ethics is the cultivation of ethical excellences of humaneness (*ren* 仁), appropriateness (*yi* 义), ritual propriety (*li* 礼), wisdom (*zhi* 知), and trustworthiness (*xin* 信) within human relationships beginning with, but not limited to, the family.Patrilineal descent and inheritance, exogamy and patrilocal residence, and gender segregation of the [inner-outer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucian-gender/#NeiWaiGendDiff) domains among the elite, are contingent features of the social system established in China since the Zhou dynasty (1046−256 BCE). These were incorporated into the ritual system endorsed by Confucians, but they were taken for granted rather than affirmed after critical reflection. The ossification of Confucianism and its degeneration into oppressive practices resulted from rigid adherence to ritual forms and treating the traditional social system as if it had absolute value rather than a historical contingency that might no longer be desirable.

The cultural accomplishments of the Zhou dynasty Confucius appreciated was not the specific aristocratic-patriarchal social system supported by rituals but the function of ritual and kinship ties in securing social harmony and providing a conducive environment for people to cultivate themselves. In retrospect, we could see how that social system actually inhibited the personal cultivation of women and those of lower social status.

As a teacher, Confucius himself did not discriminate among rich and poor, noble and base. It would therefore be consistent with his philosophy to reject a discriminatory social system for a society in which everyone, regardless of gender or class, would have equal opportunity for personal cultivation, which goes beyond formal education to include experience in various domains that could contribute to a person’s physical, emotional, social, and intellectual growth. A feminist account of Confucianism that draws on its core values of personal cultivation and ethical relationships, while discarding historically contingent patriarchal practices, can offer important insights to contemporary discussions of gender equality and social justice. Ancient wisdom can evolve to meet modern moral imperatives.
