# Sartre, Matisse, and the radical openness of reality

*How to see the real through the unreal*

**By Alex Taek-Gwang Lee** | *22nd September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,365 words*

![Sartre, Matisse, and the radical openness of reality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/sartre-matisse-and-the-radical-openness-of-reality.webp)

---

*We tend to think of reality as being just one, singular way, glimpsed more or less accurately through perception. This is a mistake, argues South Korean philosopher and writer **Alex Taek-Gwang Lee**, drawing on Sartre’s philosophy of the imagination. Inspired by the radical innovations of the artist Henri Matisse, Sartre claimed that the imagination’s freedom to play with supposedly fixed metaphysical categories reveals reality’s total openness. There is no single way reality is, and so no rules governing how it must appear in consciousness. Imagination is where that liberating negative truth breaks through.*

What does it mean to see the real through the unreal? This question lies at the centre of Jean-Paul Sartre’s theory of imagination, and it finds one of its most vivid artistic examples in the paintings of Henri Matisse. For Sartre, art is not simply a copy of the world. Nor is it a decorative object placed before the viewer for passive enjoyment. A painting is a material support for an act of consciousness. It enables us to move beyond what is immediately given and to enter the domain of possibility.

This is why Matisse matters for Sartre. In the intellectual landscape of twentieth-century Paris, the presence of Matisse’s *Harmony in Red* (*The Red Studio*) in Sartre’s philosophy takes on profound symbolic significance. The painting’s overwhelming red expanse embodies the radical break with convention that characterized both Fauvism and existentialism. Just as Sartre sought to liberate philosophical thinking from traditional metaphysical assumptions, Matisse liberated color from its subordinate role in pictorial representation. Fauvism, whose name derives from *les fauves*, the “wild beasts,” was therefore more than another artistic movement. It constituted a fundamental reconceptualization of how painting could create meaning. The hierarchy that subordinated color to form was overturned. In Matisse’s hands, color became the primary agent of pictorial organization, capable of creating spatial relationships and emotional resonances through its own internal logic.

This revolution is powerfully demonstrated in Matisse’s [*Window at Collioure*](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/106384-open-window-collioure) (1905), a watershed moment in modern painting. Here, the traditional function of the window as a frame organizing perspective and delimiting space is transformed. Rather than using color to fill in a pre-established architectural structure, Matisse allows chromatic relationships themselves to generate the painting’s spatial organization. The window frame becomes not the boundary between interior and exterior, but one element in a unified field of color relations. The parallel with Sartre is striking. Just as Matisse freed color from its subordination to form and perspective, Sartre sought to liberate human consciousness from [predetermined essences](https://iai.tv/articles/art-essence-and-mortality-auid-2560) and [metaphysical frameworks](https://iai.tv/articles/metaphysics-doesnt-describe-the-world-it-allows-us-to-change-it-auid-3579). In both cases, what had been considered secondary or derivative, i.e., color in painting, existence in philosophy, is revealed as primary and constitutive. The overwhelming red of *The Red Studio* creates a pictorial space that resonates with [Sartre’s insistence on the primacy of existence over essence](https://iai.tv/articles/can-we-reinvent-ourselves-auid-1133).

Traditional Western painting had generally treated color as secondary to drawing and composition, a prejudice that reflected deeper assumptions about form and matter, essence and accident. While Impressionism and Pointillism still remained tied to natural phenomena, the Fauves enacted a more radical break. They transformed color from a tool of representation into a direct vehicle for emotional expression. Their spontaneity was not instinct but “considered immediacy,” an approach that condensed affect into coherent pictorial form. Matisse’s “emotional perspective” captures this perfectly: a way of organizing space not according to optical rules but according to affective relationships.

Sartre’s observation that Matisse’s paintings “remind us of the sense of [the real through the unreal](https://iai.tv/articles/the-power-of-the-unreal-in-scientific-discovery-auid-2030)” represents a transformation of phenomenological thought into aesthetic theory. Husserl’s phenomenology provided Sartre with crucial methodological tools, but Sartre recognized that phenomenology could justify itself fully only if it accounted for [imagination, not as a secondary faculty, but as a fundamental mode of consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real). This concern led to *L’Imagination* and later to *The Imaginary*, where he criticized traditional theories that treated mental images as quasi-objects, diminished copies of perception stored in consciousness. Sartre argued that [this thing-like conception of images misunderstood both consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-not-an-object-we-can-observe-auid-3608) and imaginative experience.

Matisse’s paintings do not merely represent the world as it appears to ordinary perception. They transform it. In works such as *Harmony in Red* and *The Red Studio*, color is no longer subordinate to line, perspective, or naturalistic description. Red does not simply fill a pre-existing space. It becomes the very force that organizes the painting. It creates space, rhythm, atmosphere, and emotion. The room is not presented as a faithful copy of an actual interior. It is offered as an imagined reality, a world made possible through color.

When he suggests that Matisse’s work reveals “the real through the unreal,” he is pointing to a fundamental characteristic of artistic creation. The painting does not simply reproduce reality or express subjective feeling. It creates a new mode of reality, one that reveals aspects of the real precisely through its deliberate departure from representation. Matisse’s radical treatment of color and form does not retreat from reality into pure subjectivity. It operates like a phenomenological reduction in paint, stripping away habitual ways of seeing to reveal essential structures of experience. [Imagination, properly understood, is therefore not opposed to truth](https://iai.tv/articles/imagination-is-not-an-escape-from-reality-auid-3002); it can be one of the privileged means of accessing it.

Sartre’s concept of the *analogon* helps us understand the relationship between painting and imagination. In *The Imaginary*, Sartre rejects the traditional view that images are mental copies stored inside consciousness. An image is not a weaker version of perception. It is not a ghostly object hidden in the mind. Imagination is a distinct mode of consciousness. It does not reproduce the world; it negates the given world and opens another possibility. A painting functions as an *analogon* because it provides consciousness with a material support for imagining. The canvas, pigment, texture, and surface are real.

They belong to the physical world. Yet the object that appears through them is not simply physical. When we look at Matisse’s red interior, we do not merely perceive red paint on canvas. We encounter an irreal object: a room that is not quite a real room, a space that cannot be reduced to ordinary perception. The painting enables consciousness to project itself beyond the given. This is the meaning of Sartre’s claim that art can reveal the real through the unreal. The unreal is not falsehood. It is not illusion in the ordinary sense. Rather, it is a mode through which consciousness surpasses the limits of perception. Art does not abandon reality. It reveals reality otherwise. It makes visible possibilities that ordinary perception leaves hidden.

Sartre’s theory of imagination thus transforms phenomenology from within. Husserl had argued that consciousness is always consciousness of something. Sartre accepts this insight, but he pushes it further. What happens when consciousness is directed towards something that does not exist? What happens when we imagine a fictional character, a mythical creature, or the impossible space of a modernist painting? For Sartre, such acts are not secondary or marginal. They reveal something essential about consciousness itself. To imagine is to negate. It is to say that the world as given is not the whole of reality. Consciousness is not trapped within the actual. It can detach itself from what is present and project what is absent, possible, or impossible. This capacity for negation is the foundation of freedom. Human beings are not simply determined by the world because they can take distance from it. They can imagine it differently.

Matisse’s red interiors provide a visual demonstration of this power. Their intense colors and flattened spaces do not correspond to ordinary physical reality. Yet this departure from realism is not a failure. It is the very condition of their truth. The painting does not show us the world as it is usually seen. It releases us from habitual perception and invites us into another experience of space, feeling, and presence. In this sense, Sartre reverses the old Platonic suspicion of art. Plato feared painting because it seemed to lead us away from truth. Art, for him, was a copy of a copy, an image twice removed from reality. Sartre turns this judgement inside out. What Plato feared was precisely art’s power: its ability to present what does not exist, to disturb the given order, and to open a space for another world. Painting is not merely imitation. It is negation and creation.

This aesthetic theory has consequences far beyond art. Sartre’s philosophy of imagination is inseparable from his theory of [human freedom](https://iai.tv/video/freedom-and-fate). If consciousness can negate the given world, then no situation is [absolutely closed](https://iai.tv/video/hilary-lawson-on-closure). This does not mean that we are free in a simple or naïve sense. Sartre never denies the force of history, class, institutions, bodies, or social conditions. We are always situated. We inherit languages, expectations, limitations, and structures of power. But these conditions do not fully determine us. They become meaningful through the projects by which we live them. Freedom, therefore, is not the absence of constraint. It is the capacity to act within constraint, to interpret one’s situation, and to project oneself beyond what already exists. For this reason, Sartre’s thought became so influential in [moments of political struggle](https://iai.tv/articles/sartre-camus-radicalism-and-solidarity-black-lives-matter-auid-1602), especially in contexts of decolonization, anti-imperialism, and social transformation. His philosophy suggested that thought does not have to begin from abstract universals imposed from above. It can begin from lived experience, from concrete situations, from the urgency of one’s historical position.

Sartre’s model of the intellectual is highly significant. The intellectual, for Sartre, is not someone who stands outside society as a [neutral observer](https://iai.tv/articles/why-the-news-can-never-be-neutral-and-what-we-can-do-about-it-auid-3115). Intellectuals are formed by institutions, class structures, education systems, and ideological forces. They are not innocent. Yet their task is precisely to recognize these conditions and to turn this recognition into critical engagement. The intellectual is contradictory: shaped by society, but capable of criticizing the very structures that shaped them. For Sartre, neutrality is often a disguise for complicity. To think is already to be situated in the world. The question is not whether intellectuals should be engaged, but how they acknowledge their engagement. Philosophy cannot remain a purely academic exercise. It must respond to war, colonialism, capitalism, racism, exploitation, and the struggles of oppressed peoples. Thought becomes meaningful when it risks itself in relation to the world.

Color can become space. A painting can become a philosophy. Consciousness can exceed the real in order to return to it differently.

This does not mean abandoning rigor. On the contrary, Sartre’s engagement shows that philosophical rigor and political commitment need not be opposed. The task is not to replace thought with activism, but to understand thinking itself as a form of action. Concepts are not neutral instruments. They shape how we perceive reality, how we name injustice, and how we imagine transformation. Philosophy matters because it can disclose possibilities that the existing order tries to conceal. Sartre’s critique of psychology and psychoanalysis follows the same logic. He rejects any theory that reduces a human being to a collection of causes, instincts, childhood traumas, or universal mechanisms. A person is not a bundle of psychological elements. A person is a totality. Even the smallest gesture, preference, habit, or mannerism can reveal a whole way of being in the world.

This is the basis of Sartre’s existential psychoanalysis. Unlike traditional psychoanalysis, which often seeks hidden drives or universal explanations, existential psychoanalysis asks how an individual’s actions express a fundamental project of existence. To understand someone is not to classify them under a general law. It is to grasp the singular way in which they make themselves in relation to their situation. Human beings are intelligible, but they are not reducible. Sartre’s criticism of attempts to explain Flaubert’s literary vocation through adolescent psychology is revealing. It is not enough to say that Flaubert became a writer because he had ambition, emotional intensity, or a need for escape. Such explanations merely replace the singularity of a life with abstract categories. They do not explain why this person, in this situation, made literature into the form of his existence. What needs to be understood is the concrete project through which an individual gives shape to a life.

Here again, we return to Matisse. The artist does not merely express an inner state, nor does he simply reproduce an external object. He creates material conditions for a new experience of the world. The painting is not a psychological symptom, nor is it an optical copy. It is an analogon: a support for [imaginative consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/constructing-consciousness-colm-toibin). Through it, both artist and viewer participate in the creation of an irreal object. Matisse’s red, then, is not just red. It is a philosophical event. It suspends the ordinary function of color and reveals its capacity to organize thought, space, and feeling. It shows that perception itself can be reorganized. It teaches us that the visible world is [not fixed once and for all. There are always other arrangements, other relations, other ways of seeing](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-closure-and-the-illusion-of-truth-auid-3399).

Sartre’s reflections on imagination remain urgent today. We live in an age dominated by reductionisms. Human experience is increasingly explained through psychology, neuroscience, computation, economics, and data. We are told that desire is behavior, consciousness is information, intelligence is processing, and freedom is an illusion produced by systems we do not control. Against this, Sartre reminds us that human reality cannot be exhausted by explanation. To be human is not only to be caused. It is to negate, imagine, choose, and transform.

Art matters because it keeps this freedom visible. It gives form to the unreal, not as escape, but as possibility. It allows us to experience the world otherwise. Matisse’s painting does not ask us to flee reality. It asks us to see that reality is not closed. Sartre’s philosophy gives this aesthetic experience its existential force: imagination is not the opposite of truth. It is one of the ways truth becomes open to change. The lesson of Sartre through Matisse is therefore simple but profound. The world is given to us, but it is never merely given. It can be negated, reimagined, and remade. Color can become space. A painting can become a philosophy. Consciousness can exceed the real in order to return to it differently. Freedom begins in this movement: the refusal to accept the given as final, and the courage to imagine what does not yet exist.
