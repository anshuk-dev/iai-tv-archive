# Anger is not a guide to truth or morality

*The hidden pitfalls of a powerful emotional tool*

**By Benjamin Matheson** | *26th November 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,563 words*

![Anger is not a guide to truth or morality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/anger-article-2.webp)

---

*In moments of anger, we often feel certain that our position is right, both morally and factually. Sometimes we even imagine we are right because we are angry. But as philosophers have long noted, anger is as prone to exaggeration, distortion, and self-justification as it is to truth. Philosopher Benjamin Matheson examines how anger can both reveal and obscure moral reality—and why treating anger as reliable, simply because it feels righteous, is a mistake.*

When we’re wronged, we often get angry. If someone cuts you off in traffic, you might angrily swear at them. If a friend betrays you, you might resent them and refuse to treat them with the same affection you once did. If a friend is violently attacked, you might feel indignant at the attackers. If your government is complicit in an atrocity, you might join with others in anger by protesting outside government buildings.

But is such anger ever justified?

It might at first seem strange to ask whether an emotion like anger can be justified. Emotions might just seem like things that happen to us, and not things we do. Emotions, it might seem, are [no different from natural events](https://iai.tv/articles/marcus-aurelius-the-unemotional-stoic-auid-1227) that force us to do things: anger may seem no different from a gust of wind that moves us. Just as we can’t justify moving because of the wind, it might seem we can’t justify feeling angry.

But we also commonly consider people to be insufficiently or excessively angry. If you become so incensed at the person who cut you off that you try to run the other driver off the road, your anger seems excessive. And if you shrug off the violent attack on your friend without feeling angry, you seem to be insufficiently angry. In both cases, your anger is implicitly being evaluated as unjustified.

In the first case, you might well be right that being cut off merits anger, but you are mistaken that it merits so much anger. In other words, you’ve accurately identified that there’s something worthy of being angry—being recklessly cut off—but your anger hasn’t accurately reflected the extent to which being recklessly cut off (at least in this instance) is worthy of anger. To borrow some useful terminology from philosophers [Justin D’Arms and Daniel Jacobson](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2653403), your anger is the correct *shape*,but it doesn’t have the correct *size*.

In the second case, because you do not feel any anger, your feelings about the attack on your friend have both the wrong size (you feel insufficient anger) and the wrong shape (you do not feel anger at all when you have reason to). So, justified anger is at least anger that has the right shape and size.

\_\_\_

We should be cautious about being angry precisely because it can sometimes distort rather than illuminate the world around us.

\_\_\_

There are many cases where anger is justified in this way: we feel angry about [what merits anger](https://iai.tv/articles/the-emotion-double-standard-kirsty-thomason-auid-2686) and we feel angry to the correct extent. This leaves us the impression that anger is a reliable guide to truth: when we feel it, we are accurately identifying the facts of a situation. For example, when we feel angry at the person who cut us off, we have the thought that this person’s actions justify us being angry, and so the person has done something wrong (otherwise our anger wouldn’t be justified). So, our anger seems to point towards facts—in this case, that someone else has done something wrong. At least, that’s how our anger can feel to us.

But cases where anger is excessive suggest that anger may not be reliable after all because it makes us feel justified even when we are not justified. I now want to suggest that we should be cautious about being angry precisely because it can sometimes distort rather than illuminate the world around us and what others have done.

Consider first how your anger at the driver might make you irritable with your friends, family, or co-workers for the rest of the day. So even if your anger is right that the driver wronged you, it can have a hangover that distorts and disrupts the rest of your day. This is perhaps a hangover that can be regulated and managed, so it doesn’t speak against anger itself as being generally unreliable in the sense that it distorts more than illuminates the world around us and what others have done. But it does suggest that anger can make us subsequently mistaken about what others are doing by inclining us towards seeing what others do as slights against us, even if they are just behaving normally.

Let’s now consider how anger itself might sometimes be unreliable. Your anger with your friend for betraying you might also make you see them as a thoroughly bad person. Anger can do so by *narrowing in* on a perceived failure and the person’s offending traits of character, which makes them, in your angry state, seem to be all that the person amounts to, and so makes them seem to be a thoroughly bad person. It is often the case that once we can think of such a person’s other good acts and character traits that our action will lessen and sometimes cease altogether.

This narrowing-in feature of anger gives us reason to be skeptical about the value of anger in personal life. Anger is not really helping us to see the true nature of wrongs and the people who act wrongly. Rather, it is at least sometimes distorting both the significance of those wrongs and whether and the extent to which those who wrong us are bad people. So, anger itself seems to be a bad guide to the world.

Should we give up anger, then? It’s not so obvious. While anger *can* lead us astray – away from truth—it also plays a range of valuable functions. Fans of anger—such as [Audre Lorde](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/wsq/509/) and [David Shoemaker](https://philpapers.org/rec/SHORRO)—often point out that it helps us to respect ourselves, that it calls for others to recognize us as worthy of respect, and that it can help foment social progress through aiding the marginalized and oppressed to stand up for themselves and to fight social ills, such as racism and sexism. It can also [help people come together](https://iai.tv/video/strange-affects-and-collective-emotions) to protest presently occurring injustices.

I don’t think we should give up anger just because it can be dangerous (insofar as it leads away from truth, among other reasons). But I also think we shouldn’t be so optimistic about anger’s value, even though it has a range of positive functions. [As with many emotions](https://iai.tv/articles/the-hidden-logic-behind-emotions-auid-2814), we have to find the right balance. We are all aware of how anxiety can be both useful (for helping us avoid dangers) but also how anxiety can be debilitating (by making us excessively concerned for danger). Anxiety and anger are both tools, but not infallible ones. We cannot rest easy that, just because we feel angry, our anger is getting things right. Anger can take on a life of its own.

This is most notable when our reason for being angry in the first place has been undercut but we immediately replace it with another available reason. Suppose you find out that your friend didn’t actually betray you. Now you have no reason to be angry. You should just return to normal. But this doesn’t always happen. Sometimes we stay angry and we search for a new reason to maintain our anger. We can sometimes engage in what might be called *grievance excavation* by appealing to something else that our friend has done to justify our continuing to be angry. This may even be some slight that we thought we had let go and forgotten. Of course, we don’t always do this; sometimes we just stop being angry when our initial reason for being angry has been undercut. But it is a notable aspect of anger that sometimes it searches for justification to maintain itself.

[We cannot rest easy](https://iai.tv/articles/on-forgiving-and-forgiveness-auid-1059) when we are angry. It primes us and motivates us to stand up for ourselves, to confront those we think have wronged, and perhaps on occasion to act violently in our defence. There are, of course, times when all these are justified and valuable courses of action. But we won’t always be able to tell [simply because we’re feeling angry](https://iai.tv/articles/emotions-run-deeper-than-reason-auid-3049) that we are in one of those cases where our anger is overall justified and valuable, or whether we are in one of those cases where we just think our anger is overall justified and valuable, but it is, in fact, overall dangerous.

Anger is a tool. We have [a responsibility to hone and develop our tendencies to feel it](https://iai.tv/video/the-passion-of-reason). And like any craftsperson, we cannot just assume that now we’ve mastered it, even if it feels like we have. So, while I agree with [Audre Lorde](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/wsq/509/) that “Anger is a source of empowerment,” I disagree with her that “we must not fear” tapping it “for energy”. It is perhaps the energizing quality of anger that makes it so seductive and that can lead us to be over-confident about what it tells us about the world. This is not to say that Lorde’s anger is unjustified. But it is to say that we shouldn’t rely on the feeling of anger itself to guide us towards truth. And just as we should approach our own anger with trepidation, we should be cautious about those who aim to make us angry for the same reasons.
