# Performative listening is the latest form of virtue signalling

*Social media rewards speaking, not listening*

**By Miriam Rasch** | *24th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,326 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Performative listening is the latest form of virtue signalling](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/performative-listening.webp)

---

*The call to listen has become the cheapest virtue in public life. Politicians tour the suburbs, platforms ask what’s on our minds, and content creators stop people in the street for their ‘takes’, yet little follows from any of it. **Miriam Rasch**, philosopher and research coordinator at the Willem de Kooning Academy, argues that this performative listening does active damage: it turns listening into a broadcast, amplifying the views it claims to hear whilst widening the divisions it claims to heal. The solution? Take listening out of the public view. Listening is private and relational, a bond between one listener and one voice that is spoiled the moment it is displayed.*

Tall, thin, steel legs support a dozen rectangular speakers made of concrete, from which undefined noises are heard. You have to enter the circle and bend down close to a loudspeaker to discover different sounds in the rumble: voices speaking an unfamiliar language, ambient noise, a vague pounding.

The latter turns out to be a heartbeat, recorded among the women featured in the art project Oyayi by the Filipino collective Kwago. The recordings were made in a women’s prison. The voices are those of inmates reciting poetry, amid the murmurs of the densely packed crowd. Sometimes the sounds swell into a drumming din. The heartbeats, voices, and prison space are the instruments in a composition that listeners can never fully absorb.

Kwago uses technology to create a positive listening experience. Listening, says the collective, is political. Visitors are invited to turn their ears to voices that are different from their own and that they don’t normally hear. The voices do not tell a clear story or communicate a clear message. Standing within the ring of loudspeakers, you are made part of a place.

\_\_\_

On social media, value is created when people voice their opinions, and it’s multiplied by how loudly they speak. The act of listening is difficult to share or sell.

\_\_\_

Who’s heard and who’s not, asks Kwago? How can you give those unheard space to make themselves heard, in such a way that you don’t speak for them, silencing them again? One should watch out for the phrase “to give a voice to,” as if privileged people—or media, platforms, and algorithms—are being so kind as to allow someone to speak, for which they should then be grateful. Rather, those voices were already there, talking, expressing opinions, and sharing insights, but no one has bothered to notice them.

These questions have been around for a while. “Who speaks? Who listens? And why?” bell hooks asked in 1994, for example. An implicit call to the environment of “new voices,” which, as noted, are not new to and in themselves. A call to the dominant order, though it isn’t always clear who belongs to it and who doesn’t (yet). For this reason alone, every speaker should also be a listener.

\*\*\*

In the circle of loudspeakers, the answer to the question “who listens” is clear: I am. At this moment, I have the privilege not only of making myself heard, but also of leaning toward the concrete box and silently, attentively letting the sounds flow into my ear.

What do I do, what can I do, what are we able to do—those of us who enter this literal circle as well as our figurative social circles? These are ethical questions that go beyond the call for representation. They speak directly to me, as a listening ear. They address the relationship between me and the crowded world out there. Listening immediately establishes a relationship: it builds a bridge between the one listening and what is listened to, whether it’s another human being, nature, or one’s own inner voice.

As with all political and ethical questions today, the question of listening is related to technology. How you position yourself in the world and how it presents itself to you—that is, your relationship with it—is profoundly affected by technology. More specifically, listening relationships are mediated by technology, ranging from sophisticated sound equipment to social media, and technologies understood more broadly, such as conversation techniques or musical improvisation. Technologies can facilitate listening, as in Kwago’s work, or hinder it.

Listening, then, requires not only goodwill but also­ infrastructure, and today’s infrastructure prioritizes other things. It propagates speaking rather than listening. On social media, value is created when people voice their opinions, and it’s multiplied by how loudly they speak. The act of listening is difficult to share or sell. Sound doesn’t always have a visible source and dissipates before you know it. To catch it, it helps to remain quiet and immobile, but in stillness, there is no data. In tech logic, listening gains value only when it conforms to that logic’s conditions, such as visibility, growth, and engagement. In short, listening is valued once it becomes an expression. But in that case, it doesn’t have a lot to do with listening anymore.

Because [technology mediates relationships](https://iai.tv/video/technology-and-freedom) and thus impacts our being in the world, design is a form of applied ethics, argues philosopher Peter-Paul Verbeek. The same can be said about how we use technology. Platforms, networks, and devices establish a certain relationship between humans and the world (just as art installations like Kwago’s do), but so do I. Relationships are never static; something happens between the things that are connected. In the same way, something happens in listening. Composer Pauline Oliveros, who developed the practice of deep listening, has called this the “listening effect”: the act of listening changes something in the world. And that makes you responsible for how you listen.

While technology mediates, I, too, am a medium through which sounds travel, which I try to decipher and then relay. A medium communicates with the dead, with what is absent and perhaps wishes to remain so. Similarly, as a listener, you’re dealing with the spectral quality of sound. Its fleeting and even ghostly nature demands a heightened attention and an openness to that which cannot be represented.

Leaning forward with my body like a giant auricle focused on the voices echoing from the loudspeakers, I do not make them visible or measurable. I enter into a relationship with them. Listening is impossible without a listener.

\*\*\*

When I tell people that I’m researching listening, I don’t primarily refer to the voices of Filipino women prisoners. I often add, even if it’s only part of my explorations: “. . . In times of polarization.” Aha! Of course, everyone understands that listening in polarized times is urgent, difficult, and important.

When politicians promise to listen, as, for example, Hillary Clinton and the Dutch prime minister of a right-wing cabinet, Dick Schoof, have done, it should at least partly be understood in the context of polarization. Public debate has hardened, and supposedly nobody listens to one another anymore, but these politicians will set a good example by going on listening tours in the suburbs or inner cities. Who, then, is the one to listen to? Or, in the case of disappointing election results, who has not been listened to enough?

On the one hand, there are those who proclaim themselves to be “unheard,” however loudly. They are the poles of polarization, or at least one of the poles. The idea is that for too long, discontent has not been listened to, while there are valuable lessons to learn. On the other hand, political listening focuses on “the silent middle,” which deserves extra attention precisely because it’s silent. The poles make so much noise that moderate voices are drowned out.

\_\_\_

Without a basis of radical openness, I think there is no point in listening at all. You listen to hear something you don’t know yet, and that includes where the limits to your listening are.

\_\_\_

Regardless of which group is involved—and the distinction isn’t always clear—I’m afraid much of this listening is performative. It seems to deliver a promise to act on what is said, but one does not follow from the other. In this sense, political listening is, and perhaps should be, noncommittal. After all, you can’t promise to do something before knowing what it is.

Performative listening may be noncommittal; it is also morally charged. It implies that we would all be nicer people if only we listened to one another better, that it would prevent our falling for fake news and getting carried away so easily. And while I don’t believe that listening is such a miracle worker (because miracle workers don’t exist), for me, too, polarization is an important reason to delve into the subject.

The idea of listening as a miracle worker to combat social unraveling has been around for some time. Since the beginning of this century, the media has pointed its microphone at the “common man,” to pick up the popular voice that it supposedly missed before.

One might wonder if that hasn’t helped foster polarization. Giving attention to ideas and opinions allows them to spread further. Moreover, this form of listening is often preceded by suggestive questions that aim to confirm a predetermined narrative. Above all, over time “listening to” has turned into a matter of expression. It zeros in on individuals and their opinions, who are offered the stage but not engaged in conversation. The relationship established is often simplistic and crude, and thus, polarizing.

The line between providing a platform for certain voices and legitimizing or reinforcing them is blurry. Performative listening in politics and media erases the line even further. This form of listening often amounts to broadcasting, such as when an article or program relays the words of the person being “listened to” to the audience. It presents a dilemma for the individual listener. Is there a limit to listening? What if there is little or no “listening back”? Is reciprocity a prerequisite?

I want to know how to escape the dynamics of hardening without becoming indifferent or turning away altogether. And I’m not alone in wanting to know what listening can mean “in [times of polarization](https://iai.tv/articles/why-we-live-in-alternate-political-realities-auid-3239).” Countless books and articles discuss how listening skills can help with heated discussions across the kitchen table, at school, or in the office.

They teach us that rather than responding to an unwelcome opinion with a sneering retort, it’s better to inquire about the reasoning behind it. They advise us not to immediately [come up with counterarguments](https://iai.tv/articles/we-need-to-stop-talking-about-politics-auid-3375) or fact-check another person’s beliefs, not to attach a [moral judgment](https://iai.tv/articles/studies-reveal-the-real-political-divide-is-over-the-nature-of-morality-auid-3462) to a view with which you disagree. This doesn’t just keep things friendly.

In theory, open conversation will prevent antagonisms from deepening. Some authors say it’s necessary to keep listening even if the other person has tumbled down the rabbit hole. [Conspiracy thinking](https://iai.tv/articles/911-and-the-genealogy-of-conspiracy-theories-auid-3700) often stems from real problems or wrongdoings, and listening would help us understand how such radical ideas emerge. There may even be a kernel of truth in what comes across as complete madness.

\*\*\*

“What’s on your mind?” is a Facebook original. A friendly invitation to share your thoughts that has turned into a rampant spectacle of opinions. Musk’s platform asks, “What’s happening?!” It sounds like the smiley face with its tongue out and one eye shut, as if it’s saying, “Suppose nothing is happening; then that must change immediately!”

So what’s on my mind? I have no idea, but I can think of something. What I say out loud, however, is rarely what I’m actually thinking about. What I’m really thinking is difficult to capture in an on-the-spot response. I often think of nothing at all or twenty things at once. While musing on the question, the moment passes. I’m always a bit behind online, and offline as well. Nevertheless, the invitation to share your thoughts or something that has happened hasn’t been unwelcome. We are recognition-seeking creatures, and the demand for our thoughts is flattering. Just open your mouth or your laptop and report the first thing that comes to mind. The likes, and thus recognition, will follow.

But what should you do if you don’t think anything? Do you just say nothing and disperse into “the silent middle”? Do you come back to it later, with your thoughts organized and carefully put into words, only to find that no one is interested in them anymore? Even if someone is, it will probably stay under the algorithms’ radar.

I could never keep up with the constant reflection on breaking news, which was happening at one point especially on Twitter. I first realized this during the attack on the US Capitol in Washington, DC, in January 2021. Commentaries and explanations from experts and “ordinary” people followed one after another in real time. I was speechless as I tried to process what I saw and heard. While I’m often grateful for the insights that others share during such times—though the shouting and grumbling easily drown out sensible reactions—I’ll never understand how this manner of immediate responding works. So I say nothing. Nothing about the news, but also nothing about my latest publication or a book I read, nothing about the pernicious influence of social media on the formation of political opinions. I don’t want to contribute to the commotion; I don’t want to support the revenue model. Nor do I really believe that my opinions matter at times like these. I’m not usually very interested in the opinions of others, so why would they want to hear mine?

The question is whether this holds up. Doesn’t my silence contribute to the problem of “interpassive” indifference? Am I like the bystander who watches someone being robbed? Am I complicit in injustice if I don’t speak out against it? In short, isn’t speaking up a civic duty?

Even if that’s the case, it must go hand in hand with a listening ear. Not in the performative way, like politicians do, nor like it’s taught in leadership courses, which tends to resemble how Simone Weil’s describes her students, who furrow their brows and tense their muscles when they are asked to [pay attention](https://iai.tv/articles/simone-weil-the-attention-economy-and-the-annihilation-of-autonomy-auid-3532). It looks good, but it doesn’t have much to do with listening.
