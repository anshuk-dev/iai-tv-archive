# Why the 10th commandment about jealousy is wrong

*The virtuous side to jealousy*

**By Kristján Kristjánsson** | *27th March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,764 words*

![Why the 10th commandment about jealousy is wrong](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Why-the-10th-commandment-about-jealousy-is-wrong2-.webp)

---

*Since the 10th commandment was inscribed in stone over 3,000 years ago, we have assumed jealousy is always a vice. But according to Kristján Kristjánsson, this clear-cut assumption masks the true complexities of jealousy. We need first to understand the nature of jealousy as an emotion and its relation to other emotions like envy. In this article, Kristjánsson argues that some forms of jealousy can even be considered virtuous and necessary.*

**Negative emotions?**

Psychologists typically divide emotions into “positive” versus “negative,” or more technically framed as “positively or negatively valenced.” This is just a fancy way of saying that emotions feel either pleasant or painful. That said, theorists dating back to Aristotle have pointed out that most, if not all, emotions have mixed valence. In any case, compassion is “negative,” in this sense, by being at least more painful than pleasant, and schadenfreude is “positive” by being at least more pleasant than painful.

Yet when I tell first-year students that compassion is considered a negative emotion and schadenfreude a positive emotion, they balk because this specification clashes with our everyday understanding where the words “negative” and “positive” are used to denote moral evaluation rather than phenomenological valence. Surely, the students will correctly observe, compassion is positive in the moral sense (even a full-blown human virtue) whereas schadenfreude is negative (a human vice).

More commonly, these two senses of negativity versus positivity coincide, such as in the cases of envy and jealousy, which are both negatively valenced (i.e., feel bad overall) and typically considered morally negative (i.e., morally unjustifiable), – even prototypical vices. Common intuitions may not always be right, however, so it is worth subjecting this particular moral intuition to scrutiny. This short article focuses on jealousy, but we need to say something first about the envy-jealousy pair.

**Envy and jealousy as emotions**

An emotion is a complex psychological phenomenon that involves perception, cognition (thought), feeling, and behavioral tendency. For example, when Amy experiences jealousy towards her sibling Betty because Amy considers their father Chris to favor Betty over Amy, Amy *sees* what is happening in a certain way, *harbors a thought* about this being wrong, *feels* physically and/or psychologically bad, and *is inclined* to take some action in response (e.g., complain to the other parent or to a friend). What distinguishes between emotions is predominantly the cognitive component: different negatively valenced emotions may “feel bad” in pretty much the same way and call for similar responses (e.g., expressing grievance).

Envy and jealousy are both *rivalrous* emotions. They involve upward social comparisons in which one construes another person as a rival for some object, person, advantage, or quality. They are also *self-comparative* in the sense of comparing one’s situation unfavorably with that of a perceived rival. Finally, envy and jealousy are called *hostile* emotions. “Hostile” must be understood in a non-moral sense here. Anger – analogously – is a hostile emotion although it may, arguably, often be morally justified. “Hostile” simply means that the emotions involve an overall negative evaluation of the rival’s perceived superiority vis-à-vis oneself, and a desire to reduce the gap to the rival.

This is pretty much where the similarity between envy and jealousy ends, however. Envy thus involves two people and some possession of which one person envies the other (the possession in question can be an object, status, etc.). In contrast, jealousy involves three people: One person is jealous of a rival because of a favor he believes his rival has received – or is set to receive – from a third party. The technical term “favor” stands for anything from material goods to professional opportunities to attention and affection. Othello is jealous of Cassio because of the affection that he believes (incorrectly) Desdemona bestowed upon Cassio. Sometimes the primary focus of the jealousy is the rival; sometimes the person doling out favors.

This is not the only structural difference between envy and jealousy, however. Most emotion theorists consider jealousy to be a “compound” or “composite” emotion, in which a number of less complex emotions have coalesced into an inseparably experienced whole rather than just co-occurring. It is, however, when unpacking the underlying emotions in the proposed jealousy-compound that serious dissonance emerges. My own conceptual view of jealousy is that the relevant compound-making emotions are envy, righteous indignation, and anger. In Othello’s jealousy, then, he is *righteously indignant* that Cassio, rather than himself, is getting the perceived relative favoring from Desdemona. Othello *envies* Cassio and wants to move Desdemona’s attention from Cassio (back) to himself. Moreover, Othello is *angry* at Desdemona for her unfair differential treatment. It may be odd to see envy listed here as one of the emotions underlying the jealousy compound, but the idea would be that the attention the rival is getting creates envy.

Aristotle basically argues that if you make yourself all honey, the flies will devour you, and that you need to retain the capacity to experience the negatively valenced emotion of anger at the proper times in order to retain your integrity and self-respect.

\_\_\_

This is, however, just one conceptualization among many of the putative composite nature of jealousy. Other candidates for the relevant compound emotions range from disappointment, anxiety, and frustration to suspicion, fear, sadness, and embarrassment – and envy is often seen as completely distinct from jealousy rather than as one of its composite simple emotions.

**From analysis to evaluation: is jealousy then a vice?**

As there is much more disagreement about the conceptual contours of jealousy than simple envy, I have no space to delve here into the pros and cons of different specifications and will just stick to my own view (which is shared, more or less, by various other theorists). If jealousy is a complex compound emotion, made up of, say, righteous indignation, envy, and anger, what does that tell us about the question of whether jealousy is always “negative” in a moral sense and even a typical human vice, as is commonly assumed?

For those who would be inclined to swim against the stream and defend the moral credentials of jealousy, the obvious first port of call will be Aristotle’s famous justification of proper anger as a golden-mean state (virtue) between the extremes (flanking vices) of irascibility (excess) and inirascibility or undue meekness (deficiency). Aristotle basically argues that if you make yourself all honey, the flies will devour you, and that you need to retain the capacity to experience the negatively valenced emotion of anger at the proper times in order to retain your integrity and self-respect. Could we argue something similar for jealousy?

We stumble upon a serious obstacle here, however. If jealousy includes righteous indignation (i.e., pain at someone’s undeserved good fortune), then a moral justification of jealousy seems to be ruled out in cases of romantic or sexual jealousy, because for something to be morally justifiable, it first has to be rational; but it is irrational to consider oneself to “deserve” the love of any romantic partner. To be sure, one can be filled with simple anger and simply envy if a spouse breaks her marriage vows and takes off with somebody else, but if these simple emotions are mixed with a sense of righteous indignation to form the compound emotion of jealousy, then that emotion seems, strictly speaking, to be irrational and fail to pass muster as virtuous. To clarify, it is not so much the general emotion of rational indignation that is irrational here but rather the specific instantiation of it as indignation over someone getting the love from my (previous) romantic partner that I deserve more, because romantic love is not something that can be earned and deserved in the strict sense.

To fully understand jealousy, not least its developmental underpinnings in early sibling jealousies, it is important to study its non-romantic forms also.

\_\_\_

Unfortunately, the great majority of studies of jealousy has, until recently, been confined to the romantic or sexual forms (as in the above example of Othello), and some psychologists even seem to use “jealousy” as a synonym of “sexual jealousy.” However, the tide is turning, and recent years have seen more research on sibling and workplace/classroom jealousies. Those fall neatly within the above-suggested structure of jealousy, except that the “favor” in question is not romantic but rather parental attention or the desired non-differential treatment by a parent/boss/teacher; and the rival is not a competitor for romantic love but rather for other forms of attention. Consider, for example, the case of a student who is jealous of classmates because of perceived unfair differential treatment (undue attention, favoritism) by a teacher. To fully understand jealousy, not least its developmental underpinnings in early sibling jealousies, it is important to study its non-romantic forms also.

This broader understanding of jealousy is crucial for tackling the question of whether jealousy is always a vice. For once we begin to think of sibling or workplace/classroom jealousies, we enter much more promising Aristotelian territory. A child deserves as much love and attention from parents as any of her siblings. A student or an employee deserves non-differential treatment from her teacher or boss. If it is really the case that a person has been deprived of a favor that she rightly deserves, she is entitled to feeling bad. More specifically, not being inclined to suffer and utter a moral grievance when a teacher or boss undeservedly favors another person over you – not being inclined to resent this favoring and to remove the favor from other party – is the sign of a lack of assertiveness and self-respect, such a cringing spirit of tolerance even (not to mention a lack of sensitivity to injustice), that it can only be deemed a vice.

Now, ordinary readers may not find this claim startling, but the received wisdom among philosophers and psychologists is still that *all* jealousy includes a claim to exclusivity and “ownership” of another person’s favors and that that any such claim is anti-social and hence morally bad. Hence, the view I am representing here is a heterodox one.

Notice that nothing in my justification entails that jealousy is most often felt in a virtuous way. Indeed, given the prevalence of irrational sexual jealousy and of excessive forms of sibling or friendship jealousies, of which world literature is full, there is every reason to believe that experiences of virtuous jealousy are, by comparison, rare. Even when an experience of jealousy can count as rational, there are often other complicating factors in the situation which should steer a person of harmonious overall virtue away from being jealous. In any case, I must make it abundantly clear that any putative justification of jealousy as morally valuable can only be of appropriate jealousy, constructively expressed.
