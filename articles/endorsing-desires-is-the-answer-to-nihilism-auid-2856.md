# Nietzsche and overcoming nihilism

*Affirming life in the human condition*

**By Alex Silk** | *10th June 2024* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,200 words*

![Nietzsche and overcoming nihilism](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Nietsche-endorsing-desire-to-overcome-nihilism.webp)

---

**Should we embrace nihilism, [as Nolen Gertz suggests](https://iai.tv/video/nihilism-and-the-meaning-of-life-nolen-gertz), or try to overcome it? For Nietzsche, nihilism must be overcome – if we're strong enough. The key, argues Alex Silk, is to see how nihilistic beliefs – that, say, nothing matters – derive from nihilistic feelings and bodily states. Understanding the basic features of human nature and experience at the root of nihilism paves the way toward a healthier, affirming perspective on ourselves and human life. Nietzsche’s rhetorical style helps us incorporate such a perspective by engaging our feelings as well as our rational faculties.**

**“Nihilism”: what?**

There is a popular conception of Nietzsche as nihilist and arch “anti” figure of all things moral. “Conclud[ing] that there is nothing good, nothing beautiful, nothing sublime, nothing evil in itself” (D 210) [1] might seem to invite such an understanding. And yet, in a characteristic Nietzschean twist, the self-described “Antichrist” and “first immoralist” (EH III:2, IV:2–4) is in fact an “*anti-*nihilist” (GM II:24, A 58), whose ideal is a wholehearted “affirmation” and “ultimate, most joyous […] Yes to life” (BGE 56, EH BT:2). What, then, is at issue in the talk of nihilism? Why is it so “danger[ous]” (GM III:14)? How, if at all, can we overcome it? One must tread carefully with an author who aims to “reduce to despair” any reader “who is ‘in a hurry’” (D P:5). We’ll do our best.

First, “nihilism”: what? Consider, to start, a state that might accompany acceptance of the so-called “death of God.” [2] Perhaps Fritz, once a devout Christian, has begun to lose his faith. Whereas he would once weep, “Lord, I come […] Dear savior image for sinners!” [3] at the thought of “God himself sacrific[ing] himself for the guilt of humanity” (GM II:21), now the same thoughts fail to move him. Belief in God no longer seems credible.

Fritz is profoundly disoriented. His interests, projects, pursuits no longer grab him with the same immediacy. How can they have any real meaning if they aren’t grounded in something higher, something independent of his own perspective? “Now that the shabby origin of [his] values is becoming clear, the universe seems to have lost value, seems ‘meaningless’” (WP 7[1887–88]).

The disorientation and uncertainty experienced in cases such as Fritz’s can be acutely painful, indeed a kind of suffering. Yet there are reasons for thinking it isn’t Nietzsche’s ultimate concern under the heading of “nihilism”.

\_\_\_

Rather than nihilistic beliefs that, say, nothing matters or that there are no higher values leading to feelings of weariness, hopelessness, and despair, the direction may be the reverse.

\_\_\_

A prominent theme in Nietzsche’s writings is the physiological origins of various psychological phenomena. Why, now, do existential thoughts of “why?”, “why bother?”, repeatedly spring to mind and persist in one’s attention? Or do nihilistic conclusions suddenly seem so plausible, in a way one can’t seem to shake? Rather than nihilistic beliefs that, say, nothing matters or that there are no higher values leading to feelings of weariness, hopelessness, and despair, the direction may be the reverse. The new nihilistic beliefs, “along with proofs, refutations, and the whole intellectual masquerade are only *symptoms* of a changed taste” (GS 39) – “the expression of a physiological regression” (WP 395[1887–88]). “[Affective nihilism](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-0378.2008.00314.x)” (Gemes 2008) can lead to cognitive nihilism.

**Roots of affective nihilism**

Where, then, does the affective nihilism – the “decline,” “weariness,” “disgust with life” and “[one]self,” and “desire for the ‘end’ ” (GM I:12, III:13; CW P; EH IV:7) – come from? Nietzsche doesn’t treat this “physiological depression” (GM III:17) as basic. The tendency – even historical trajectory – toward affective nihilism has its roots in central features of human nature and experience. [4][[4]](#_ftn4)

Our existence begets a spectrum of negative attitudes that need to be expressed. Pain and suffering are all too common. We inflict pain on others and ourselves; loved ones die; there are pandemics, natural disasters, unjust social structures; we struggle to make sense of ourselves and our place in the world. There are also strong, sometimes aggressive, drives that we have from our biology, shared with other animals. We aren’t always in a position to express these attitudes. Perhaps you can’t stop dwelling on your boss’s asinine emails and whether you’re going to lose your job, your baby’s middle-of-the-night crying is becoming intolerable, you’re about to burst out of your skin, and it’s just you, the baby, and your cat. Circumstances constrain what one can do; and one’s values, internalized social norms, and self-conception will constrain what one treats as an option, something *I* might do (punch a wall? pick a fight? yell at a child? kick a puppy?).

This constellation of factors – pain and suffering, conflicts among our biology and explicit values, and constraints from circumstances, our sociality, and sense of self – threatens to leave our physiological-psychological state deeply unstable. Call this the *human predicament*. One may not even be cognizant of what one is feeling, just a “constantly accumulating” vague underlying “secret pain” or restlessness (GM III:15).

Such attitudes cannot build up indefinitely. Sometimes circumstances cooperate, and some socially sanctioned venue for “erupt[ing]” or “releasing [one’s] emotions” is available (GM I:11, III:15). Perhaps the gym is open and you’re due for kickboxing. Or you live in a raiding culture and it’s raiding season. And if not? Something will need to give.

For instance, one’s *agency*: You might burst and find yourself behaving in a way that doesn’t make sense to you or doing something you think you have no reason to (say, snapping at a friend). [5]

Alternatively, one’s *values* might give: Over time, part of you may come to be able to see yourself as someone who would, say, hit a child or injure themselves. Whereas, formerly, after the fact you might say “it wasn’t me,” it was as if you were “possessed,” perhaps not even remembering doing it, now, at least in the moment, what was once unthinkable no longer is.

Or what might give are one’s *will* and capacity to feel: As a coping mechanism, your will begins to flag. Deprived of expression, your drives weaken, [6] and with it the pain that follows their unsatisfaction. In the extreme, you drift toward an emotional death, feeling nothing, lifelessly wishing for “deep sleep” (GM III:17).

Not all responses to the human predicament are equally healthy. For instance, the Third Essay of Nietzsche’s *On the Genealogy of Morality* aims to awaken people to dangers of a prominent instance of the second type of response – what Nietzsche calls *the ascetic ideal* (roughly, a state of treating some of one’s desires as blameworthy in any circumstance) – and trace its development in Christianity, (non-)religious worldviews promoting “escape” from the world, and modern secular scholarship. Notable here is that each of these targets is also branded “nihilistic.” [7] What unifies them, I suggest, is their failure to address the human predicament in a suitably healthy way.

\_\_\_

The goal is a perspective expressing an unqualified affirmation of life: a “courage and […] excess of strength” for “the highest affirmation, born of fullness, of overfull-ness, a Yes-saying without reservation, even to suffering”

\_\_\_

Categorically denouncing certain of one’s desires rationalizes acts of struggling to overcome those desires, and punishing oneself (emotionally, via guilt or self-loathing, or physically) for having or acting on them. Such acts of self-denial and self-punishment mitigate effects of the human predicament; they afford an outlet, and they exercise one’s agency, strengthening the will. [8] However, the relief is only “for a time,” and it reinforces maladaptive coping strategies, making one “sicker” over time (GM III:17, 21). [9] “The darkening of the sky above humanity has deepened in step with the increase in humanity’s feeling of shame at *being human*” (GM II:7). One fails to come to terms with certain parts of oneself.

To recap, “nihilism,” for Nietzsche, isn’t necessarily a judgment of existential meaninglessness, or a felt state of disorientation, despair following a denial of objective values (though it can be). It is any evaluative perspective that fails to adequately organize one’s “animal” and distinctively human attitudes, and overcome the human predicament.

**Overcoming nihilism: learning to feel, think, and do differently**

Understanding nihilism in this way sheds light on the array of figures deemed nihilistic in Nietzsche’s view. It also illuminates Nietzsche’s constructive project, and something “more important” (GM P:5): *overcoming* nihilism. The goal is to incorporate a perspective on ourselves, pain and suffering, and our place in nature and society which structures our attitudes and channels them to promote psychic unity, health, and human flourishing. [10]  Let’s briefly expand on this.

First, the goal is a particular *evaluative perspective*: an affective standpoint which orients us in the world and directs our focus to certain features of things, which we perceive, judge, etc. Truth is to be sought, not in some “view from nowhere,” but in learning from a plethora of diverse perspectives. [11]

Moreover, the goal is a perspective expressing an unqualified *affirmation of life*: a “courage and […] excess of strength” for “the highest affirmation, born of fullness, of overfull-ness, a Yes-saying without reservation, even to suffering” (EH BT:2). Categorically denouncing certain desires has no place. Neither, however, does [being a “wanton”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2024717) (Frankfurt 1971). Our attitudes conflict; at times some must be prioritized. How to promote regulating one’s desires, even “on occasion banish[ing some] totally from one’s mind,” without conceiving them as “*evil*” (D 76), remains a challenge.

\_\_\_

A vital insight from Nietzsche is the importance of engaging the affects in securing changes in our evaluative perspective. Though we must “learn to think differently,” we must also learn “to feel differently” (D 103).

\_\_\_

Third, the affirmation of life is to be an affirmation of life *as it is*. Our values should be coupled with understanding, in particular of ourselves – our history, biology, psychology, sociality, what is common among humans and where some may differ – lest we again risk the “sickliness” (GM III:13) begetting nihilism. [12]

Overcoming nihilism isn’t guaranteed. Many of the problematic beliefs, values, habits are entrenched by selection pressures shaping core features of our constitution. A vital insight from Nietzsche is the importance of engaging the affects in securing [changes in our evaluative perspective](http://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-ignorance-sets-us-free-auid-2445). Though we must “*learn to think differently*,” we must also learn “*to feel differently*” (D 103).

A critical issue is what resources we might use to help internalize life-affirming meanings and values, and promote healthier patterns of thinking, ways of being. Nietzsche’s rhetorical style may be seen as just such a resource. We are given pictures of “sublimating” basic drives, such as an instinct of cruelty or “will to power”, in pursuing knowledge, developing human excellences, even affirming one’s suffering and overcoming nihilism. [13] Visions of “*amor fati*,” “eternal return,” “the Dionysian” engage the affects. [14] A key role is given to art. [15] How to fill in the details for a particular individual or community in an overall perspective remains a task for “philosophers of the future” (BGE 42, 203) – and perhaps present.
