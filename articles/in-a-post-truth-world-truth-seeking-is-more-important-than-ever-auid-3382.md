# In a post-truth world truth-seeking is more important than ever

*Knowing is a moral act*

**By Jason Baehr** | *8th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,969 words*

![In a post-truth world truth-seeking is more important than ever](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/post-truth-we-need-truth-seeking-image-of-an-eye-taking-in-information.webp)

---

*The age of 'post-truth' has jumped off the philosophical page and come to life. With almost every news story today, there are competing perspectives, with competing truths. Many have simply given up in their quest for knowledge. In such a world, philosopher Jason Baehr argues, knowledge is a survival skill. One that societies must cultivate to sustain trust and democracy in the digital age. While technology has expanded access to information, it has also eroded our ability to think well. The antidote, he suggests, lies not in more data or smarter algorithms, but in cultivating the intellectual virtues that make genuine understanding possible: persistence, rigor, and humility.*

As humans, we care about forming true beliefs and avoiding false beliefs. We want to know, understand, get things right. We want to avoid cognitive error and ignorance. These are our [twin epistemic aims](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/will-to-believe/88D690DCCEAE345A756AB3A915A759FC).

Our success in achieving these aims depends on many factors. Some are systemic and technological. But others are personal. When gathering information, what questions do we ask? How hard do we work to get to the truth? Do we consider alternative perspectives and explanations? Scrutinize the quality of our sources? Attend to the limits of our evidence? These are things we *do* as individual thinkers. They express our [agency, values, attitudes, and commitments](https://academic.oup.com/book/60879). Whether we do them, and do them well, can significantly contribute to the success or failure of our inquiries.

\_\_\_

What our sophisticated information technologies give with one hand they take away with the other.

\_\_\_

One might expect that revolutionary information technologies like the internet or AI would make our lives as epistemic agents much easier. Alas, things are not that simple. While we have unprecedented access to information about innumerable topics, the sheer quantity of information can be overwhelming, which can short-circuit our motivation to inquire. Moreover, the quality of this information is uneven and can be very difficult to evaluate. Making matters worse, the architecture of our information landscape is designed to show us what we want to see and tell us what we already believe. We find ourselves, often without knowing it, in [echo chambers and epistemic bubbles](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/abs/echo-chambers-and-epistemic-bubbles/5D4AC3A808C538E17C50A7C09EC706F0?utm_campaign=shareaholic&utm_medium=copy_link&utm_source=bookmark). What our sophisticated information technologies give with one hand they take away with the other.

This leaves many of us wrestling with questions like: What sources of information can I trust? Am I capable of distinguishing between credible information and [misinformation](https://iai.tv/articles/misinformation-is-the-symptom-not-the-disease-daniel-walliams-auid-2690)? Are objectivity and impartiality hopeless ideals? What is the best—or least problematic—way [to navigate the minefield that is our epistemic landscape](iai-academy/courses/info?course=the-epistemology-of-fake-news)?

One optimistic thought is that we can *outsource* the relevant epistemic labor by simply “trusting the experts.” While [occasionally helpful](https://philpapers.org/rec/MATADY), this suggestion is not as straightforward as it seems. For one thing, it demands distinguishing between actual and purported experts, which can be just as difficult as gauging the credibility of a particular information source or claim. For another, it is not uncommon even for genuine experts to disagree. Accordingly, to achieve our twin epistemic aims, some demanding intellectual work is required. In what follows, I’ll briefly describe two familiar and tempting ways of dealing with this predicament, each of which leaves something to be desired.

\_\_\_

But responsible action is informed action.

\_\_\_

The first is to *surrender and withdraw*. Overwhelmed by the sheer quantity of information, and uncertain about how to approach it, I might elect simply to give up, stick my head in the sand, and suspend judgment about any number of important topics in politics, science, health, technology, economics, and ethics.

This approach is not without benefits. It relieves the anxiety associated with trying to negotiate our [perilous information landscape](https://iai.tv/articles/navigating-reality-in-the-misinformation-age-auid-2849). It also guarantees total success with respect to one of our epistemic aims: if I suspend judgment about political, scientific, or related matters, then by definition I will not end up with any false beliefs about these matters.

Even so, the costs of the surrender and withdraw approach are severe. While guaranteeing success with respect to one of our epistemic aims, it guarantees total failure with respect to the other: if I suspend judgment, then while I won’t end up with any false beliefs, neither will I form any *true* beliefs. This approach also has severe practical consequences. Whether as neighbors or citizens, most of us care about acting responsibly. But responsible action is *informed* action. Should I get this vaccine? How worried should I be about climate change? Should I stop drinking alcohol? Which candidate should I vote for? If I’ve given up trying to get to the truth about these or other high-stakes issues, my capacity for responsible decision-making will be limited.

A second approach is less passive. It involves a policy of *selective exposure*. While not withdrawing altogether, I might elect to go with the algorithmic flow, paying attention to sources that mirror back to me what I already believe and avoiding sources that could provoke doubt or cognitive dissonance. Content with my tribe’s one-sided perspective, I might hunker down in my echo chamber.

\_\_\_

Insulation from counterevidence and competing perspectives can make us hyperconfident.

\_\_\_

Here too, there are benefits. This approach spares us the difficulty of having to reckon with conflicting perspectives or adjudicate disagreements between putative experts. And provided our tribe isn’t wrong about everything, it allows that we’ll end up with at least some true beliefs.

Yet the downsides of the selective exposure approach aren’t difficult to identify. Insulation from counterevidence and competing perspectives can make us hyperconfident. And because no particular tribe or epistemic bubble has a monopoly on truth, this approach is bound to limit progress relative to both of our epistemic aims: many truths will remain beyond our ken, and we’ll be ill-equipped to identify inaccuracies in what we already believe.

What might a better way of handling our epistemic predicament look like? I’ll propose an answer that draws from the field of “[virtue epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-virtue/),” which is an approach to the philosophical study of knowledge and knowing that foregrounds a concern with “intellectual virtues.” Intellectual virtues, as many virtue epistemologists think of them, are the [personal qualities or character attributes of good thinkers and inquirers](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-excellent-mind-9780190096267?cc=us&lang=en&), such as curiosity, open-mindedness, intellectual humility, intellectual courage, and intellectual perseverance. They are, as [one philosopher](https://philpapers.org/rec/MONEVA) has put it, the character attributes that “a truth-desiring person should want to have.”

To see how these qualities might bear on our question, consider how the approaches sketched above go awry. The first approach involves *premature surrender*. Whether due to fear, laziness, or some other factor, it involves giving up too quickly on one’s attempts to reach the truth. The second approach, while not commending outright surrender, is *insufficiently critical* and *dangerously insular*. Its practitioners are too accepting of familiar ideas and are cut off from potential counterevidence and alternative points of view.

This suggests an important insight: viz. that to [successfully navigate our epistemic environment](https://iai.tv/articles/the-perils-of-knowledge-in-a-pandemic-auid-1392), we must *persist* in our efforts to get to the truth (vs. surrender). And once we are intellectually engaged, we must proceed in a way that is simultaneously *intellectually* *rigorous* (vs. uncritical) and *open* *to alternative perspectives* (vs. insular).

These principles underscore the importance of three clusters of intellectual virtues. The first consists of virtues that compel us to *engage and persist* in our efforts to get to the truth: e.g. intellectual autonomy, courage, and perseverance. Autonomy helps us form our own thoughts and judgments. Courage provides the strength and confidence to take intellectual risks. And perseverance prevents us from prematurely surrendering in the face of intellectual challenges and obstacles. The second cluster consists of virtues that ensure an appropriate *demandingness* to our inquiries, such as intellectual carefulness, thoroughness, and rigor. Carefulness attunes us to and helps us avoid potential mistakes. Thoroughness equips us to probe for understanding, seek explanations, and make connections between ideas. And rigor compels us to employ high logical standards in our assessment of our own and others’ ideas. The third cluster comprises virtues that help prevent our thinking from becoming *overly narrow or rigid*, including intellectual humility, open-mindedness, and curiosity. Humility makes us aware of and willing to own our intellectual mistakes and challenges. Open-mindedness disposes us to consider and give a fair hearing to alternative perspectives. And curiosity keeps us wondering and asking thoughtful questions.

My suggestion is that we treat the kinds of virtues just described as [*regulative ideals*](https://academic.oup.com/book/32898/chapter-abstract/276568825). More specifically, I propose that a better understanding of what these virtues amount to—of their defining features, conditions for application, and mutual supportiveness—[can yield helpful and concrete guidance](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003083108-4/democracy-information-technology-virtue-epistemology-jason-baehr) when it comes to navigating our precarious and polluted information environment. The relevant virtue concepts are richly personal and attractively normative ways of fleshing out what might be involved with persisting, demonstrating sufficient rigor, and remaining appropriately open in this context. By better understanding their nature and demands, we can better understand how to conduct our inquiries. And by implementing this guidance, we can significantly improve our epistemic lot.

Of course, a fuller explanation and defense of this proposal would require much more than can be conveyed in a brief essay. How exactly would the proposed guidance work? Is it [sufficiently concrete or action-guiding](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/abs/educating-for-intellectual-virtue-a-critique-from-action-guidance/DABD504216E9F0E4210AC8BFDE5C7A9F)? On any given occasion, how are we to decide between the competing demands of specific virtues, such as courage and caution or humility and autonomy? These are important questions. While I cannot take them up here, they have [not been altogether neglected](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/action-guidance-and-educating-for-intellectual-virtue-a-response-to-kotzee-carter-and-siegel/2E49BC403C645D444FF2672E16BC3435) [by virtue epistemologists](https://academic.oup.com/book/60879/chapter-abstract/530453804?redirectedFrom=fulltext).

Nor is this proposal meant to be a comprehensive solution for our epistemic woes. Without a doubt, [social and institutional measures](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/670860/summary) [are called for as well](https://social-epistemology.com/2017/07/24/the-bad-news-about-fake-news-neil-levy/). Policy makers, media companies, regulatory agencies, journalists, and others must also contribute to improving the quality of our information environment. Nonetheless, our individual values, attitudes, and choices [still have a role to play](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003083108-4/democracy-information-technology-virtue-epistemology-jason-baehr) in how we navigate this complex terrain, especially in its highly imperfect state. And, again, my suggestion is that we can sharpen our understanding of this role via reflection on virtues of the mind.

At present, these virtues appear to be in relatively short supply. Nor is the very idea of intellectual virtues familiar to most people. Thus, for the suggested remedy to be implemented, a significant amount of education and training would be in order. Where or how might such training occur?

The final point I’d like to make is that our *institutions of formal education* are particularly well-suited to undertake this kind of intellectual formation. Intellectual virtues are central to academic learning. They are the personal qualities or character attributes needed for the successful pursuit and transmission of knowledge. Given the academic focus of most schools, a concern with fostering attributes like curiosity, open-mindedness, and intellectual courage [can be a very natural fit](https://www.routledge.com/Intellectual-Virtues-and-Education-Essays-in-Applied-Virtue-Epistemology/Baehr/p/book/9781138098695?srsltid=AfmBOooW_4GOs74hoUloPm5EMaRzvOSGFAxTFMZ6XRwArCGG_Yr7Ts6z).

Indeed, many educators and educational institutions would describe themselves as already fostering these qualities. As [John Dewey noted](https://search.worldcat.org/title/603884):

[I]t is [education’s] business to cultivate deep seated and effective habits of discriminating tested beliefs from mere assertions, guesses, and opinions; to develop a lively, sincere, and open-minded preference for conclusions that are properly grounded … No matter how much an individual knows … if he has not attitudes and habits of this sort, he is not intellectually educated.

While the best schools and teachers surely do help students make progress in these “deep seated and effective habits,” in my experience, it is the rare academic administrator or teacher who can wax eloquent about what exactly they involve or how their institutions or classrooms are designed to foster their development. Thus, a question worth pondering is: What would it look like for academic institutions and instructors to train students in disciplinary knowledge and skills but in ways that are deliberately and systematically designed to support students’ cultivation of intellectual virtues?

If we were to [take this question seriously](https://hep.gse.harvard.edu/9781682536704/deep-in-thought/), we’d be in a better position to help a wide swath of the population responsibly and competently navigate our fraught epistemic landscape. We’d also be equipping them to engage in the kind of thinking and reasoning that are a *sine qua non* of morally and politically responsible action.
