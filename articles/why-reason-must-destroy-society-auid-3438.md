# Why reason must destroy society

*The inherent chaos of modernity*

**By Steve Fuller** | *5th December 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,860 words*

![Why reason must destroy society](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Steve-fuller-rationality-destroys-society.webp)

---

*Many have tried to ground social order in reason. But according to **Steve Fuller**, such attempts are bound to fail. Reason is inherently anti-social, Fuller argues, because it treats society’s institutions—whether they be a political regime or scientific paradigm—as temporary arrangements. Subjected to the critical eye of reason, the world as it is holds value only as a means to some higher end. As such, reason is not a vehicle for stability, but a catalyst for permanent revolution.*

Why is it that whenever highly rational beings are placed in charge of anything, there are likely to be disruptive social consequences?  The single-minded scientist, the efficiency expert, and the superintelligent machine stand out as exemplars familiar from both fact and fiction. Nowadays, it’s easy to reduce the problem to one of lacking “people skills,” which in the case of humans might be diagnosed along the autism spectrum. However, I believe that the problem lies in the logic of reason itself.

The logic of reason is ultimately about the materialization of an idea (if you think in Plato’s artistic terms) or the realization of a potential (if you think in Aristotle’s organic terms). In both cases, it consists of two parts: the specification of an end and the specification of means to the end. Kant’s [*Critique of Judgement*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/48433)did justice to both. And it provides the framework within which modern ideas of progress make sense. However, we can already see how reason might be anti-social, insofar as reason treats the social order not as an end in itself but as a means for achieving other ends.

When the sociologist Max Weber first introduced this approach to reason, he was quick to observe that pre-modern societies did not think like this at all. They accepted the naturalness of certain norms and expectations, simply based on their sheer survival over time. He called such societies “traditional.” Moreover, he deemed some of them “irrational,” if the natives could not justify their activities beyond appealing to tradition.

Behind Weber’s distinction between the “rational” and “irrational” lies a profound difference in how people relate to their society. In irrational societies, people are received and recognized in terms of prescribed roles, which all the members understand and accept if they are to remain. In rational societies, by contrast, people regard themselves as co-creators of the social order and hence society’s practices need to be justified to them in terms of promoting the interests of its members, both now and in the future.

For Weber and most of the founders of sociology, the signature moment came when the law no longer obliged individuals to occupy their parents’ social position but rather allowed for a more transactional sense of personal identity. Thus, I can be different things to different people under a mutually agreed arrangement. How one becomes a spouse and a worker thus became different things legally. The logic of exchange reflected in this setting was the basis for social contract theory, which remains the cornerstone of both political thought and legal education in liberal societies. The details of the contract underwrite the rationality of the arrangement.

\_\_\_

Both sides share the idea that the people who create and maintain the institutions take precedent over the institutions they have created and maintained.

\_\_\_

So far so good—except that once the contract is in writing, the possibilities for its violation are laid bare. Pre-modern concepts like “trust” and “virtue” may be wishfully introduced to ensure that the contract is not read so strictly that it becomes virtually unenforceable. Whenever such appeals work, it is often because people either can’t read too well or don’t read too carefully—or perhaps don’t want to read at all. Modern polities have found a workaround strategy in the form of periodic displays of public accountability, such as elections.

However, if people are regularly invited—or perhaps even forced—to decide on whether the rulers have delivered on their promises to the polity, there is always an opportunity to radically change course. Consider the societal sea change that resulted from the election of, say, Adolf Hitler or even Donald Trump. The secret to their success partly involved dividing the people against their polity’s established institutions, as in Trump’s repeated reference to the US federal capital as “the swamp” and “the deep state.” Some democratically elected radicals have gone much further and succeeded in replacing their nation’s constitution, the ultimate shredding of the social contract. Neither Hitler nor Trump went so far.

The larger point here is that to think about society “rationally” is to think about its current institutional framework as simply a means to whatever ends the members of society, understood either individually or collectively, might wish to pursue. To be sure, all the disputes between capitalism and socialism turn on this last distinction. However, both sides share the idea that the people who create and maintain the institutions take precedent over the institutions they have created and maintained. If a society is to be judged by other than its current members, they should live in the future, not the past.

Putting the point this way suggests [the revolutionary potential of rational thought](https://iai.tv/articles/science-is-rebellion-auid-455). Weber himself thought that science and politics in the modern era are characterized by complementary forms of rationality.

\_\_\_

The Popperian impulse to subject scientific theories to endless stress tests is not so different from the perpetual political task of purging the rogue elements from the vanguard party who hold back the realization of utopia.

\_\_\_

Science is about following the path of inquiry wherever it may lead. Weber stressed the disciplined nature of science, which draws inspiration from monastic rules for the direction of body and mind. Its secular descendant is the experimental method, as practiced by both Bacon and Descartes. In this context, truth is to be found by staying the course regardless of the consequences. In fact, Weber believed that science buries its inquirers, since the price of the principled pursuit truth is endless error along the way. Interestingly, he originally made this statement to graduate students in a lecture called “Science as a Vocation.”

Turning to politics, truth is replaced by utopia. Unlike the pursuit of truth, in which the goal itself is unclear and perhaps even unattainable, the pursuit of utopia is presented by modern politicians as already clear and well within our reach if we try hard enough to achieve it. To be sure, its full realization may take several generations. Nevertheless, the task is not about understanding what we are seeking but removing the obstacles to reaching it. The political journey also ends up burying its travellers, this time sometimes literally. Moreover, like science, politics sees the world before our eyes as not simply all that there is, but a horizon of possibilities waiting to be realized.

Although Weber did not fully share [Hegel’s grandiose vision of reason unfolding in history](https://iai.tv/articles/hegel-vs-marx-ideas-change-the-world-not-economics-auid-3244), he certainly presumed that rationality requires an expanded and transcendent sense of human agency in the world. Taken to its extreme, it can result in the world as it is no longer being meaningful, except as a means to some higher end. From that standpoint, any social order—be it a scientific paradigm or a political regime—is just a temporary arrangement.

Consider the origin of the Marxist phrase “permanent revolution,” which as Eric Voegelin observed, was coined by the liberal publicist Charles—not the positivist Auguste—Comte, who was reflecting on the failure of the 1789 French Revolution. Comte invoked the spirit of trial-and-error, as in “If at first you don’t succeed, try, try again.” Karl Popper adopted this sense of “permanent revolution” to distinguish his own position from [Thomas Kuhn’s more conservative view](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/kuhn-vs-popper/9780231134286/) of scientific revolutions.

\_\_\_

Kuhn made good on Max Planck’s claim that science only advances one funeral at a time.

\_\_\_

Whereas Kuhn held that a scientific paradigm’s tradition of successful problem-solving should not be easily abandoned, simply because some long-standing problems remain, Popper saw that attitude as fetishizing history, which he notoriously dubbed “historicism.” Instead, Kuhn’s policy of epistemic tolerance should be replaced by a more concerted effort to root out error, a process Popper dubbed “falsification.” Indeed, the error may ultimately lie in the paradigm’s foundations. But does this purge of error eventually result in truth?

The Popperian impulse to subject scientific theories to endless stress tests is not so different from the perpetual political task of purging the rogue elements from the vanguard party who hold back the realization of utopia. The one can end up in scepticism, the other nihilism. Popper himself finessed the similarity by arguing that science can do in its simulated world what politics can’t do in the real world. Thus, death to theories should come more easily than death to people.

Kuhn’s paradigm-centred account of science confused the two situations by reducing the fate of a scientific theory to the lives of its adherents (and vice versa), which made it very difficult for scientists to justify changing their minds. Equally, though this was not in Popper’s sights, Kuhn’s vision makes it hard for a defunct theory to be resurrected by later scientists, now equipped with better evidence, reasoning and larger societal support. Taken together, Kuhn made good on Max Planck’s claim that science only advances one funeral at a time.

Two interesting yet neglected works, very different in spirit, that capture the antisocial—and anti-Kuhnian—character of reason that I’m defending here are Gyorgy Lukacs’ 1954 book, [*The Destruction of Reason*](https://www.versobooks.com/en-gb/products/2695-the-destruction-of-reason?srsltid=AfmBOoqh5vmRSBBJfri2F_paL2_pIxNmYb2VY9gWoCpaXqPHDfeJogKk), and Ernest Gellner’s 1988 book, [*Plough, Sword and Book*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/P/bo3644789.html). Both were written by spiritual *Mitteleuropäer*, the former a socialist tilting to Moscow, the latter a liberal tilting to London.

Lukacs’ mammoth opus is a critical history of nineteenth and early twentieth century philosophy. The “destruction of reason” refers to the widespread bourgeois cultural reaction to the inexorable march of Marxism, Lukacs’ gloss on Hegel’s world-historic trajectory of reason. Intriguingly, the many philosophies he condemns as “irrational” are very much with us today, including both continental and analytic philosophy. All are charged with fetishizing whatever passes for human experience as the foundation of knowledge rather than as merely a means to the sort of genuine knowledge that Marxism promises to deliver, as humans further engage with each other as equals productively in the world.

Gellner, a follower of Popper, projects a rather different world-historic horizon, which on the surface seems inimical to Lukacs’ Marxism. Gellner’s sweeping philosophical anthropology associates the advance of reason with the detachment of truth as a goal from the means used to maintain social authority. This involved the establishment of alternative forms of accountability in society—in effect, a new form of social power. The printing press—[and the corresponding spread of literacy](https://iai.tv/articles/truth-is-dead-and-thats-a-good-thing-auid-3354)—epitomized this development for Gellner, resulting in the sort of relatively reversible, contract-based social order to which Weber drew attention.

Beyond their differences in rhetoric and emphasis, Lukacs’ socialism and Gellner’s liberalism are agreed in accepting reason’s mainly destructive character in the face of an established social order. Lukacs, always the dialectician, embraces this prospect enthusiastically because he envisages various counter-revolutionary forces trying to impede if not outright destroy reason itself. In contrast, Gellner, rather like Weber, is more ambivalent about the prospect of reason adequately replacing all that it leaves in its path of destruction. And so, in the end, we are like Walter Benjamin, left staring at Paul Klee’s *Angelus Novus*.
