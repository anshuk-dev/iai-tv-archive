# The state can read your mind, we must act now to stop it

*Your mind is in your pocket, and it isn't protected*

**By Simon McCarthy-Jones** | *2nd July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,486 words*

![The state can read your mind, we must act now to stop it](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-state-can-read-your-mind-we-must-act-now-to-stop-it.webp)

*In 2025, the UK's Technology Secretary was forced to hand over his conversations with ChatGPT. Most of us would feel unease at giving up our search histories, our notes, our late-night messages—yet the law treats all of it as data the state can demand, not thought it must leave alone. Psychologist and neuroscientist **Simon McCarthy-Jones** argues this rests on a mistake. We still imagine thinking happens sealed inside the skull, but we now think on our phones and through the machines we talk to. Some of what sits in our pockets is part of how we reason, remember and work things out, and it deserves the same protection as the thoughts in our heads. The law already treats freedom of thought as absolute. It should extend that protection beyond the skull: when what we type is genuinely exploratory and unfinished, the state should be no more able to read it than to reach inside our minds.*

The law protects your body. It protects your property. It protects your speech. But does it protect something arguably more fundamental: your thoughts? This seems like a dry legal question that can be safely left to lawyers. But it isn’t, and it can’t. Law, claimed the legal philosopher H.L.A. Hart, is too important to be left to lawyers. Hart’s observation is particularly true when it comes to safeguarding thought, due to its centrality to human dignity, autonomy and the functioning of democratic societies.

The problem is that the law has a distorted image of thought. Its picture of thought is encapsulated in Rodin’s famous statue, *The Thinker*. Here, thought occurs inside a solitary figure, invisibly gestating beneath furrowed brows. This internalist conception of thought is intuitive and comes to us early. It is already [grasped by four-year-olds](https://doi.org/10.2307/1166124), who understand there is a thing called thinking, distinct from talking, which happens inside people’s heads.

\_\_\_

A society that punishes thinking in public will not produce better thinkers. It will only produce better liars, cowards and conformists.

\_\_\_

Yet, as philosophers and cognitive scientists [have long argued](https://academic.oup.com/book/6746), thinking also occurs *outside* our heads. It happens in notebooks, in marginalia, and under the crossed-out sentence; in online search queries, diagrams, and unsent emails; in voice notes, arguments, and in conversations in therapy rooms, classrooms, and on generative artificial intelligence (AI) platforms. We don’t always think and then express our thoughts. The expression can be the thinking.

Socrates is a better guide to thought than Rodin. Socrates viewed thinking as something that happens in dialogue, through questioning and answering. Following Socrates, the [philosopher Agnes Callard](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/321013/open-socrates-by-callard-agnes/9780141994833) calls the idea that thinking is something you can do by yourself a “solipsistic illusion.” Thinking, she argues, is something that happens through conversation, rather than being the activity of a “special being, the mind, that lives inside us.”

The law’s conception of thought is impoverished because historically it has drawn a neat line between thought and speech, then focused on protecting the latter. Discussions of free speech fill up volumes of legal cases and journals, whereas free thought is rarely mentioned. Such neglect made some sense when our thoughts appeared inaccessible. However, with the advent of neuro-technologies and the use of machine learning to infer what is going on inside people’s heads from the data they leave online, the situation changed. Our minds became leaky.

This prompted a [revival of interest in the right to freedom of thought](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009539616). But these discussions will be too narrow if they only focus on brain scans, neural implants and hypothetical futuristic mind-reading machines. The greatest threat to free thought may come not from technological overreach but conceptual underreach; treating everything outside the skull as speech, data or property, when it sometimes functions as part of our thinking.

[Philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3328150) famously argued that “cognitive processes ain’t (all) in the head.” According to them, if a process in the external world would be recognized as thought if it happened inside our head, then it should count as thought.

In this view, using pen and paper to perform calculations constitutes thinking, even though it happens outside our heads. For someone with dementia, a notebook can function as their memory. When we write things down, such as we might do in a diary, this may count as thinking, rather than merely an expression of pre-existing thought. As [Peter Carruthers has put it](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511583360), in some cases “the thinking is the writing.”

Today, many such activities are performed on our phones. Our phone has become a device for thinking: a memory prosthesis, a sketchpad, a space for reflection, a partner in deliberation, a confessional. As a result, we carry fragments of our minds in our pocket: notes, drafts, searches, reminders, maps, conversations with others, conversations with machines.

Legal recognition of this fact matters because the rights typically invoked to protect the content of our phones, such as speech and privacy, are qualified rights. They can be overridden due to competing governmental concerns, such as national security or public order. Freedom of thought, by contrast, is deemed by many legal systems to be an absolute right that cannot be violated for any reason. So, if parts of your phone are deemed to be your thoughts, then the state will no more be able to rummage through them than it could rummage through your brain.

It is a tricky question as to whether, when thinking extends beyond the head, absolute protection travels with it. I think it should. But with the recognition that clearly not everything we say or search for online, for example, should count as thought. We need criteria for what subset of these activities should count as thought. Here we may consider whether the activity is exploratory and dialogic rather than declarative; whether it is deliberative, uncertain, self-questioning, truth-seeking; and whether the external object or exchange is playing a functional role in memory, reasoning, imagination or self-understanding. This approach would be a step towards ironclad protection for the workshops of thought.

AI introduces further complications. When we interact with generative AI tools, we may be thinking with and through the machine. Conversations with AI can take the form of deliberations, involving tentative, exploratory, and sometimes stupid questions and positions. These verbal explorations should be seen as thinking and be as private as the thoughts within our head. We may need what [legal scholar Marc Blitz terms](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-the-law-of-algorithms/artificial-minds-in-first-amendment-borderlands/C60AF9211B5E72991DB73D6BD2D5155B) “a right to think with technology.”

The state is already trying to peer into your thinking with AI. In 2025, the British Government’s Technology Secretary [was forced to reveal his conversations with ChatGPT](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2025/mar/13/peter-kyle-technology-secretary-freedom-of-information-chatgpt). But if the law deems some of our AI interactions to comprise thought, then the state would be prevented from ever accessing such thinking.

Although machines are becoming thinking partners, flesh and blood humans have served this role for millennia. Yet such public thought formation is increasingly difficult thanks to cancel culture. A society that punishes thinking in public will not produce better thinkers. It will only produce better liars, cowards and conformists. [Bertrand Russell saw this a century ago](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/44932/44932-h/44932-h.htm), noting that “thought is not free if the profession of certain opinions makes it impossible to earn a living.” Public thought needs the strong protection of the right to freedom of thought.

Yet it is not simply other people who are a threat to our thinking. We can be an enemy to our own thought. [Philosopher Keith Stanovich argues](https://doi.org/10.1080/13546783.2018.1459314) that evolution has shaped us into “cognitive misers” who are encouraged to think using mental shortcuts rather than slow and deliberate chains of reasoning. As Daniel Kahneman is reputed to have said, thinking is to humans as swimming is to cats; both can do it, neither likes it very much.

Cognitive miserliness will tempt many people, particularly those with a low “[need for cognition](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.42.1.116),” to outsource their thinking to AI. Why go to the effort of thinking, sit with uncertainty, or cultivate judgment, when a silicon slave can almost effortlessly and instantly answer? Protecting thought hence also means creating conditions in which thinking is practiced, valued and rewarding.

That said, a right to freedom of thought should also include a right not to think. Otherwise, we risk a Rousseauan project of forcing people to be free, whose outcome would be similar to mandatory swimming lessons for cats.

None of the above implies that the law must necessarily import wholesale a conception of the extended mind into its jurisprudence. Law is quite entitled to [come up with its own definition of thought](https://doi.org/10.1093/hrlr/ngac028), which need not map onto that developed by philosophy or mind sciences. After all, the law must do the tricky job of balancing competing interests.

But the legal conception of thought cannot drift entirely unanchored from the actual nature of thought. If the right to freedom of thought is to do what it says on the tin, it must protect the actual practices by which twenty-first-century humans reason, reflect, focus, doubt, imagine, revise and understand. The first step in this process is to stop picturing thought as a solitary figure on a plinth.
