# Emotions are rational

*Reason and emotion are not enemies*

**By Daniel Vanello** | *22nd January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,160 words*

![Emotions are rational](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/emotions-are-rational.webp)

---

*Emotion is often portrayed as a threat to rational thought. **Daniel Vanello** argues the opposite: that emotions are themselves rational, shaped by our values and open to justification or criticism. If this is right, emotional development should not focus on control and management, but on learning how to reason with what we feel.*

Emotional development is central to a person’s wellbeing. Just think how important it is that we make and sustain attachments with others, both within the family and in society through friendships and romantic relationships. It would not be possible to do so without a balanced relationship with one’s emotions. Think of the person who is always too jealous and cannot preserve a romantic relationship. Or the person who is always angry at their parents and does not allow themselves to enjoy parental love.

It is not a surprise then that emotional development is part of the [United Nations’ 4th Sustainable Development Goal](https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/education/), a list of aims needed to better human life on this planet. It is also part of the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) [early childhood development goal](https://www.who.int/europe/activities/improving-early-childhood-development-(ecd)), which aims to give every child the opportunity to reach their full potential and flourish. Empirical evidence supports these commitments. Healthy emotional development is reliably correlated to [future academic success](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15427609.2012.729907), [prosocial behaviour](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0885200612000713?via%3Dihub) and [building friendships](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/best-start-in-life-a-research-review-for-early-years/best-start-in-life-part-2-the-3-prime-areas-of-learning). It is not a coincidence, then, that, more locally, the British government included emotional development as a key learning goal in its [early years national curriculum](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/early-years-foundation-stage-framework--2) and that its Department for Education (DfE) included it in its [annual priority areas of research interest](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/department-for-education-areas-of-research-interest).

And yet, there is a persistent and fundamental problem with how children’s emotional development is understood in educational policy. It is seen merely as an instrument to facilitate children’s ability to focus and complete tasks given to them by the educational system. The problem is not that the educational system wants children to learn how to complete tasks and manage their emotions in doing so. This is undoubtedly important. Rather, the problem is that this picture of emotional development leaves out its most important aspect: rational agency. Emotions are more than psychological states to be managed; [they are shaped by what we value](https://iai.tv/articles/the-divide-between-belief-and-emotion-is-an-illusion-auid-3396) and can be assessed as rational or irrational depending on whether the things that provoke them are rational or not.

The picture we get of emotional development is one where emotions need to be managed in order for children to carry out their task-solving functions appropriately.This picture ignores the most powerful aspect of emotions: they are rational.

\_\_\_

Understanding emotional development as underpinned by this rational capacity transforms how we should approach it in education. Right now, though, we overlook it. A paradigmatic example of this is the early years curriculum in Britain. Early years teachers are asked to ensure that children can demonstrate appropriate skills [in three areas](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/best-start-in-life-a-research-review-for-early-years/best-start-in-life-part-2-the-3-prime-areas-of-learning): self-regulation, managing self, and building relationships. The curriculum focuses on two psychological constructs to identify the appropriate skills for sustaining these three areas: emotional competence and executive functions.

Emotional competence is a psychological construct devised by American psychologist Carol E. Izard to design the Head Start program in the United States. The Head Start program originally aimed at improving the learning success of children from disadvantaged backgrounds by targeting their emotional development in the early years. Emotional competence is composed of two further related psychological constructs: emotional knowledge and self-regulation. Emotional knowledge refers to the ability of labeling facial expressions with a correct basic emotion, e.g. happy, sad, angry; and the ability to give a causal explanation of a basic emotional episode, e.g. the child is angry because that other child took her toy. Self-regulation refers to the ability to control and manage one’s emotions and their overt expressions.

Both emotional knowledge and self-regulation are seen as important within the British early years curriculum mainly because of their role in a child’s ability to complete a task. This is even more evident when it comes to executive functions. As [neuroscientist Adele Diamond states](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750): “Executive functions… refer to a family of top-down mental processes needed when you have to concentrate and pay attention, when going on automatic or relying on instinct or intuition would be ill-advised, insufficient, or impossible.” So the picture we get of emotional development is one where [emotions need to be managed](https://iai.tv/video/better-left-unsaid) in order for children to carry out their task-solving functions appropriately.

This picture ignores the most powerful aspect of emotions: they are rational. How so? After all, aren’t we taught from an early age that emotions and rationality are in conflict? Don’t emotions cloud our judgments? Yes and no. To understand how we can answer in both ways, we need to understand in what sense emotions are rational, and why this matters for education. To do this, we need to turn to the philosophy of emotions and education.

Let me begin with the example of anger. Suppose you are on the bus and you feel someone pushing you from behind. You suddenly feel enraged. Why on earth would anyone push you? Don’t they know it’s dangerous to push someone on a moving vehicle? You then turn around and see that you were accidentally pushed by a mother trying to hold onto the pole while holding a baby. Your rage disappears and, after helping the mother in need, you feel a little guilty that you felt enraged. Of course, you didn’t know what the circumstances were, and you stopped feeling anger as soon as you found out. All good. You continue with your day.

This example is sufficient to explain the rationality of emotions. First, notice that your anger can be either justified or unjustified. Had you been deliberately pushed by a rude person, you would have had reason to be angry. In this case, your anger would have been justified. As it happens, you were accidentally pushed by a mother protecting her baby. This means you didn’t have a reason to be angry, and your initial anger was unjustified. The fact that anger can be either justified or unjustified means that anger is open to rational assessment. Contrast this with visual experience. It would make no sense for me to ask you to justify why you are seeing typed words while reading this article. The only kind of explanation it would make sense for you to give is a causal one: you are seeing typed words because the light is reflecting from the surface of the screen into the retina of your eyes, which send signals to your brain, and convert this into an image. Visual experience, then, is not open to rational assessment like emotions.

\_\_\_

 Emotional development is not primarily a matter of managing one’s emotions. It is primarily a matter of learning how to respond to the reasons out there in the world.

\_\_\_

We can generalize the lesson of anger to all emotions. Fear can be [justified or unjustified](https://iai.tv/articles/the-emotion-double-standard-kirsty-thomason-auid-2686) depending on whether the thing one is afraid of is fearsome or not. Shame can be justified or unjustified depending on whether the thing one is ashamed of is shameful or not. And so on. But we can go further. The rationality of emotions is inextricably connected to the fact that our emotions involve an evaluation of the situation we are responding to. Suppose that, in fact, you were deliberately pushed by a rude person. In that case, you have reason to be angry because the push was, among other things, offensive. So, in feeling angry, you are evaluating the push as offensive. Generalising to other emotions, emotions involve an evaluation of the situation one is responding to emotionally, and it is in virtue of this evaluation that one has a reason justifying one’s emotion. In correctly evaluating the deliberate push of a rude person as offensive, you have reason to be angry: your anger is justified by the offensive nature of the push that your emotion correctly picked up on. We value safety on public transport; it is rational to value our safety, and it is therefore rational to be angry if that safety is violated.

We can now see why the same emotion can either cloud our judgement or not, and, in so doing, be either in conflict or in harmony with reason. If the emotion wrongly evaluates a situation, for example by evaluating a mother accidentally pushing you as offensive, then it is unjustified, and it is clouding your judgment. But if the emotion correctly evaluates a situation, for example by feeling compassionate toward the mother in need, then it is justified. In this case, not only does it not cloud your judgment, but it is grounding your ability to make the correct judgment that the mother is in need of help. It is in virtue of feeling compassion that you make the correct judgment and act accordingly.

The upshot is that our emotions are tied to the way we value things. We feel angry at a friend’s betrayal because we value friendship, and betrayal is a violation of that value. We feel ashamed of having lied at a job interview to earn an advantage because we value honesty, and lying is a violation of that. We feel intense joy in hearing our child mumbling words for the first time because we value our children. And all of this falls under our rational agency. We have reason to value friendship, honesty and our children. Our emotions are a response to these reasons.

When it comes to emotional development, then, we need to emphasize its rationality. Emotional development is not primarily a matter of managing one’s emotions. It is primarily a matter of learning how to respond to the reasons out there in the world that can either justify or criticize our emotional responses. In turn, this involves learning how to evaluate things, how to give them the right importance. It is only once we view emotional development as the rational process that it is that we can properly ask questions such as: how do children learn how to feel the right emotion? How can we teach them? And what should an educational system that recognizes the emergence of rationality in children’s emotional development look like in practice?

A first step is to teach children that emotions are rational and that they should change in accordance with reasons. One of the great injustices done to children is that no one teaches them to think about their emotions as responding to reasons, and yet they are chastised with questions like “Why did you get angry?” when they didn’t have a reason to do so. We should also teach them that the reasons they provide when explaining their emotions are connected to the importance they give to the things that provoke those emotions, and that sometimes those things don’t have that importance. Inevitably, a child’s emotional development will lead to questions about values. That is, the value that people see in things and whether those things are indeed valuable. We shouldn’t shy away from discussing these issues with children.

This might all seem too intellectual. But it isn’t. The suggestion is not that we sit them down and get them through classes on the theory of reasons. The suggestion is that, when embedded in everyday contexts, we teach them how to reflect on emotions they actually felt in response to things they actually think are important. Children are [very receptive to these kinds of conversations](https://srcd.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2006.00960.x). Moreover, this doesn’t mean that we can simply choose how to feel. The point is that the things we respond to emotionally either do or don’t have a certain importance, and our emotional development should involve our learning how to correctly evaluate these things. So whether we feel a given emotion or not should depend on whether we correctly evaluate whatever we are responding to. We don’t typically *choose* to feel genuine joy when seeing friends. Rather, it is because friendship is valuable that we suddenly feel joy in seeing friends.

Educational policymakers worry about self-regulation and executive functions that can help children pay attention to problem-solving tasks within the educational system. But this would be taken care of if we taught children that emotions respond to reasons and that there is reason to do well in school. If children respond differently, then it is either because they don’t see any reason to do well in school or, more troublingly, because children haven’t been taught how to recognize reasons and change their emotions accordingly.

Emotions are at the heart of children’s flourishing. Healthy emotional development determines in great part whether a person lives well, providing one with the ability to sustain attachments, like family, friends and romantic partners, with the ability to do well in school and in the job market, and with the ability to think about how to value things and assign them importance. This is because emotions are a constitutive part of our rationality. In education, as in other parts of our lives, we should treat them as such.
