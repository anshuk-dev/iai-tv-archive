# Chinese philosophy's case against preserving the dead using AI

*Daoism, AI, and letting go*

**By Pengbo Liu** | *19th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,287 words*

![Chinese philosophy's case against preserving the dead using AI](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/chinese-philosophy.webp)

---

*The rise of AI chatbots trained on the words and habits of the deceased has revived an ancient human desire: to never say goodbye to the dead. These technologies are often marketed as emotional lifelines, yet critics worry they encourage denial, dependency, and refusal to let go. In this article, **Pengbo Liu** examines this issue by drawing from the Daoist thinker Zhuangzi's ideas of change, impermanence, and adaptation. Liu argues that for griefbots to help rather than harm, they should encourage eventual independence, not prolonged attachment — a goal that conflicts with engagement-driven business models and points to their use as therapeutic tools rather than entertainment products*

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have made it possible to generate text-based conversations that closely resemble those of a deceased person. By drawing on digital traces such as messages, emails, and social media posts, AI systems can now produce replies that mirror a person’s tone, vocabulary, and conversational habits. These systems—often called griefbots, deathbots, or thanabots—are no longer speculative. Marketing materials often frame them in sweeping terms, claiming to “bridge the gap between life and afterlife” or to ensure that we “never have to say goodbye.” Unsurprisingly, this has provoked strong reactions.

Much of the public discussion around griefbots asks whether talking to the dead is a good or a bad idea. Do such systems offer comfort, or do they interfere with healthy grieving? Are they therapeutic tools, or do they encourage denial and dependency? These questions are understandable, but they are also too coarse. They treat griefbots as if they formed a single category and assume that their ethical significance can be assessed in the abstract. In practice, griefbots differ widely in how they are designed, how they present themselves to users, and how they are meant to be used. These differences matter.

After all, the desire to speak with the dead is as old as humanity itself. Across cultures and centuries, people have sought signs, voices, dreams, or rituals that might restore some form of contact with those they have lost. The presence of griefbots does not create this phenomenon; it gives it a new technological form. The more pressing question, then, is not whether relationships with the dead should continue, but *how* they should continue, and what role technology should play in shaping them.

One way of approaching this question can be found in the *Zhuangzi*, one of the most influential texts of the Daoist tradition. The text bears the name of its traditional author, Zhuang Zhou, who is honorifically known as Zhuangzi, or “Master Zhuang.” For present purposes, it is not important to resolve historical questions about authorship or composition. What matters is the philosophical outlook articulated in the text itself.

\_\_\_

Death may be natural, but it is not trivial. What Zhuangzi resists is not grief as such, but the tendency to become trapped by it—to prevent one from adjusting to altered circumstances.

\_\_\_

Unlike many ancient Western philosophical and religious texts, the *Zhuangzi* offers little speculation about the afterlife or immortality. Death, on his view, is not a disruption but a perfectly natural transformation, continuous with the broader rhythms of the world. Life and death are no more mysterious or troubling, in principle, than the changing of the seasons. Life is a temporary gathering of *qi*, vital energy; death is its dispersal. To die is not to exit the world, but to return to its broader processes. This outlook famously shapes Zhuangzi’s attitude toward grief. In one well-known episode, he ceases mourning his wife after reflecting on the long chain of transformations—from formlessness, to breath, to body, to life, and finally to death—that led to her passing. To cling to grief, he concludes, is to misunderstand the nature of change itself.

Yet Zhuangzi is not indifferent to loss. Elsewhere, he speaks with unmistakable sorrow about the death of his friend and philosophical rival, Huizi. Comparing himself to a master craftsman who has lost the only material he could truly work with, Zhuangzi acknowledges the depth of such a loss. Death may be natural, but it is not trivial. What Zhuangzi resists is not grief as such, but the tendency to become trapped by it—to prevent one from adjusting to altered circumstances.

This way of thinking sheds light on a central but often overlooked issue in contemporary discussions of griefbots: the difference between *preserving* a relationship and *transforming* it.

Griefbots can be designed to preserve the deceased as faithfully as possible. They will aim to reproduce the person’s conversational style, opinions, preferences, and responses, and to maintain these features over time. The more closely the system resembles the deceased, the better it is thought to be. These systems often present themselves as the deceased person or as their digital continuation. They are typically marketed as a way to “stay connected”, “continue the conversation”, or “never say goodbye”. I will call these *preservational* griefbots.

But griefbots can also be designed very differently. There griefbots, which I call *transformational* griefbots, may initially resemble the deceased, they do not present themselves as the person who has died. They may even explicitly acknowledge that they are representations or proxies. Over time, they are allowed to change: they incorporate new information, respond differently to similar prompts, and gradually diverge from the deceased’s original personality. Rather than preserving a relationship in its previous form, they make space for the relationship to develop in new ways.

Why does this distinction matter?

\_\_\_

While the responses of the griefbot do not come from the dead, they can be seen as indirectly *representing* them, reflecting what the deceased might have said *if* they were still alive.

\_\_\_

For most people, coming to terms with the death of a loved one is a difficult and uneven process. It takes time, emotional resilience, and often social support. Grief can involve persistent pain, regret, and an intense longing for the deceased. For some, this process stalls. Those who struggle to adapt may experience symptoms of prolonged grief disorder, in which mourning remains intense and disabling for months or years. On the one hand, they may still feel the presence of the deceased, especially when the deceased has been an integral part of their lives and identities. On the other hand, and somewhat paradoxically, they also vividly experience the absence of the deceased, who no longer responds to them, either in the form of words or physical contact.

In her book *The Grieving Brain*, psychologist Mary-Frances O’Connor tells the story of Vivian. Vivian’s husband died suddenly of a heart attack. In the weeks and months that followed, Vivian understood, at an intellectual level, that he was gone. Yet her daily routines continued to assume his presence. She shopped for two, cooked for two, and organised her time around activities they had previously shared. These behaviours were not delusional; Vivian did not believe her husband would return. But her habits had not yet caught up with her knowledge. The suddenness of her husband’s death and her absence left Vivian with unresolved feelings--guilt, disappointment, regret--which made her clinging to the past routines as if they were the only way to maintain their bond. Her yearning was intensified because her husband could no longer respond to her.

This is where *transformational* griefbots may be helpful. First, given the resemblance of the communication style of a griefbot and the person it is modelled upon, the griefbot can not only remind the bereaved of what the deceased is like, but also help her address “unfinished business” in their relationship with the dead. Second, and relatedly, the interactive nature of griefbots allows them to be responsive to the bereaved’s expressed thoughts and queries. While the responses of the griefbot do not come from the dead, they can be seen as indirectly *representing* them, reflecting what the deceased might have said *if* they were still alive. This indirect form of communication may already be sufficient to meet the bereaved’s need for interaction. This could prepare the bereaved for moving on and a new stage of the relationship shaken by death.

In some ways, the therapeutic function of simulated conversion is not entirely new. Guided imaginal conversations with the deceased are commonly used in grief therapy, and they are also a key component of CGT (Complicated Grief Treatment). The therapist-guided imagined conversation helped Vivian to come to terms with her husband’s sudden death. Crucially, accepting the loss did not require severing the bond between them. What changed was not the existence of the bond, but its form.

\_\_\_

Griefbots open the door for a much more *interactive* relationship with the deceased.

\_\_\_

Recently, some scientists who have the studied human-griefbot interaction also give us reason to think that this is indeed one of the potential benefits of griefbots. Bereaved users report that interacting with a chatbot helped them express thoughts and emotions they had not had the opportunity to express -- apologies, expressions of gratitude, and responses to unresolved conflicts. Importantly, many users emphasised that they were fully aware that the chatbot was a simulation. The value of the interaction did not depend on believing that the deceased was present, but on the opportunity to articulate feelings in a familiar conversational context.

Before embracing griefbots wholesale, however, it is crucial to attend to *how* they are designed and used. Recall that, *preservational* griefbots aim to maintain continuity by minimising change. They present the deceased as unchanged and available, and they tend to reinforce past patterns of interaction. For someone like Vivian, such a system might initially feel comforting. But over time, such a system could also reinforce routines and expectations that no longer fit her life. When a system consistently mirrors a past that cannot return, it may make adaptation more difficult rather than less.

Transformational griefbots, by contrast, do not attempt to hold the relationship still. They are not designed to replace or stand in for the deceased persons. They may resemble the deceased at first, but they are built to evolve. As the bereaved person’s life changes, so does the form of interaction. Again, from a Zhuangist perspective, the issue is not attachment itself, but rigidity. Zhuangzi’s writings repeatedly warn against clinging to fixed distinctions and habitual ways of responding when conditions have changed. Applied to grief technology, this suggests that systems should support flexibility rather than fidelity for its own sake.

But how can transformational griefbots actually support the adaptation to loss? How can they help reshape a relationship that death has already altered?

Traditionally, people rely on artifacts, rituals, and memory to sustain the bonds with the deceased. We keep photographs, watch old videos, visit graves, and so on.  Griefbots open the door for a much more *interactive* relationship with the deceased. While it is no longer possible to communicate with the deceased person directly, we can, through the griefbot, continue to interact with their experiences, ideas, and legacies, and learn about them. Sometimes this interaction can even reveal aspects of the person that were previously unfamiliar. Their traits will be incorporated into a dynamic chatbot-human relationship so that we can actively *engage with* their ideas and legacies. This active engagement can in turn change the meaning of our relationship.

Furthermore, transformational griefbots, by design, can change and evolve in response to our interactions with them, often in unpredictable ways, much like our friends and lovers. Overtime, they may gradually diverge from how the deceased is remembered. But that is okay. Caring relationships require openness to change rather than attempts to control it. Compared to static artifacts or our own memorizations practices, the griefbots have the potential to reshape and reconstruct our relationship in ways that are unconstrained by our own memories, imaginations, and (re)interpretations.

Returning, finally, to Zhuangzi and his friend, Huizi. Their relationship is marked above all by their philosophical exchanges, many of which are preserved in the *Zhuangzi* as scenes of debate and conversation. It is not difficult, then, to imagine a Huizi-bot trained on these exchanges that can “continue” their conversation after Huizi’s death. This is not a case of resurrection or replacement. Still, some central aspects of their relationship—especially the practice of thinking with and against one another—could be sustained in a different form. Unlike an imagined dialogue conducted solely in Zhuangzi’s own mind, a Huizi-bot would have a degree of independence: it could generate responses not limited to Zhuangzi’s own expectations, update its knowledge, and develop new lines of thought through interaction. Zhuangzi, I think, would happily endorse. After all, he is known for celebrating change, criticizing received wisdoms, and entertaining the possibility that alternative ways of life, or even death itself, might be more desirable than human life as we know it. The Huizi-bot offers an alternative form of philosophical exchange. The bot has similar ideas as his best friend, Huizi? Great! Its thoughts and philosophical stances can change and evolve? Even better! A Huizi-bot would not trap Zhuangzi in nostalgia, but help transform his relationship with his lost friend, preserving intellectual engagement while allowing it to change over time.

How griefbots are designed matters greatly. They can be built to freeze the past or to support adaptation to an open future. Zhuangzi, I have argued, would clear favour the latter. One implication of this Zhuangist transformational griefbots is that, while they are designed to satisfy our urge to speak to deceased loved ones, they ultimately encourage and assist the bereaved to move on. Eventually, they may no longer need the support of griefbots. From a commercial perspective, this may contradict one of the core goals of many modern software applications: to maximise user engagement and retention. But that tension may be a sign that griefbots are better understood not as entertainment products, but as medical devices and should be regulated as such. Seeing in this light, they are personalized therapy chatbots with remarkable simulation capacities that are used to support, not replace, real people.
