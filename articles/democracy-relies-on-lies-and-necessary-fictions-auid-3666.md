# Democracy relies on lies and necessary fictions

*Plato on our political myths*

**By Catherine Rowett** | *31st August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,557 words*

![Democracy relies on lies and necessary fictions](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/democracy-relies-on-lies-and-necessary-fictions.webp)

---

***Catherine Rowett**, British academic and former Member of the European Parliament, argues democracy survives on lies. Drawing parallels between Plato's* Republic*, where a noble lie binds society together, Rowett traces this same fiction through modern democratic myths: equal votes and equal capability. Against Popper's warning that such lies pave the road to totalitarianism, Rowett reimagines the necessary fabrications of society as not only promoting order but equality.*

Plato’s philosopher kings and queens, the only ones fit to rule in the ideal state sketched in the *Republic*, are lovers of truth. They alone of all the citizens know and love, and don’t just pretend to know and love, what is good and fine and just. How, then, could *philosopher rulers* resort to lies? Why would they even need to do so? This is one of the great questions that arises among readers of Plato’s most famous work.

In the wake of the twentieth century’s uncomfortable encounters with totalitarianism, Plato’s “ideal state” earned a reputation as a blueprint for the worst kind of political oppression. Not least among the worries was the strange inclusion of the so-called “Noble Lie” as a crucial tool for governing the state. The Noble Lie is a state-promulgated myth about each citizen’s birth and origins. It is to be universally believed by the citizens. It forms (according to the Socrates character, who has the job of describing this mythical community) a crucial tool for establishing the citizens’ absolute loyalty to the state and to each other.

If loyalty and cohesion are what the rulers want to achieve, why would they not just tell the truth? If something is expedient, why not just explain it, and inspire the people to choose it for what it is? It’s a puzzle, especially as Socrates insists that the philosopher rulers are “lovers of truth”.

It did not seem much of a puzzle to Karl Popper, writing in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* in 1945. For Popper, Plato was the thinker whose malevolent spell inspired the worst examples of twentieth-century dictatorships. The *Republic*, he suggests, recommends handing totalitarian control to a privileged group, and keeping the proletariat oppressed by means of lies and class structures. Generations of students have doubtless been asked to vilify Plato’s *Republic* along these lines, and to find there a right-wing totalitarian class-based society, in which workers and public servants are manipulated into ceding absolute power to an oligarchy of philosophers, by means of a blatant lie designed to deceive them into accepting that their position in society is fixed by birth.

Certainly there are puzzles about Plato’s broader claims about lying for political expediency. However, one thing in his “Noble Lie” passage immediately gives us space for doubting the story told thus far. Socrates is emphatic that the rulers themselves must be the first to believe their myth: *first* them and the soldiers, and *later* the rest of the city. This is not manipulation. It’s more like a foundational ideology. It guides the rulers’ decisions because it forms part of *their own beliefs* about what matters and why.

Does our democratic liberal society depend on any founding myths like that? Is it something to be afraid of? Let’s look more closely at [what Plato’s myth is doing](https://iai.tv/articles/chinas-take-on-plato-and-aristotle-shadi-bartsch-auid-2710) and then turn to our own.

The story that goes under the name of the Noble Lie falls into two parts. The second part is sometimes called the “Myth of the Metals.” The first part of the story is an example of what we call an autochthony myth: that is, it claims that the earth itself (or the land of our birth) is the mother to all the citizens, the same mother for all, so that all belong to the land and all will care for the land of their birth as their mother(land). This part of the myth is designed to deliver a collective sense of family-belonging, so that citizens will care for each other as siblings and courageously defend their motherland when she is threatened.

In accordance with this myth, the rulers are just as earthborn as their companions. Indeed, the removal of family loyalties delivers a kind of leveling effect. No one is more important or less important by birth. This is not a myth that marks out one group as privileged or of higher birth than another. No one is born an aristocrat. No one is born a slave.

The second part of the story adds some differences that will mark out different people as fitted for different roles in society. Citizens will grow up believing that they received their education while they were in their prenatal gestation under the earth, and that during that period some god wove copper, silver or gold threads into their souls so that by the time they came to birth, equipped, educated and ready for action, their appropriate destiny was written into their souls. They emerged from this process equipped and prepared for a life of productivity, military achievement or philosophical enquiry respectively, according to whether their soul was threaded with copper, silver or gold.

This myth repays a bit of close attention. First, like other autochthony myths in the Greek world, the imaginary “birth” from the earth delivers *adult, fully grown citizens*, not infants. Far too often, this myth has been read as if it required the rulers to examine newborn infants and put them into classes from infancy. In a way, it is about the opposite: it is about giving absolute equality of opportunity to every newborn infant, and allowing them to develop natural talents with no preconceptions about what those talents are likely to be. A comprehensive education for all citizens up to a certain age is evidently implied (though philosophers will then go on to higher education in maths and sciences for many more years after that).

Second, the young adult who is “born” from this education process recalls their nurture and education as a process of gestation, in an underground womb. The womb of the earth here can be usefully compared to Plato’s famous cave image: a cave in which we are captivated by half-truths and media distortions, but from which we must escape if we are to see reality as it really is. Similarly, the child is educated on myths and stories from childhood, but emerges from the womb of the earth in a kind of birthing into the light when the education is complete.

It is during this communal education that children grow to become who they will be. That process of growing up in the state nursery and schooling is the period when “the god wove gold, silver or copper into their souls.” The myth implies that by the time they graduate from high school, their fitness for a suitable role in society has become apparent (doubtless through a mixture of aptitude and choices). This is when they are assigned to serve with the military, to join one of the productive professions, or to proceed to further training for philosophy.

Combined with the leveling role of the first stage of the myth, the key message from this “myth of the metals” is that people will show differences of aptitude by the end of their schooling—but their right to become a ruler or to join any other profession is not determined by a difference in inherited privilege. This represents a huge change in the ideology that would be normal in the ancient world, in which a cobbler’s son would have little option but to become an apprentice cobbler, and the expensively educated son of a prominent politician or businessman would be well-placed to stand for powerful public office. It’s not hard to see that our own society suffers from remnants of that traditional ideology, despite the myths we tell ourselves.

So the first part of the myth, about Mother Earth, ensured that all are deliberately ranked as children of the same parentage. This is precisely to prevent the authorities from judging them based on the status of their genetic parents. Instead, the decision about how they will contribute to society, going forward, must be taken at the end of their education, on the basis of what “metal” they have in their soul—which is code for what they turn out to be good at when they come of age.

There are two good reasons why the rulers themselves need to believe these myths first. It’s the rulers who are to oversee putting the young people into appropriate roles. They must not be tempted to make false judgements based on whether their parents were aristocrats or military leaders. The rulers, more than anyone, must be completely convinced that you cannot judge anyone by who their (natural birth) parents were. For sure, these young people must have been conceived by a father and mother, and born in the normal way. But by the time they emerge from their “gestation” in mother earth, that must be completely forgotten. Indeed, denied.

And second, the rulers above all must be settled in their “universal brotherhood” mentality. They must see every young person as a sibling to care about, to love, as much as any other. The leveling of Mother Earth, and the unleveling of the metals woven mysteriously into the growing soul: both are absolutely vital to the founding ideology. Together, they deliver equal opportunities without regard to family privilege, together with a meritocratic outcome that reflects actual natural abilities and achievements, not the effects of social privilege or favouritism. Unquestioning commitment to these beliefs is especially important for the rulers.

So this noble lie is the community’s shared ideology. It expresses the community’s commitment to eliminating inherited privilege, instilling collective belonging, and promoting equal opportunity to deliver [meritocratic division of labour](https://iai.tv/articles/if-we-lose-meritocracy-we-risk-liberal-democracy-auid-3041). Socrates is quite right to say that the rulers themselves must be the first to believe it. The success of their utopian project depends on it. No one must question whether it is scientifically true; one should not test it to see if any correlation exists between children’s aptitudes and their birth parents. No: it’s better not to know, because such a correlation, if there were any, must remain irrelevant. The only thing that matters to society is the actual aptitude of the child, once they’ve been given space to explore their interests and develop their potential. A foundational myth seems an ideal way to engineer this outcome.

And notice that it is irrelevant whether it is empirically true or false: one must believe it and never check, even if it is true—even if it is in fact no lie at all but only a fictional presentation of the truth.

Should we be outraged by this recognition that fair treatment of the young, and the best outcome for society, depends upon commitment to an ideology? I doubt it. I think we have similar ideologies that are essential for us to live by, in our own world. We need them and will always need them to ensure equality of opportunity, among other things. Eliminating prejudice about the physical or cognitive abilities of people of different races or genders depends not upon undertaking empirical studies to see if they are indeed equal, or might differ in some respects, but rather upon not doing that, but instead insisting that such studies are irrelevant for deciding who gets a chance to prove themselves. [Democracy depends on a similar myth](https://iai.tv/articles/democracy-and-liberalism-are-incompatible-auid-3662), the claim that everyone deserves one vote and is equally entitled to a say about who to elect, or what policies are best. The myth tells us that everyone can do this at age 18. These are political myths that are foundational for our democratic community and for ensuring equal rights and life chances for its citizens.

It seems pretty clear that totalitarianism is not a result of doing the kind of thing Socrates was recommending, since [Plato’s myth is designed to deliver fairer outcomes and better opportunities for all](https://iai.tv/articles/plato-hobbes-mill-and-the-never-ending-fight-for-inequality-auid-3079). Perhaps rather totalitarianism arises in exactly the opposite way in some cases. For example, it might start to find a hold when the foundational myths of democracy lose their efficacy—when society loses its commitment to that universal belonging that holds us together as brothers and sisters with the same rights and privileges. Or when we cease to believe in each other’s equal capacity for making good judgements and instead we come to believe that some individual is innately born for power.

Some of our current ideologies seem a lot less healthy, however. Particularly problematic is the ideology that stamps binary gender identities on infants at birth, and then encourages parents to raise children with divergent opportunities, gendered toys, gendered clothing, schooling and social activities, and increasingly stigmatizes and sanctions non-compliance among children and adults. It can be hard for us to see that this ideology is just that: a myth of classification at birth that is designed to pigeonhole people without giving them space to express their developing sense of self. There are similar forms of repression generated by cultures with apartheid ideologies that treat some social groups as less than human or as objects of fear. These too may have a decisive hold on the society where they are taught and perpetuated.

When we compare our noble lies to that of Plato, we can also see that there is something good about Plato’s self-awareness: his recognition (as author) that this is a kind of lie. We might ask ourselves whether we endorse the founding myths of democracy in that same spirit, in full awareness of their status as politically important fictions. Or perhaps we should understand Socrates to mean that he, as the founder, imagining his fictional utopia, sees that the story he wants them to believe is a myth, but the people in his new community would not themselves see it as such. They would believe it without question, and teach it as truth. Perhaps similarly we have to believe and teach our founding myths in that way.

But then, when we recognize that there can also be myths that teach us to hate and to exclude, sowing division, fear and apartheid, perhaps we should be wary and ensure that at some point we sit up and take notice, and ask ourselves whether something on which we have built our society is actually a pernicious lie. And in that case, if we do start to question whether this ideology is founded on lies, our next question might be whether, in such a case, empirical science can then uncover the truth and debunk the insidious myth that currently holds society in its grip.

That is not a question I can ask or answer here, but what I do hope to have shown is that Plato’s myth is not (as it has often been supposed) a tool to deny opportunities and social mobility to the young, but a tool for the opposite effect, to create a society in which privilege is not to be inherited or conferred but earned. And I hope to have opened the question concerning what myths we might need to use to achieve the corresponding kinds of leveling and [social mobility](https://iai.tv/video/riches-and-rags) in our own society—and what myths might be supporting ideologies that are downright dangerous or destructive in our current world.
