# Quantum mechanics describes our relationship with reality, not reality itself

*We are not spectators of the quantum world*

**By Michel Bitbol** | *28th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,196 words*

![Quantum mechanics describes our relationship with reality, not reality itself](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/quantum-mechanics-describes-our-relatonship-with-reality-not-reality-itself.webp)

---

*The physicist Antony Valentini recently argued in IAI News that quantum mechanics is a straightforward—though incomplete—description of material reality. Today, philosopher and former Director of Research at the renowned French National Center for Scientific Research, **Michel Bitbol**, makes the opposing case. Scientists, he argues, mistakenly assume that quantum mechanics must either be an incomplete description of a deterministic reality or a complete description of a genuinely random reality. A third option, too often ignored, is that the inevitability of probability at the core of physics is bound up with the agent's participation in its environment, and thus with our inseparability from the world.*

So many stunning technological achievements stem from quantum physics that it is difficult to believe it rests on a set of *negative* features. Here are three of them:

***Unpredictability:*** Individual events at the microscopic scale are generally unpredictable, except to the limited extent permitted by probabilistic estimates.

***Indeterminacy:*** Certain pairs of properties of a particle, such as its position and momentum, cannot be known simultaneously with arbitrary precision (“Heisenberg’s ‘uncertainty’ relations” or “Heisenberg’s indeterminacy principle”).

***Informational boundedness:*** According to an axiom proposed as a basis for deriving the formalism of quantum mechanics, “[there is a maximum amount of relevant information that can be extracted from a physical system](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02302261).”

To the satisfaction of physicists, these negative features do not hinder the theory’s predictive power and explanatory capacity; they are precisely what enable its extraordinary quantitative successes. One of its most remarkable accomplishments is the prediction of a nonzero minimum energy, which follows from Heisenberg’s indeterminacy. This implies, among other things, that even the *vacuum* exerts measurable pressure on closely spaced conducting plates, a phenomenon known as the Casimir effect.

But philosophers, and many of the physicists of the time who were philosophers at heart, sought to look beyond the theory’s practical successes and [understand the *meaning* of its negative features](https://iai.tv/video/the-metaphysics-of-quantum-mechanics-alyssa-ney). Accordingly, in the formative period of quantum theory a century ago, the debate among its pioneers focused largely on the *origin* of the “[indeterminism](https://archive.org/details/determinismindet0000cass)” that [appeared to permeate it](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01397477).

The options seemed simple and aligned with the ordinary dualistic theory of knowledge: the source of indeterminacy was sought in either the subject or the object, the human observer or the observed world, finite knowledge or an inexhaustible reality. Accordingly, when the chance occurrence of individual events was at issue during the famous [Solvay Conference in Brussels](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0609184), which brought together the creators of quantum mechanics in October 1927, some invoked a “choice of the observer,” whereas others spoke of a “choice of nature.” The dilemma was also reflected in the three words Heisenberg hesitantly used to characterize his relations: do they express merely our uncertainty (*Unsicherheit*) or imprecision (*Ungenauigkeit*) about a reality that is otherwise determined? Or do they reveal a genuine indeterminacy (*Unbestimmtheit*) of nature?

The dilemma became even more explicit in the [dramatic debate](https://iai.tv/articles/einstein-vs-bohr-quantum-reality-is-still-up-for-grabs-auid-3131) between Albert Einstein and the “Copenhagen school” (represented mainly by Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, and Max Born) over whether nature is fundamentally deterministic or indeterministic. Heisenberg and Born argued that the probabilistic nature of quantum formalism marks the ultimate collapse of causality and determinism in microphysics. [Einstein](https://iai.tv/articles/einstein-was-wrong-about-quantum-mechanics-tim-maudlin-auid-2700), by contrast, maintained that these probabilities merely reflect our ignorance of individual underlying deterministic processes, and that quantum mechanics should therefore be considered an *incomplete,* merely statistical, description of nature. This debate echoed a disagreement about the status of probabilities themselves. Do probabilities represent objective attributes of an indeterminate reality, whether as observed frequencies or intrinsic propensities? Or do they express our subjective expectations concerning events that may or may not be determined, but whose causes remain unknown to us?

But at that time, a dissident voice began to be heard, one that suggested the waning of the dualistic predicament. Attention shifted toward the transaction between the two poles of knowledge, moving beyond their opposition toward the interface between them. Werner Heisenberg thus insisted that [quantum mechanics should not be regarded as an image of nature](https://iai.tv/video/quantum-and-the-unknowable-universe), but as a description of our *relation to* nature. Bohr likewise claimed that we are actors in, rather than spectators of, nature’s drama, meaning that the very idea that we can bypass our engagement with nature and stand outside it to draw an external picture of it is flawed. It is flawed because the nonzero value of Planck’s constant imposes a quantitative limit on our separation from what we observe—another negative feature of the quantum domain! Under these circumstances, could quantum indeterminacy itself not be a characteristic of the inseparability of observer and observed, rather than of either considered separately?

\_\_\_

Our inseparability from the world imposes absolute limits on our *knowledge* of it.

\_\_\_

The latter view was already implicit in one of the fundamental postulates required to “quantize” a classical theory, namely, to transform it into its quantum counterpart. This is the *non-commutativity of observables*, whose scale is again set by Planck’s constant. All that remained was to decipher this postulate to uncover the unprecedented understanding of quantum indeterminacy it entailed. Roughly speaking, the fact that two observables do not commute means that the outcomes of successive measurements depend on the order in which those measurements are performed. And this order-dependence reveals that quantum measurement outcomes cannot, in general, be understood independently of the experimental context in which they are obtained.

Now, [as early as 1929](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.34.163), it was demonstrated that Heisenberg’s relations could be derived from the non-commutativity of observables. In this light, the conclusion seems unmistakable: *Quantum* *indeterminacy stems from a fundamental* [*limit on our separation from what we observe*](https://iai.tv/articles/introducing-the-realism-debate-auid-1763)*.*

This lesson, however, was far from immediately accepted in the decades following the advent of quantum mechanics. Most practitioners of “standard quantum mechanics” were indifferent to the meaning of quantum indeterminacy. And several prominent thinkers employed remarkable theoretical ingenuity in an attempt to show that quantum mechanics is either an incomplete, statistical approximation of a deeper deterministic layer of reality (as Einstein would have it), or a faithful description of an intrinsically non-deterministic reality.

On the side of intrinsic determinism, David Bohm thus proposed in 1952 a celebrated model of non-local deterministic “[hidden variables](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.85.166)” reproducing the predictions of quantum mechanics. A few years later, Louis de Broglie suggested that the apparent indeterminism of quantum mechanics results from the interaction of particles with a hidden “[subquantum thermostat](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/La_thermodynamique_de_la_particule_isol.html?id=DwSxAAAAIAAJ&redir_esc=y).” Particles here undergo fluctuations caused by an underlying deterministic subquantum dynamics, in a way analogous to the Brownian motion of mesoscopic particles driven by molecular impacts.

On the side of intrinsic indeterminism, an elegant theory has recently been [formulated by Jacob Barandes](https://doi.org/10.31389/pop.186). According to him, quantum predictions can be recovered by assuming that particles undergo continuous, intrinsically random, “indivisible” processes. These processes [cannot be divided into a sequence of independent stochastic steps which thus behave as if they retained a memory of their past](https://iai.tv/video/rethinking-the-fundamentals-of-quantum-physics-with-jacob-barandes), unlike ordinary Markovian processes.

But what happens if we do not content ourselves with positing the memory-like behavior of random processes as a feature of reality-in-itself, but seek to understand what might account for it? Here, the non-commutativity of quantum observables offers a clue: in sequential measurements of incompatible observables, changing which observable is measured first can change the statistics of the subsequent measurement. This suggests a distinction between two senses in which the past seems to matter. A process may have an intrinsic memory of what happened before. But what happened before may also matter simply because the ongoing context in which events are produced has been established in the immediate past. Let me illustrate this with a familiar example. I ask some people if they would like an ice cream, and a large majority of them answer “yes.” The statistically dominant response might be explained exclusively by their memory of liking ice cream. But it might instead depend to a large extent on the current weather context in which I ask the question: today’s heatwave.

The lesson from the early days of quantum theory was that quantum indeterminacy is a sign of (our) inseparability from the world. This insight may have been swept under the rug for a while, but, as we have just seen, it stubbornly recurs in various guises.

The resurgence of the thesis linking inseparability and indeterminacy is hardly surprising, since, as we’ll now see, its philosophical grounds extend far beyond quantum physics, into nothing less than the nature of knowledge and the nature of being (epistemology and ontology). It is significant that this link can be seen simultaneously from both perspectives—epistemological *and* ontological—thus overcoming the traditional dualistic opposition that would ascribe indeterminacy *either* to imperfect knowledge *or* to a feature of being.

\_\_\_

An experimental event is not a revelation of preexistent reality. It is an act of creation.

\_\_\_

One of its clearest prima facie *epistemological* statements was offered by Karl Popper [in a book written in the mid-1950s](https://archive.org/details/openuniverseargu0000popp). What Popper shows is that our inseparability from the world imposes absolute limits on our *knowledge* of it. Yet because these limits are indeed insuperable rather than merely accidental, they may also be envisioned as a constitutive feature of the *Being* of the whole we form with the world. We’ll develop this alternative ontological view in its own right later, but let’s first turn to Popper’s epistemological argument.

The reasoning runs thus: Since a predictor is embedded in the natural process it seeks to predict, Popper observes, the prediction itself becomes a causal factor altering the future state it is meant to predict. A complete prediction would therefore have to predict itself and its consequences, making perfect prediction in principle impossible, [regardless of whether the (hypothetical) laws governing these processes are deterministic or indeterministic](https://doi.org/10.1080/24740500.2022.2155200).

Even earlier, in the 1930s, similar arguments were formulated in two philosophies of probability. One was [Bruno de Finetti’s](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01236563); the other was [Jean Cavaillès’s](http://www.jstor.org/stable/40898496). For instance, Cavaillès strikingly weaves together the two threads of our now-familiar argument, namely (1) our “insertion” into nature through action, and (2) probability *qua* wager as an inescapable foundation of science:

*To know the world is to bet—to bet that certain acts will succeed ... It is the law of interest that guides us: to insert ourselves into nature, to live within becoming … (But if, indeed) every physical law is no more than a wager for action, the scandal of probability ceases; far from being an inadequate substitute for our power to know, it is the very type … of all scientific activity.*

However, the most relevant formulation of this idea took shape in [Quantum Bayesianism](https://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-and-our-part-in-creating-reality-auid-1881) (QBism), a subjectivist and probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics. According to QBism, quantum mechanics is a normative scheme that guides agents in assigning, on the basis of prior experience, probabilities for possible future experiences resulting from their experimental actions. Crucially, this must be supplemented by the recognition that an experimental event is not a revelation of preexistent reality. It is [an act of *creation*](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.85.1693): the outcome of a deeply enmeshed transaction that brings forth a new experience for the agent together with a manifest object of this experience. Here, indeterminism can be understood as arising from the fact that experimental outcomes are not given in advance, but come about through our active engagement with what we seek to know. In other words, the inevitability of probability at the core of physics is bound up with the agent’s *participation* in its environment—what John Wheeler called “[observer-participancy](https://iai.tv/articles/to-solve-quantum-gravity-we-must-go-beyond-the-physical-auid-3237).”

Taking the *ontological* implication of this approach further, we could say that indeterminacy expresses the consequences of the thorough embeddedness of our *being* in the world—an embeddedness so organic that it resembles our *embodiment*, the way we intimately partake in the life of our own bodies. Accordingly, I call this indeterminacy-generating mode of being embedded, **“**[*en-worldment*](https://hal.science/hal-04294454v1/file/QBism_Eco-Phenomenology_Proofs.pdf)**.”**

Among philosophers, Maurice Merleau-Ponty was the one who gave this ontological turn its fullest expression. His aim was to overcome the traditional dualism between sentient subjects and sensed objects through a penetrating phenomenological analysis of human experience. Careful attention to experience reveals a two-sided mode of being that Merleau-Ponty called “the flesh.” Indeed, our bodily flesh has the capacity to serve alternately as sentient and sensed, thereby joining what is traditionally held apart.

But Merleau-Ponty did not stop at this point: he universalized this dual-aspect mode of being to what he called “[the flesh of the world](https://nupress.northwestern.edu/9780810104570/the-visible-and-the-invisible/).” Little extrapolation is needed to recognize here the suggestion of a world coextensive with experience, even as experience is *reflected upon* only from the local perspective of our living body. I call this suggestion “[*en-experiencing the world*](https://hal.science/hal-04294454v1/file/QBism_Eco-Phenomenology_Proofs.pdf),” which complements the “en-worldment” of experience.

By replacing the standard face-to-face relation between knower and known with a participatory, immersive, and even reciprocally merging mode of being, Merleau-Ponty could readily render the inherent indeterminism of quantum mechanics intelligible. “[Probability here enters into the texture of reality](https://www.classiques.uqam.ca/classiques/merleau_ponty_maurice/La_Nature/La_Nature.pdf),” he declared. Yet this does not mean that nature itself is indeterminate: to understand the inescapability of probabilities, we need only recognize that “[quantum physics … brings the contact between the observer and the observed *into its very definition of ‘reality’*](https://nupress.northwestern.edu/9780810104570/the-visible-and-the-invisible/).”

To recapitulate, quantum indeterminism should be understood as an unequivocal sign of our bond with the fabric of the world—a bond that is not merely carnal, but also ecological. The upshot is striking: beyond their habitual tendency to treat ecosystems as merely one object of knowledge among others, the natural sciences have [encountered a limit that reveals their own ecological foundation](https://www.librairiealbinmichel.fr/livre/9788869763717-philosophie-quantique-le-monde-est-il-exterieur-michel-bitbol/).
