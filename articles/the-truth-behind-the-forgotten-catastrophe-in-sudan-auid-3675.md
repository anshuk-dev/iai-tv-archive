# The truth behind the forgotten catastrophe in Sudan

*Peace deals are only keeping Sudan’s war machine running*

**By Matthew Benson-Strohmayer** | *9th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,099 words*

![The truth behind the forgotten catastrophe in Sudan](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-truth-behind-the-forgotten-catastrophe-in-Sudan2.webp)

---

*When the West looks at Sudan's war, it sees a humanitarian catastrophe beyond its understanding, despite its astronomical toll. But historian **Matthew Benson-Strohmayer**, Sudans Research Director at LSE's Conflict and Civicness Research Group, argues this account does not explain how Sudan's warring generals fund their violence: both sides have become commercial empires, commanding gold, trade and foreign patronage beyond any government's control. Until a peace deal can dismantle this machinery and heed the civilian actors working to take back control, ending the fighting will only reset the conditions for the next war.*

Sudan is often understood as a catastrophe in which the world’s worst humanitarian crisis seems perpetual and therefore both lamentable and unsolvable. To the extent that media coverage is available, the world sees the numbers, or at least is told them. Since April 2023, the war has [forced around 14 million people to flee their homes](https://www.unhcr.org/news/briefing-notes/three-years-war-weary-sudanese-remain-move). [Nearly 34 million](https://sudan.un.org/en/313793-three-years-too-long-people-sudan-need-peace) now require humanitarian assistance. Earlier this year, [almost 19.5 million people were experiencing acute food insecurity, while 825,000 children were expected to suffer severe acute malnutrition during 2026](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/details-map/en/c/1163315/). Yet the international humanitarian appeal [remains less than one-third funded](https://www.un.org/sg/en/content/highlight/2026-07-09.html).

To say that Sudan has been forgotten is therefore true, but incomplete. Sudan is made visible through a particular story, framed as a crisis and represented by famine warnings, displacement numbers, and images of devastated cities. While they can illustrate the depth of the crisis, Sudanese people appear primarily as victims of mass killing, evidenced through [satellite images of massacre sites](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2025/dec/05/rsf-massacres-sudanese-city-el-fasher-slaughterhouse-satellite-images), and urgently in need of humanitarian relief. The resulting narrative blames the armed leaders who began the war, but in doing so, it paradoxically makes them seem like the only ones capable of ending it. The structures that finance those leaders, and the Sudanese civic actors who have tried to dismantle them, disappear from the narrative.

While such media attention is necessary to generate a much-needed humanitarian response, the all-too-familiar humanitarian lens often defaulted to for this seemingly distant tragedy also narrows the field of vision. The consequences of war come into focus, while the political and economic systems that make war possible and profitable are obscured. Sudan is presented as a place to which catastrophe has happened, rather than as a political crisis produced through a long history of militarized, internationalized extraction. The war has become a shorthand for familiar stories of primordial grievance, tribal war, or yet another African conflict. Such explanations make the conflict appear inevitable rather than politically produced, and therefore open to transformation.

So, while Sudan’s war is conventionally described as a civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), led by Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), led by Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as Hemedti, that description obscures the reality. While it identifies the main belligerents, it mistakes their struggle for a war between two social constituencies. Most Sudanese are represented by neither side and held hostage by rival military formations.

But, by April 2023, the SAF and RSF were not simply two military organizations competing for control of a state. They had become fiscal-military complexes: armed organizations able to control commercial assets to generate revenue, and to cultivate foreign relationships beyond civilian oversight. Together, they formed the armed core of what I call a hostage economy, in which military organizations command the revenues needed to sustain coercion, while civilians and public institutions face the costs.

The SAF sits at the center of an extensive military-commercial economy spanning defense production, construction, agriculture and other sectors. The RSF developed its own sources of autonomy through gold, trading networks and paid military deployments abroad. During Sudan’s attempted transition after the 2019 revolution, [Hemedti could pledge](https://timep.org/2023/07/26/money-is-power-hemedti-and-the-rsfs-paramilitary-industrial-complex-in-sudan/) over a billion dollars to Sudan’s Central Bank while the civilian government could not command much of the country’s own public revenue. This money revealed where effective power lay, and that this inequality was more than a minor defect in an otherwise civilian transition.

It is therefore inadequate to describe this as evidence of corruption or state failure. Corruption implies that resources are being diverted from a functioning public system, and state failure implies the decay of institutions. Sudan’s history points to a more deliberate process, wherein financial control is dispersed among military companies, security agencies, regime-linked banks, political enterprises, commodity networks and foreign creditors.

This history reaches well beyond the present war. In a process I have described elsewhere as [fiscal fragmentation](https://doi.org/10.1080/17502977.2025.2576462), successive governments, instead of drawing on citizens’ taxes and cooperation, found alternative sources of finance. This first took place through tariffs on customs and export agriculture, then external borrowing via debt and foreign assistance, later the extraction of oil reserves, and increasingly unregulated mining of gold and other off-budget income. War has deepened this process. Cash-strapped governments outsourced counter-insurgency to militias that were permitted to finance themselves through looting and predation. Rebel movements developed their own coercive systems of taxation and administration. The organizations produced by war were then incorporated into formal institutions without surrendering their fiscal autonomy.

\_\_\_

Militias and other bodies grew their predatory capacities at the cost of institutions that were democratically accountable to Sudan’s citizens, deliberately unbuilding the state that was meant to protect them.

Much like the media narrative that obfuscates even as it seeks to illuminate Sudan’s crisis, international peacemaking repeatedly misreads this political economy. Negotiations tend to treat Sudan’s wars as conflicts about territory and government representation. While race, religion and regional marginalization are undeniably part of these conflicts, they do not explain how armed actors acquire the fiscal autonomy to keep them going. These identity-based considerations follow from the war’s political economy, rather than drive it. Peace agreements divide offices of state and establish revenue-sharing formulas for development. But they only set out how official, recognized revenue should be allocated without confronting the domestic and transnational networks through which armed actors actually finance themselves and the wars they wage.

The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement between the Government of Sudan and the SPLM/A constructed an elaborate formal architecture for oil sharing, public auditing and fiscal transfers. However, it did not dismantle military-commercial holdings, bring security expenditure under civilian control, or transform the off-budget networks sustaining coercive rule. Similarly, the 2020 Juba Peace Agreement, signed between Sudan’s transitional government and several armed movements from Darfur and other conflict-affected regions, contained [extensive provisions on fiscal federalism and wealth sharing](https://www.peaceagreements.org/agreements/2325/), but redistributed recognized sources of income and public offices without ending the financial autonomy of the SAF and RSF.

Real outcomes happen as a result of this; real institutions are formed, and budgets create real transfers of wealth. But there is a fiction that lies in the performance of a unified and accountable fiscal order, while the financial channels that sustain coercive power remain untouched.

This is part of a pattern that [I have described elsewhere](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17502977.2025.2576462?scroll=top&needAccess=true) as [predatory peace](https://peacerep.org/publication/from-war-economies-to-predatory-peace/), in which peace settlements end up reorganizing wartime resource extraction rather than dismantling it. Armed elites exchange battlefield positions for military rank and access to public funds. Hemedti, for example, started out as a militia commander and then gained official positions during the 2019 transition, while his RSF kept control of gold mining operations and trading networks. Under al-Burhan, the SAF continued to hold extensive military and commercial interests, such as defense production, construction and agriculture, even as they signed peace agreements promising fiscal reforms. International recognition has the unintended consequence of converting coercive actors into official partners. Peacemaking thereby risks stabilizing the political economy of war. It asks who should sit at the table without asking what finances their seat.

Sudan’s belligerents do not sustain themselves within Sudan alone; instead, their commercial, procurement and recruitment networks cross borders and continents. [Recent sanctions actions have traced explosives and military supplies entering SAF-linked networks through foreign companies](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0544), while [another transnational network recruited former Colombian soldiers to fight for the RSF](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0330). Gold, weapons, credit, logistics and political sponsorship connect Sudan’s war to markets and governments far beyond its borders.

The familiar descriptions of Sudan as a distant “forgotten war,” or as a “civil war” seemingly confined within the country’s borders, conceal these relationships. It is not so much that the world is simply absent from Sudan as that parts of the world are present, but as suppliers, financiers, brokers and diplomatic patrons, entangled more than indifferent.

And the humanitarian framing obscures a further part of Sudan’s story: Sudanese people have not only endured the state’s unbuilding; instead, they have repeatedly developed alternatives to it. The revolution that removed Omar al-Bashir in 2019 was not merely a demand to replace military rulers with civilian politicians. The idea of [*medania*, which is often translated as civilian rule or civicness](https://peacerep.org/publication/sudans-civic-future/), also contained a claim about political economy: public resources should sustain collective life rather than coercion.

Civic organizations were quick to diagnose the financial underpinnings of authoritarian rule. Neighborhood-based resistance committees coordinated sit-ins and civil disobedience, while professional associations of doctors, lawyers, teachers, and engineers continued Sudan’s strong tradition of political organizing. Along with civil servants, small-business coalitions and other activist groups, they demanded that military-owned companies fall under Ministry of Finance jurisdiction. They called for audits of security-sector revenues, recovery of captured assets, transparency in the gold sector, and public scrutiny of budgets, while opposing austerity measures that raised the cost of food and fuel for ordinary people while leaving the military’s commercial empire untouched.

This movement is a form of civic fiscal resistance: an effort to make opaque revenue systems intelligible and accountable while constructing an alternative vision of public finance. For a brief period, some of this agenda acquired institutional form. The Empowerment Dismantling Committee sought to identify and recover assets captured by the former regime and security sector. Its fate is revealing. After the military coup of October 2021, [the committee was suspended and the deep state moved to reverse the civilian transition](https://c4ads.org/reports/breaking-the-bank/). Its leaders were detained, and recovered assets were seized. When civic reform threatened the material foundations of military power, military power dismantled it.

Since war began in April 2023, many of the same traditions of civic organization have reappeared in neighborhood mutual-aid networks and Emergency Response Rooms. [Volunteers have kept civilians alive, often at immense personal risk](https://sudan.un.org/en/308542-human-rights-chief-addresses-press-port-sudan), while the formal humanitarian system has struggled with access and funding. Yet international policy still tends to treat them as part of an amorphous ‘civil society’ to be consulted, rather than as political thinkers with valuable ideas about peace.

Inviting civic actors to consultations is not the same as recognizing their analysis. Sudanese civic movements have argued that a peace process that includes civil society symbolically, ignoring their arguments that there can be no meaningful civilian transition without civilian control of public finance, will reproduce the conditions of war.

Humanitarian relief in Sudan must, therefore, be joined to a political account of why relief remains necessary. That means [following the money as closely as the fighting](https://iai.tv/articles/the-weaponization-of-money-auid-2077): tracing the gold, companies, credit arrangements, procurement routes and foreign relationships that sustain armed rule. It also means returning asset recovery to the political agenda—but through transparent, rights-respecting civilian institutions able to complete the work cut short by the 2021 coup. Military-commercial power and civilian fiscal jurisdiction must be central to ceasefire and settlement design, not deferred as technical governance questions. Local aid networks and civic coalitions should be recognized as political actors able to shape institutions, not merely as substitutes for services the state has abandoned.

Above all, it requires abandoning the assumption that the men with guns are the realists in the room while those demanding civic government are idealists. Sudan’s armed elites have repeatedly produced agreements unable to secure peace. Civic actors, by contrast, identified the fiscal systems that made authoritarian restoration and renewed war possible. Their proposals were politically vulnerable, but they were not politically naïve.

Sudan is not inherently difficult to see, but familiar tropes make it visible in ways that hide the mechanisms of its destruction. We see hunger but not the systems that turn extraction into armed power. We see peace agreements but not the revenue networks they leave intact and often formalize into a [predatory peace](https://peacerep.org/publication/from-war-economies-to-predatory-peace/). We see victims but not citizens engaged in the difficult work of rebuilding public authority. The world will not understand Sudan until it learns to see all three. Until it does, peacemaking will continue to reproduce the conditions of war and the hostage economy that it generates in the country in times of peace and war.
