# You are both one self and many selves

*Inner conflict and psychological democracy*

**By Luke Roelofs** | *17th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,113 words*

![You are both one self and many selves](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-self-is-a-divided-self.webp)

---

*Is there one self or many competing selves within us? Some argue we contain multiple selves, warring for attention and even control of the organism. But philosopher Luke Roelofs argues that we must distinguish between two accounts of the self to make sense of this: a persona emerging from mental processes and the substrate that underpins them. We can have multiple personas in conflict with one another, but the substrate in which they are in conflict remains unified. We should allow our multiple selves to democratically coexist in order to live a richer, more harmonious, internal life.*

The Ancient philosopher Plato famously argued that our soul has three parts (usually translated as “reason”, “spirit”, and “appetite”), which are often at war with one another. His argument? We routinely find ourselves wracked with inner conflict, where we seem to simultaneously want a particular thing and also reject it. In [one dramatic example](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/55201/pg55201-images.html#BookIV) he cites, the character Leontius struggles with both abhorrence at a pile of corpses and a desire to look at them, eventually breaking down and looking while screaming angrily at his own eyes “look, you wretches”.

We often describe our struggles and turmoils in terms suggesting a multiplicity of agents (or even [a pair of wolves](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/inside-you-there-are-two-wolves)). We say we are at war with ourselves, or that we are not in control of ourselves, that a part of us wants something or believes something, but another part disagrees.

\_\_\_

We can broadly distinguish approaches where the self is something that emerges out of mental processes, which we might call a “persona”, from approaches where it’s something that underlies and enables mental processes, which we might call a “substrate”.

\_\_\_

Is Plato’s argument a good one? Does the experience of inner conflict really demonstrate that we are, in some sense, more than one agent? Are we each, ultimately, more a collective than an individual, with many selves and not just one? It’s hard to answer this question without a theory of the self, and [philosophical debate](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/01/17/opinion/severance-the-substance-hollywood.html?unlocked_article_code=1.p04.7Za_.YY7D1yd-hcW6&smid=url-share) on that question is ongoing and complex. But here is one observation: We can broadly distinguish approaches where the self is something that emerges out of mental processes, which we might call a “persona”, from approaches where it’s something that underlies and enables mental processes, which we might call a “substrate”. And an argument like Plato’s is likely to be much more compelling on one type of approach than the other.

For example, people sometimes connect the self to memories. If your memories were transferred to another body, maybe that would be *your* body. Altering one person’s brain so that what they can remember at work is completely discontinuous with what they can remember outside (as in the TV show [Severance](https://tv.apple.com/us/show/severance/umc.cmc.1srk2goyh2q2zdxcx605w8vtx%255d),)) would maybe split them into two distinct people. But, clearly, memories can only be formed if there is already a working brain in the picture: the brain enables mental processes, out of which a consistent stream of memories emerges. Similar things could be said about the self as a personality, a narrative, or a set of values and commitments. All of these things are downstream from whatever enables moment-to-moment awareness, perception, and action. Mental life has to already be happening, before the self can emerge.

On the other hand, people sometimes connect the self to the brain, or the body, or the soul, or more broadly to whatever it is that enables our mental life to happen. After all, the fact that I don’t remember your experiences and you don’t remember mine, significant as it may be, is itself fairly obviously explained by the fact that those experiences leave traces in two quite distinct neural structures. And intuitively it might seem that as long as my brain remains in working order, supporting an ongoing mental life, that will be *my* mental life, even if its content changes radically through processes of learning, injury, or personal growth.

I’ve suggested the terms “personas” and “substrates” to capture this difference concisely, while leaving it an open question which one, if either, is best viewed as our real self. So when we ask whether Plato is right that the self is divided in 3, are we interested in divisions among component personas, or between component substrates?

On the one hand, inner conflict does seem directly relevant to divisions among personas. If the self comes from the emergence of a consistent set of values, goals, memories, habits, or similar, then sharp differences or oppositions in values and goals would seem to be one step towards there being multiple selves. Indeed, some people do explicitly report having multiple selves – or, as they would say, some human bodies produce reports from multiple people. Such “plurals” [raise fascinating issues](https://aeon.co/ideas/what-we-can-learn-about-respect-and-identity-from-plurals) about identity and respect, which require ongoing examination, but it’s clear that if there are in fact multiple selves here, it is because of a division among personas, not a division among underlying substrates. The members of each plural system still share one brain, arising not from different material organs but from different patterns of activity within one organ. One way to take Plato’s argument is as claiming that there is only a difference of degree between plural systems and the more typical arrangement (being a “singlet”), and taken this way there might be something to it.

On the other hand, inner conflict doesn’t seem like a compelling reason to postulate a division at the level of substrates. It seems to be perfectly possible for one brain, or soul, or whatever it is that makes our mental lives possible in the first place, to support opposing attitudes towards one and the same thing at the same time, just as many waves of different directions, speeds, and amplitudes might be passing through the same water at once.

That said, the brain is, as a matter of fact, divisible into parts. Indeed it is highly divisible, in a way that is at odds with the [perfect unity of the soul](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4020-6893-5) that many historical philosophers insisted on . The most famous of its divisions is that between the left and right cerebral hemispheres, which turn out to be each [capable of sustaining a stream of consciousness](https://philpapers.org/rec/BLAHAI-2) even with the other one removed, and which may even sustain [two simultaneous streams of consciousness](https://academic.oup.com/book/10111) when the fiber bundle connecting them is severed in the “split-brain” operation.

One striking thing about this division, however, is that while the two hemispheres do show some psychological differences (most notably, only one can use language), these differences can easily be exaggerated. Splitting them does not separate, say, “reason” from “appetite”, or “good” from “evil”, in any clean and straightforward way. The divisions revealed by our experience of inner conflict do not perfectly map onto anatomical divisions. This suggests that if our selves (both as personas and as substrates) are divisible, they are not divisible into robust, pre-formed, anatomically defined, parts: rather, they are fluidly divisible, able to be carved and shaped and put together and pulled apart in an open-ended variety of ways. We might have composite selves, but in a way much messier than Plato’s neat three-item list suggests.

However exactly we interpret their metaphysical implications, claims about the unity or compositeness of the self tend to have political resonance. After all, inner conflict is usually an unpleasant experience, so it’s natural to see it as a problem in need of fixing, by getting the parts that are “at war” within us to reach some sort of peaceful arrangement. And Plato explicitly employs his account of the soul as an analogy for political justice. More specifically, Plato justifies a certain sort of benevolent dictatorship, rule by the most rational section of society, the “philosopher kings”, by analogy to an ideal of “psychological dictatorship” of reason over spirit and appetite. (In fairness to Plato, it’s very unclear how seriously this is meant, and how much is tongue-in-cheek.)

But while the ideal of dictatorial rule by the wise is less popular nowadays, the idea that your mind ought to be ruled by the “rational part” of you is much more common. The ideal of taming and disciplining your unsavory desires and inconvenient feelings is popular and mainstream, perhaps most obviously in diet culture, which encourages people to view an entire facet of their physical existence, eating, as a potentially endless battle of wills.

Plato insists, of course, that this sort of dominance is benign, because the rational part of the soul (and the “philosopher kings” in which it predominates) is by its very nature concerned with figuring out what is good for everyone. But one only needs a little cynicism to suspect that someone who justifies their power-seeking by philanthropic concern for humanity might actually like power for its own sake. And equally we might suspect that even when the desire for self-domination is justified by appeal to rational estimates of the greater good, it might actually come from desiring power for its own sake. We know that people sometimes respond to feelings of powerlessness by lashing out at whatever target is nearby, just to feel a sense of control over something. What is more nearby than your own self? And if we can recognize that impulse to assert control as a bad guide to action, even when it’s understandable, we might likewise worry about the drive to assert control over oneself, to divide into a consciously-identified-with “rational self” and a part of oneself seen only as weakness. There is now considerable evidence, for example, that diets routinely do more harm than good, suggesting that [diet culture](https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/lessons-from-the-fat-o-sphere-kate-harding/1102592502?bvstate=pg:2/ct:r) is sustained less by a sober assessment of risks than by an aesthetic attraction to an ideal of discipline, of refusing to “let yourself go”.

Indeed, philosopher Amanda Evans argues that disorders like anorexia involve a pathological development of the impulse to control through denying one’s own desires, producing an [illusion of agency](https://philpapers.org/rec/EVAANI). Anorexics aren’t really in control of themselves, but whereas people with other conditions like drug addiction *feel* their lack of control, anorexia feels like supreme self-control because it feeds this drive to self-domination.

So we might be interested in finding a different political metaphor for how to reconcile the parts of our selves. Philosopher Jeff Sebo argues that, to whatever extent different stages of one person’s life can be viewed as distinct selves, they exist in precisely the kind of [circumstances](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10790-014-9465-6) that require groups of humans to establish rules of justice: conflicting needs but a shared fate and a consequent need to co-exist peacefully.

\_\_\_

We might envisage an ideal of “psychological democracy”: a way for us to conduct ourselves that would provide, to all the parts of our self that exist or might emerge, as much satisfaction and control as is compatible with the same for every other part.

\_\_\_

But what sort of justice? Plato defines justice as reason being obeyed by the other parts of the soul. At the opposite extreme, David Hume famously said that “Reason is and ought only to be the [slave of the passions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD8Hume.html#ReaOugOnlSlaPas)”. Somewhere in between, we might envisage an ideal of “psychological democracy”: a way for us to conduct ourselves that would provide, to all the parts of our self that exist or might emerge, as much satisfaction and control as is compatible with the same for every other part. While this formulation might sound abstruse, it manifests in practical principles that are actually pretty familiar:

* Avoid repression and denial: If there is pain in your soul, feel that pain, and if there is a thought in your head, think it clearly, even if it conflicts with the others.

* Embrace variety: You might be capable of enjoying and desiring many different things, performing many different roles, inhabiting many different lifestyles.

* Resist asceticism: It can be tempting to embrace denial and suffering because it gives a sense of control. But hurting one part, or potential part, of yourself in order to gratify another is unfair, and in the long run, the democrat might claim, it will only make life worse for the whole collective.

* A final principle might be tolerance of inner conflict. Just as a democratic society must allow for protest and disagreement, a democratic self would allow space for indecision, ambivalence, hypocrisy, and even inconsistent behavior. And just as a defender of democracy might note that, however noisesome such conflicts are, they are less destructive than the authoritarianism required to prevent them, a defender of the democratic self might say that, however frustrating inner inconsistency can be, it’s still better than trying to stamp it out through repression, rigidity, or self-punishing efforts as self-mastery. It might be best to simply say, with [Walt Whitman](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45477/song-of-myself-1892-version), “Do I contradict myself? Very well, then I contradict myself, (I am large, I contain multitudes.)”
