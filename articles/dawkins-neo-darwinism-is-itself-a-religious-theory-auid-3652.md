# Dawkins' neo-Darwinism is itself a religious theory

*Organisms are not passive, they engineer evolution*

**By Jessica Riskin** | *11th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,446 words*

![Dawkins' neo-Darwinism is itself a religious theory](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/26-08-11-Dawkins-neo-Darwinism-is-itself-a-religious-theory.webp)

---

*We often see evolutionary theory as the enemy of religion. But in Darwin’s day, many scientists thought otherwise. Neo-Darwinism, today championed by atheists like Richard Dawkins, was founded by a devout Christian, who explicitly tied its mechanistic vision of life to a world ordered by God’s laws. In contrast, argues Stanford historian of science **Jessica Riskin**, the anti-mechanistic theory of Lamarck—which grants living beings genuine creativity—was attacked as dangerously atheistic. Riskin argues that today’s supporters of neo-Darwinism have forgotten its theological roots, and that Lamarck’s theory, with its radical, anti-theological motivations, should be taken more seriously.*

One of the longest-running debates in the history of science must surely be the one about whether living beings exert creative agency to influence the course of evolution. When whales adopt a new hunting technique, birds change their mating patterns, or lizards migrate to a colder altitude and find a way to warm themselves on the sunbaked rock, do they shape their descendants by these behaviors? Or are they subject to an evolutionary process over which they exert no influence? For a century and a half, this argument has raged. Neo-Darwinists have held the dominant position. [According to them](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Selfish_Gene.html?id=ekonDAAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y), animals are passive, the objects of random variation and natural selection. But [revisionist challenges](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691262413/evolution-evolving?srsltid=AfmBOoozpXOzzHYh3v89EP4sRx_QesDAKf5ooE7_mk5csOCa2gY2ZOr-) have multiplied in recent decades, pressing for a [broader picture of evolution](https://iai.tv/articles/denis-noble-the-broken-paradigm-of-neo-darwinism-auid-2210) in which other factors, including animal behavior, come into play.

This long debate over the evolutionary agency of organisms grew out of an earlier one about whether evolution took place at all. [The person who set off all this controversy was a Frenchman named Jean-Baptiste Lamarck](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/763654/the-power-of-life-by-jessica-riskin/). In 1802, Lamarck coined the word “[biology](https://archive.org/details/hydrogologieou00lama/page/8/mode/2up?q=biologie)”; and in 1809, the year of Charles Darwin’s birth, he published the first theory of evolution in his magnum opus, *The Zoological Philosophy*. In [this work of audacious radicalism](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5675762f), Lamarck usurped God’s monopoly on Creation and reassigned it to mortal beings.

Life originated in spontaneous generation, Lamarck wrote, brought about by natural forces such as electricity and heat acting upon inanimate matter, causing the emergence of the simplest organisms: single-celled protozoa and algae. A property intrinsic to living matter then exerted itself, a tendency to complexify, which Lamarck named “the power of life.” This was a material force akin to those of contemporary physics: electricity, magnetism, gravity. As organisms grew more complex, another cause of transformation entered, according to Lamarck: responsiveness to the environment. In the simplest beings this responsiveness was rudimentary—an irritability or sensitivity and reflexive reaction—but birds and mammals were capable of “acts of will,” forming “habits” and “ways of life” in response to their circumstances. The giraffe somehow became the emblem of this idea, although Lamarck only wrote a couple paragraphs about giraffes in all his voluminous writings. He [suggested](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5675762f/f289.item) that by stretching to reach high leaves, giraffes elongated their necks by infinitesimal amounts, then passed on these tiny changes, which accumulated over the generations to produce the giraffe’s long neck.

Not only did living beings create and transform themselves, in Lamarck’s view, but they also created the inanimate world. Plants played the primary role here because [they could produce themselves from just sunlight, air, and water](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5557937w/f303.item.texteImage). Animals then turned the work of plants into more elaborate compounds, sharing the defining ability of all living beings: to compose and build. Lamarck invented the neologism “biology” to name a new science he thought should exist: a science of all and only living beings, the creators. There were broader sciences, such as natural history, which included inanimate things like rocks and rivers; and narrower ones, like botany or zoology. But he thought [there should be a science of *life*: biology](https://archive.org/details/hydrogologieou00lama/page/8/mode/2up?q=biologie).

The same year that Lamarck coined “biology,” 1802, saw the publication of a very different model of life. Across the English Channel, an Anglican clergyman named William Paley brought out a book entitled *Natural Theology, or Evidences of the Existence and Attributes of the Deity Collected from the Appearances of Nature*, in which he recounted a kind of fable. “In crossing a heath,” [Paley wrote](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Natural_Theology.html?id=wwQUDAAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y), “suppose I pitched my foot against a stone, and were asked how the stone came to be there.” He might reasonably reply that the stone could’ve been there forever. “[But suppose I had found a watch upon the ground](https://archive.org/details/naturaltheologyo1802pale/page/n13/mode/2up?q=heath).” Surely, he couldn’t give the same answer; someone must have made the watch and left it there.

This was a pithy expression of [the argument from design](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2014/entries/teleological-arguments/), proving the existence of God by showing that natural things, especially living ones, were complex machines perfectly suited to their places in nature, and must therefore have a designer. But consider the watch in Paley’s story. Sure, it differs from the stone in having complex moving parts, but it also differs from most living things by lying passively waiting to be kicked aside. What if it had yelped and scuttled away? The more self-driven activity we recognize in the living world, the less it seems to require a supernatural source of order and meaning. Lamarck’s theory was dangerous indeed.

It provoked the withering scorn of his colleague and arch-nemesis, the paleontologist Georges Cuvier. A favorite of Napoleon and member of his scientific inner circle, Cuvier was a devout Lutheran who categorically rejected the idea of evolution. His central anatomical principle was the principle of correlation of parts: he claimed to infer all the parts of a creature from any single part. As the creations of a rational designer, the parts must imply one another by rational necessity. [Cuvier had considerable success](https://archive.org/details/b22375971/page/8/mode/2up) with such predictions based on his extensive knowledge of comparative anatomy; these seemed to confirm his claim that organisms exhibited rational design. Animal behaviors, he insisted, followed from their structures—they behaved as they were designed to behave—[whereas Lamarck said it was just the opposite](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k4226016j/f70.item): animal structures followed from their behaviors.

Cuvier was the victor who wrote the history, quite literally: as director of the French National Institute, he wrote Lamarck’s official “eulogy,” which is surely the nastiest eulogy ever written, dismissing Lamarck’s theory as poetry rather than science. Following Cuvier, many ridiculed Lamarck’s science. Because it emerged in the midst of the French Revolution and replaced God with the creative agency of organisms, it smelled to contemporaries of atheism, revolution, and regicide. These ill odors promised trouble not only for Lamarck and Lamarckism but for evolutionary theory more generally.

Charles Darwin struggled over his relations to both Lamarck and Paley. In his later years, he recalled that as a student at Cambridge, he’d had to study Paley’s works so closely he reckoned he’d practically memorized them. From Paley and the argument-from-design tradition, Darwin took the idea of fitness: organisms were consistently suited to their environments. At the same time, he drew importantly on Lamarck’s theory in formulating his own. He adopted the idea that [living beings were always varying](https://iai.tv/video/organisms-are-processes-not-things-with-wahida-khandker), although Darwin didn’t assume the variations necessarily tended toward greater complexity. Even without such a tendency, he believed, variations could help explain how organisms came to be environmentally adapted.

An important aspect of Darwin’s explanation was the now-familiar principle that living beings had to compete for scarce resources in the struggle to survive. Those with advantageous variations would survive longer and reproduce more, passing on their beneficial traits to their offspring in the process Darwin called natural selection. But he also retained from Lamarck various forms of agency within the evolving organism, shaping variation and survival, including Lamarck’s principle that animal behaviors produced heritable bodily effects, which Darwin called “[the inherited effects of use and disuse](https://darwin-online.org.uk/content/frameset?itemID=F880.2&viewtype=text&pageseq=1).” In this sense, Darwin was a Lamarckian, and he [conscientiously acknowledged his debt to Lamarck](https://darwin-online.org.uk/content/frameset?itemID=F381&viewtype=text&pageseq=1). But he also insisted on the distinctness of his own theory; he wanted people to accept his ideas and, as he wrote to his friend the geologist Charles Lyell, he knew an association with Lamarckism would be “[very injurious](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/?docId=letters/DCP-LETT-4038.xml).” After Darwin died, his followers eliminated all traces of Lamarckism from their Neo-Darwinist theory, rendering organisms as passive as they had been at the hands of Cuvier’s and Paley’s divine creator. The eradication of Lamarckism became the defining feature of Neo-Darwinism.

A founding figure in this movement was the German biologist August Weisman, who drew a sharp distinction between somatic (body) cells and germ (reproductive) cells. He said no changes taking place in the somatic cells could ever be transmitted to the germ cells. This was the “Weismann barrier,” eliminating the possibility of “inheritance of acquired characteristics.” In a rather cruel series of experiments, Weismann chopped the tails off several generations of mice to show that their offspring had healthy tails. He acknowledged that this had no real bearing on Lamarck’s theory: neither Lamarck nor Darwin had claimed that amputations were inherited, only internally generated changes. But [Weismann said](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48132/pg48132-images.html#Page_419) his experiments would defeat popular belief in inheritance of acquired characteristics. They were indeed rhetorically powerful and still appear in biology textbooks. A half-century after Weismann’s experiments, following the identification of the DNA molecule, Francis Crick restated the Weismann barrier as the “Central Dogma” of molecular biology: transcription happens only in one direction, from DNA to RNA to protein. From this fact, Crick concluded there could be no inheritance of acquired characteristics, finding a vindication of Weismann in the double-helix molecule.

But there was an aspect of Weismann’s theory they ignored: he had emphasized that his approach to science rested on the assumption of a divine presence behind the whole process. He assured his readers that his biology didn’t just leave room for a “Universal Cause” but actually demanded one. He wrote of a “Final Cause” operating “behind” the mechanism of the universe. All agency lay with this remote, divine presence and none with the evolving, mortal beings themselves. Therefore, Weismann said his science was “[absolutely opposed](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/47849/pg47849-images.html#Page_634)” to materialism.

In other words, Neo-Darwinism originated in an overtly theological revision of Darwin’s theory. This revision eliminated the creative agency that both Lamarck and Darwin had ascribed to living organisms. This might seem surprising, since the principal promulgators of Neo-Darwinism in the 20th and 21st centuries have been outspoken atheists such as [Richard Dawkins](https://iai.tv/video/the-gene-machine) and [Daniel Dennett](https://iai.tv/video/arc-of-life-daniel-dennett5). The Neo-Darwinists are the apparently unwitting heirs to Paley, filtered through Weismann. They’ve adopted a model of living beings whose purpose was to restore the creative monopoly of a supernatural designer.

As the theological dimension of this model receded from view, it attracted those keen to assume a godlike position of their own with regard to the living world. There’s a [eugenic logic](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/eugenics-genetics-evolution-modern-synthesis-darwin-riskin-1/) built into the idea that living beings are the passive objects of genetic programming and outside forces. Nearly all the Neo-Darwinists of the early through middle decades of the 20th century were fervent eugenicists. The British Neo-Darwinist biologist and eugenicist Julian Huxley rejoiced in the ability to, as he put it, become “[managing director](https://archive.org/details/NewBottlesForNewWine/page/n15/mode/2up)” of evolution. As a member of the British Eugenics Society, and later its president from 1959 to 1962, Huxley developed various proposals for eugenic programs. He sat, for instance, on the society’s Committee for Legalising Eugenic Sterilisation.

Crick is another example. “Let us take up this whole question of eugenics,” Crick exhorted his colleagues at a London conference in 1963. He observed, “I do not see why people should have the right to have children,” musing that it would be fairly simple for a government to sterilize the whole population with a chemical in the food supply, then reverse the effect only for those “[licensed to bear children](https://ia801301.us.archive.org/20/items/manhisfutureciba00wols/manhisfutureciba00wols.pdf).” These weren’t fringe views; eugenic theories and programs were mainstream in Neo-Darwinist biology. The idea of the fundamental passivity of organisms has also helped inform two centuries of environmental destruction, allowing people to regard the living world as so much raw material to manipulate and exploit for economic and imperial gain.

But [the Neo-Darwinist model has been teetering on its throne](http://[sitetree_link,id=]#https:/iai.tv/video/why-dawkins-is-wrong-denis-noble). As mentioned at the start of this essay, [challengers](https://iai.tv/articles/theres-more-to-evolution-than-genes-auid-3126) have been pointing out various way in which acquired changes can indeed be inherited. An example is [epigenetics](https://iai.tv/articles/organisms-are-musicians-not-machines-auid-3436): the inheritance of molecular structures outside the DNA that influence gene expression. Another is cultural inheritance, such as the innovatively hunting whales alluded to above. Biologists are also identifying many ways in which animal behaviors direct natural selection; the romantically selective birds and resourcefully warmth-seeking lizards mentioned above are examples. Perhaps pointing out the theology hidden at the heart of the Neo-Darwinist orthodoxy can help strengthen the case for a return toward a more Lamarckian approach to the living world in all its creative agency.
