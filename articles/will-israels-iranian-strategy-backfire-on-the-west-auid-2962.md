# Will Israel's Iranian strategy backfire on the West?

*Lebanese invasion could be Middle East's boiling point*

**By Christian Emery** | *4th October 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,069 words*

![Will Israel's Iranian strategy backfire on the West?](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Israel-Lebanon-Iran-and-the-nuclear-question.webp)

---

*In one fell swoop, Netanyahu appears to have silenced his domestic opposition, beheaded Hezbollah, and weathered Iran’s retaliation through Israeli escalation in Lebanon. But as Israel looks onto Iran, does this spell disaster for the Middle East and the West? Dr Christian Emery argues that this escalation is not intended to mark another protracted Lebanese occupation, but rather a potential prelude to Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities. If further Israeli escalation is not prevented, a disastrous regional war in the Middle East becomes ever more certain.*

Israel’s political and military leadership seems to have concluded that now is the optimal moment to inflict a decisive blow to its enemies and drastically diminish Iran’s power in the Middle East. Regardless of the factors behind this, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s belief that only overwhelming military dominance, perpetual occupation, and a willingness to escalate can create conditions for peace risks potentially dire consequences that threatens the long-term security he claims to seek.  And with strong retaliation against Iran virtually guaranteed after it [launched 200 missiles at Israel](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70w1j0l488o) in response to the assassination of Hezbollah leader Hasan Nasrallah, it is becoming increasingly difficult to see how this cycle of escalation can avoid triggering a major regional war—something everyone but Netanyahu and his extremist political allies seem eager to prevent.

\_\_\_

Until last week, Netanyahu looked to have finally reached his political dead-end in the face of intense criticism and daily protests from Israelis who condemned his inability to either eliminate Hamas after nearly 12 months of devastating conflict or secure the release of the 109 Israeli captives held in Gaza.

\_\_\_

What is clear is that Israel's unexpectedly swift neutralisation of Hezbollah's political and military leadership has temporarily improved Netanyahu's domestic standing. This itself offers a compelling explanation for why the IDF are now engaging in bloody [close quarter fighting](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/iran-says-it-has-finished-attack-israel-amid-fears-spreading-conflict-2024-10-02/) with Hezbollah fighters in southern Lebanon. Until last week, Netanyahu looked to have finally reached his [political dead-end](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/article/2024/sep/03/israel-protesters-netanyahu-gaza-war) in the face of intense criticism and daily protests from Israelis who condemned his inability to either eliminate Hamas after nearly 12 months of devastating conflict or secure the release of the 109 Israeli captives held in Gaza. He was also [at odds](https://abcnews.go.com/International/israeli-defense-officials-odds-netanyahu-hamas-hezbollah-cease/story?id=113733044) with his senior military commanders over the absence of a long-term strategy in Gaza.

Many of the divisions that have plagued Netanyahu have, for now, been set aside. Virtually all sections of Israeli society celebrated the ingenuity and ruthlessness the Israeli military demonstrated in launching its campaign against Hezbollah, viewing this as a pivotal moment in restoring Israel’s reputation for military superiority and intelligence gathering expertise – both pillars of deterrence widely perceived to have been critically weakened by the October 7th massacre. By authorising the IDF’s [carefully orchestrated](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/sep/30/deep-intelligence-penetration-enabled-israel-to-kill-hassan-nasrallah) operation against Hezbollah, Netanyahu has placated the Israeli military establishment while garnering greater tolerance for his lack of strategy in Gaza. Political leaders [across the spectrum](https://www.timesofisrael.com/politicians-across-spectrum-praise-assassination-of-hezbollah-chief-nasrallah/) are either praising Israel’s military successes in Lebanon or refraining from openly criticising Netanyahu’s decisions. With Israel now facing direct attacks from Iran, the Israeli prime minister can count on a united front.

But the blurring of Netanyahu’s personal and political motives for keeping Israel on a war footing with long-standing bi-partisan national security concerns, has made it difficult to predict Israel’s strategic intentions and risk tolerance. Some analysts suggest that Netanyahu had [called Iran’s bluff](https://www.theatlantic.com/international/archive/2024/09/iran-hezbollah-netanyahu/680070/), confident that Ayatollah Khamenei would not risk striking Israel to avenge Nasrallah, particularly when its regional proxies Hamas and Hezbollah had been so seriously weakened. Yet [others argue](https://www.theatlantic.com/international/archive/2024/09/iran-hezbollah-netanyahu/680070/) that the Israeli cabinet are actively provoking Iran because they see a window of opportunity to strike at Iran’s nuclear facilities. [Iran exercised restraint after the killing of Hamas political leader Ismail Haniyeh in Tehran, in late July,](http://iai.tv/articles/iran-has-fallen-for-israels-trap-auid-2813) but the assassination of Nasrallah was clearly the last straw.

It seems unlikely that Israeli intelligence would have concluded that Iran would not respond with attacks on Israel. After all, they did so back in April after Israel assassinated a senior commander in an embassy annexe in Damascus. Therefore, it’s not surprising that Iran would seek to bolster its increasingly fragile deterrence strategy after Israel assassinated the single most important leader within their regional alliance network, [wounded](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/irans-ambassador-lebanon-injured-by-pager-explosion-2024-09-17/) their ambassador to Lebanon, and [killed an Iranian Revolutionary Guards deputy commander](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2024/sep/29/middle-east-crisis-live-israel-bombardment-lebanon-hezbollah-hassan-nasrallah-latest) in the same strike that killed Nasrallah.

\_\_\_

For several decades, this strategy, along with a measured yet persistent advancement towards the nuclear threshold and a strong desire in the West to avoid another catastrophic conflict in the Middle East, has effectively deterred direct attacks on Iranian territory.

\_\_\_

However, it’s also likely that Israeli military and intelligence services assessed Iran’s ballistic missile barrage would cause limited damage, either by design or due to the effectiveness of Israeli air defences. But make no mistake: Iran intended to do real damage with these strikes; this was, after all one escalatory rung up from their previous strikes, and they could not afford the humiliation of having all their ballistic missiles harmlessly intercepted. Iran felt it had no choice but to follow through on its vow to avenge the string of Israeli attacks and repair its image amongst its regional partners. However, Iran, led by a generation shaped by the devastating Iran-Iraq war of the 1980s, remains unwilling to engage in a major conflict with Israel, fully aware it would fare far worse in such a confrontation. This is exactly why Iran's national security strategy relies on regional proxies to entangle its adversaries in low-intensity conflicts far from its own territory. For several decades, this strategy, along with a measured yet persistent advancement towards the nuclear threshold and a strong desire in the West to avoid another catastrophic conflict in the Middle East, has effectively deterred direct attacks on Iranian territory. However, events in Israel over the past 12 months have shifted this dynamic. Iran's leadership may have concluded that Israel's offensive against Hezbollah was a precursor to premeditated strikes on their own country, leading them to believe that further restraint would offer no advantage.

If Iran’s missile strikes were effectively factored into Israel’s planning for attacks in Lebanon, did Netanyahu consider this a price worth paying to establish a buffer zone in southern Lebanon that would limit Hezbollah's operations? [Or were the attacks also about preparing the ground for a major offensive against Iran after provoking it into attacking Israel first?](http://iai.tv/articles/strategic-ambiguity-doesnt-work-in-a-multipolar-world-auid-2902)

Resolving the situation in northern Israel is unquestionably a political priority for Netanyahu. The displacement of tens of thousands of Israeli citizens poses an intolerable threat, and the mounting pressure to restore security in northern Israel is undeniable. The stated goal is to push Hezbollah forces back across the Litani River, marking the 'Blue Line' established by the [UN resolution that ended the 2006 war.](https://apnews.com/article/unsc-resolution-1701-lebanon-israel-hezbollah-addf018ee9da719329e1726f7156d456) Success would allow the 80,000 Israelis who fled Hezbollah's rocket attacks to return home with a renewed sense of safety.

However, this is [not the first time](https://theconversation.com/israel-has-invaded-lebanon-six-times-in-the-past-50-years-a-timeline-of-events-240157) Israel has entered Lebanon to dismantle a terrorist group it viewed as an existential threat, seeking to weaken its state sponsor and secure northern Israel. History shows that while massive retaliation can weaken militant groups, it often paves the way for the emergence of even more radical factions. Without a complementary diplomatic strategy— an asset Israeli politics and society is now fundamentally unable to produce —the outcome has typically been widespread destruction and enormous loss of life without any meaningful improvement in the security of Israeli citizens. On the other hand, when Israel does engage in a diplomatic process, as it did with [Egypt in the late 1970s](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/oct/01/how-the-yom-kippur-war-led-to-a-new-order-within-israel-and-beyond) spurred by the shock of the Yom Kippur War, it can lead to a significant reduction in the threat of inter-state conflict.

Israel has decimated Hezbollah’s political and military leadership, leaving the organisation struggling to rebuild its command-and-control structures. However, it doesn’t appear that [Israel is mobilising enough ground forces](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/oct/01/israel-lebanon-attack-hezbollah-ground-operation-war-latest) to occupy large parts of Lebanon or force the creation of a new government without Hezbollah (as it is attempting to do with Hamas in Gaza). Israeli leaders may hope Hezbollah’s internal opponents will seize this moment to weaken its dominance over Lebanon’s military and political affairs, but that is highly unlikely. Lebanon is in its [weakest state](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/article/2024/aug/14/we-cant-handle-a-war-poor-and-broken-beiruts-residents-dread-the-arrival-of-fresh-conflict) in decades—broken economically, politically, and socially. Any vacuum left by Hezbollah’s setbacks is more likely to fuel further instability and dysfunction. Ultimately, without a clear political strategy, this operation offers no realistic path to addressing the deeper issues Hezbollah and other militant anti-Israeli organisations have long exploited in Lebanon, which continue to undermine Israel's own security.

Israel believes it can deal Hezbollah a crushing blow, and maybe even force it to the negotiating table, although this seems extraordinarily unlikely given how many of its members have been left either dead or crippled over the last few weeks. But it doesn't appear to be planning a prolonged military operation in Lebanon, which raises the question: is the primary goal to cripple Hezbollah's fighting capabilities to prevent it from being used as an Iranian tool of retaliation ahead of imminent strikes against Iran’s nuclear facilities?

If this is Netanyahu’s grand strategy, it is understandable that he thinks now is the moment to set it in motion, given the public mood in Israel, his temporarily revived domestic popularity, the lack of any restraining influence in Washington, and the apparent weakness of Iran’s regional proxies. However, it would be a profoundly misguided move with likely disastrous consequences for both the region, the global economy, and ultimately Israeli security.

[This is why President Biden](http://iai.tv/articles/the-myth-of-american-decline-auid-2731) has already [ruled out](https://apnews.com/live/israel-lebanon-ground-operation-updates#00000192-4e21-d827-a9bb-cf3b0ada0000) any support for attacking Iran’s nuclear facilities. Israel probably does just about possess the capability to do the job by itself, but there would be no guarantees of success, and [the risks would be enormous](https://thebulletin.org/2024/10/iran-vs-israel-redux-the-enormous-difficulties-and-ramifications-if-israel-attacks-irans-nuclear-sites/).

Iran already possesses the knowledge and capability to develop nuclear weapons, and that expertise cannot simply be bombed out of existence. While military strikes may temporarily delay its nuclear program, Iran’s leaders would very likely conclude that building a nuclear weapons capability rapidly would be the only way to deter more attacks. There is a widespread belief that this would lead to the [rapid proliferation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/sep/21/crown-prince-confirms-saudi-arabia-seek-nuclear-arsenal-iran-develops-one#:~:text=Questioned%20by%20Fox%20News%20about,will%20have%20to%20get%20one%E2%80%9D.) of nuclear weapons in the region. Without IAEA monitoring, coupled with Russia's diplomatic shielding in the UN Security Council and Iran's recent advancements in centrifuge technology, Tehran could rapidly achieve breakout capability at more secure, undisclosed locations. The IAEA's [most recent report](https://isis-online.org/isis-reports/detail/analysis-of-iaea-iran-verification-and-monitoring-report-february-2024) highlighted that the lack of monitoring equipment, especially since 2022, has created uncertainty about whether Iran has already covertly diverted centrifuges.

The escalating conflict could also severely impact the global economy. Iran might block the Strait of Hormuz, pulling the U.S. into the conflict. In response, Israel and the U.S. could target Iranian oil refineries, leading to retaliatory strikes on regional infrastructure and U.S. forces, as seen during [Donald Trump’s maximum pressure campaign](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2019/07/18/trumps-reckless-bludgeoning-of-the-iranian-economy-wont-force-it-to-capitulate-but-shows-that-america-still-wields-massive-power/). In the face of such disastrous consequences, Israel’s international support could buckle. Russia, a key Iranian ally, has enough challenges to contend with without taking on the role of security guarantor, but it’s increasing need for Iranian drones to fight its disastrous war in Ukraine conflict, and desire to strengthen ties with the 'global south', has forced Moscow to take a much more critical stance against Israel. It would not be easy for Putin to stand back and allow Iran to be continually bombed by Israel.

None of this would help return the Israeli hostages languishing in pitiless conditions in Gaza. And as international attention shifts further away from the excruciating sufferings in Gaza, the more Netanyahu and his ultra-nationalist coalition partners will have free reign to pulverise the Gazan population and extend Israel’s occupation of the West Bank.

Netanyahu appears highly confident that he has Israel’s enemies on the ropes and will undoubtedly be tempted to deliver what he thinks will be a knockout blow to Iran’s ability to threaten Israel. As desirable as this might be, his thinking merely continues his decades-long effort to convince the Israeli electorate that only he can save the Jewish State and that political compromises are neither necessary nor achievable requirements for peace. The difference now is that he seems willing to risk almost anything to prove his point.
