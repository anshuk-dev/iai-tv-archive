# Iris Murdoch and why the West’s spiritual crisis won’t be solved by religion

*Transcendence without theism*

**By Miles Leeson** | *20th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,720 words*

![Iris Murdoch and why the West’s spiritual crisis won’t be solved by religion](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Miles-Leeson-Iris-Murdoch.webp)

---

*Belief in God is on the rise among young people in the West. As many seek meaning beyond digital life and consumer culture, old theologies are in vogue. But Iris Murdoch warned against seeing religious faith as the only route to a spiritual life. In this article, Miles Leeson argues for Murdoch’s third way—grounded in transcendence without dogma—as a solution to the West’s spiritual crisis.*

News stories focusing on the rise of religious belief, and crucially religious activity, in the UK are perhaps not surprising. [The think tank Theos recently highlighted](https://www.theosthinktank.co.uk/comment/2025/05/13/why-are-young-people-flocking-to-religion) an article in *The Times* suggesting that those in Gen Z were less likely to identify as atheists than their parents, and the most recent YouGov data show that belief in God for those aged between 18 and 24 has risen from 16% in August 2021 to 45% in January of this year. While this may seem astonishing, it could very well be linked to the economic and political factors that all of us, even beyond the age of 24, have to encounter in our daily lives. The constant drive toward less personal interaction through automation and AI may well be a significant factor; if we do communicate, how much of this is undertaken remotely? That “[doomscrolling](https://iai.tv/articles/the-price-of-distraction-auid-1785)” was added to the OED in 2023 perhaps tells its own story.

But does belief, or some sort of fuzzy inner feeling or desire—being “spiritual” rather than religious—translate into outward action? For some, yes. But for a great deal of people, it seems that lip service and lethargy toward action are the preferred response. We want to be good, or at least to be better in some way, but do we make the time and put in the effort to progress in the moral (let alone the spiritual) life? The secular age we live in does not tend to promote this kind of growth, placing far more emphasis on consumption and immediate satisfaction. If there is a crisis, then, we need figures to turn to for inspiration, and Iris Murdoch can be one of them: as she tells us, “we are anxiety-ridden animals.” In her essay “Against Dryness” in 1961, she asks:

What have we lost here? And what have we perhaps never had? We have suffered a general loss of concepts, the loss of a moral and political vocabulary. We no longer use a spread-out substantial picture of the manifold virtues of man and society. We no longer see man against a background of values, of realities, which transcend him. We picture man as a brave naked will surrounded by an easily comprehended empirical world. For the hard idea of truth we have substituted a facile idea of sincerity.

Which has as much bearing on us in 2025 as it did at the beginning of the ’60s. Our moral vocabulary has become so distorted that we have no real way of discussing the moral life unless we commit ourselves to a new form of understanding. For that to happen, she argues, we need to step outside of ourselves and see the other person as unique and worthy of love, [which in itself is an act of the imagination](https://iai.tv/articles/imagination-is-not-an-escape-from-reality-auid-3002). But this is of necessity hard, daily work. Perhaps we should turn not just to Western theological sources of inspiration—although Murdoch believes that those of us who live in Western Europe and North America are so embedded in this tradition that it is difficult to reimagine ourselves outside of it—but beyond to other world religions, and particularly to Buddhism.

\_\_\_

Murdoch’s answer is to reintroduce transcendence without reintroducing theism.

\_\_\_

Murdoch herself didn’t believe in a personal God, but she recognized that losing knowledge of particular elements of Christian belief—the stories, the artwork, the buildings—would cause substantial harm to Western culture. Our contemporary landscape is haunted by these issues, and the real danger, as Murdoch saw it, was that humans can make themselves into self-sufficient moral agents. The shallowness of this mindset, one which is untethered from cultural history, is the clear outcome of this paradigm shift. Her constant refrain of “How can we become morally better?” is a key element of everything that she published. The main thrust of her philosophical work was to show how we could return to “the Good” rather than a dogmatic moral framework. A rediscovery of the sacredness of each one of us as individuals, which does not require a monotheistic approach.

Murdoch’s answer is to reintroduce transcendence without reintroducing theism. Against both nihilism and moral relativism, she insists on the existence of the Good—a Platonic concept that replaces the personal God of Christianity as the center of moral reality. In *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992), Murdoch declares, “The Good is the reality of which God was a symbol.” The Good is not an entity but an orientation; it is “a single perfect source” of value that exists beyond all human purposes. Like Plato’s Form of the Good, it is both abstract and deeply intimate: we can never possess it, only turn toward it—she was very fond of using Plato’s allegory of the cave for this purpose.

\_\_\_

The moral life is not an act of self-creation but of attention to what lies beyond the self.

\_\_\_

This redefinition is central to her response to the spiritual crisis of her time, and of ours. The “death of God,” she believes, is not the death of the sacred, but its transfiguration. What is lost is the supernatural image of a personal deity; what remains, and must be rediscovered, is the reality of the Good as the transcendent ground of moral life. Murdoch thus restores to secular ethics what contemporary philosophy had lost after the two world wars: reverence. The moral life is not an act of self-creation but of attention to what lies beyond the self. The Good, though impersonal, demands humility, patience, and unselfing—the virtues once cultivated by religion.

Murdoch envisages the future of Western religion as “a cultivation of the mystical Christ … a Buddhist-style survival of Christianity.” The mystical Christ is “unresurrected but mysteriously present,” a living symbol of unselfish love who “travels with us” even after the personal God has faded from view: Murdoch claimed to have been haunted by the figure of Christ throughout her life. Murdoch wanted Christ to become a “Buddha of the West,” a great teacher who points toward the Good, but without the baggage of dogma, and the notion of a personal God. A good man who can show us a path toward enlightenment.

This is all very well, we may say, but what of the practical implications? Is this on an individual basis, or should it be communitarian? A personal moment of enlightenment, or a social movement that draws people together? Murdoch has less to say about social groups than we might expect; her earlier commitment to (and later suspicion of) mass movements may have something to do with this.

At the heart of Murdoch’s moral psychology lies the concept of attention, adapted from Simone Weil. Attention is the capacity to look beyond the distortions of the self, to perceive reality truly. It is, according to Murdoch, “a just and loving gaze directed upon an individual reality.” [Attention, then, is not merely cognitive](https://iai.tv/articles/why-attention-matters-for-morality-auid-2711); it is spiritual. It is the secular form of prayer, the discipline by which the soul turns toward the Good. “Prayer,” she writes, “is properly not petition, but an attention to God which is a form of love.” In a post-religious world, that same act of loving attention can be directed toward reality itself, toward the faces of others, or toward the beauty of art and nature: all of these elements feature throughout her fictional works.

\_\_\_

Murdoch’s remedy for the West’s spiritual crisis is not institutional but interior. Where the churches, temples or mosques fail, attention can succeed; where belief collapses, love of the Good can endure.

\_\_\_

Through attention, Murdoch reclaims the contemplative dimension of religion for a secular ethics. The moral task becomes not to assert the self but to *see*—to apprehend what is, rather than what one desires. The unselfing that results is both ethical and metaphysical: a movement out of illusion into truth. In this sense, Murdoch’s remedy for the West’s spiritual crisis is not institutional but interior. Where the churches, temples or mosques fail, attention can succeed; where belief collapses, love of the Good can endure.

Murdoch’s response to the current Western crisis can be read as both conservative *and* radical. On the one hand, she restores to modern thought the classical conviction that moral life requires transcendence. Like Augustine or Plato, she insists that virtue is orientation toward the Good. On the other hand, she accomplishes this within an atheistic framework, without appeal to divine authority or metaphysical reassurance. In this respect, her philosophy represents a distinctive third path between [secular relativism](https://iai.tv/articles/against-relativism-auid-352) and religious orthodoxy. She neither returns to dogma nor abandons the sacred; instead, she reimagines spirituality as “attention to reality.”

As I and my co-editor Frances White observe in the introduction to *[Iris Murdoch and the Western Theological Imagination](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-92261-9)*, Murdoch’s thought “draws together a multitude of different thinkers on the subject, and her task of considering how metaphysics can be a guide to morals in a post-Christian age makes her a compelling voice today.” Her work asks us to consider that the spiritual crisis of the West is not the end of religion but its metamorphosis. In the closing pages of *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals*, Murdoch envisions the moral life as a pilgrimage without guarantee. We must live, she writes, “without consolation and without despair.” This is her final vision of the spiritual condition of the modern West: a world without divine certainty but not without grace.

So the sacred persists, transformed into moral attention (as exemplified in Murdoch’s philosophy), artistic creation (as detailed in her novels and writings on art), and love of the Good. The [Western spiritual crisis](https://iai.tv/video/on-the-nature-of-reality-rowan-williams-and-iain-mcgilchrist), then, can be seen as not simply a decline but a purification—a movement from the external to the internal, from belief to vision. For Murdoch, the task is not to restore an old theology but to learn again how to look. To see truly, to love justly, and to turn the self toward the Good—this, for Murdoch, is the new form of holiness possible in a secular age. It is the West’s path out of its spiritual crisis, and perhaps, its path to spiritual maturity.
