# What students learn is more important than how

*The key driver of progress often missed*

**By Michael Hand** | *3rd March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,559 words*

![What students learn is more important than how](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-end-of-evidence-based-education-michael-hand-article.webp)

---

*Since the Enlightenment we have valued having more knowledge, regardless of what the content of that knowledge is. However, the realisation that all knowledge is perspective-driven has made what is taught in schools a political choice. What to teach students and our children is as important, if not more important, than how we teach it. With complaints from politicians and parents about what is being taught in schools and universities, now is the time for a debate to be had. But how should we decide upon what is taught? The evidence-based revolution in education promised data could prove the best and most effective teaching methods. However, argues philosopher of education Michael Hand, that evidence doesn’t tell us what we should teach in the first place. Deciding which knowledge is worth having is fundamentally a philosophical, value-laden question.*

Evidence-based education is all the rage. The Labour Government has pledged to fund ‘[evidence-based early-language interventions in primary schools](https://labour.org.uk/change/break-down-barriers-to-opportunity/)’. Education Secretary Bridget Phillipson is ‘[doubling down on evidence-based training](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/education-secretary-speech-on-new-era-of-school-standards)’ for teachers. The findings of the Curriculum and Assessment Review, due to be published later this year, will be ‘[rigorously evidence and data informed](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/66d196b7d107658faec7e3db/Curriculum_and_assessment_review_-_aims_terms_of_reference_and_working_principles.pdf)’. But just how far can evidence take us in our thinking about education?

The idea of evidence-based education is simple and compelling. The practice of education should be informed by the best available evidence on how to facilitate learning. As David Hargreaves put it, in an influential lecture to the Teacher Training Agency in 1996, what teachers needs is ‘[evidence about what works with whom under what conditions and with what effects](https://eppi.ioe.ac.uk/cms/Portals/0/PDF%20reviews%20and%20summaries/TTA%20Hargreaves%20lecture.pdf)’.

\_\_\_

Empirical evidence can tell us many useful things about how to ensure that all pupils have access to the curriculum. But it can tell us precisely nothing about what should be on the curriculum in the first place.

\_\_\_

Hargreaves was surely right. In the 30 years since his lecture, significant progress has been made on building a body of research evidence on what works in education, and on making that evidence base available to teachers. The Education Endowment Foundation’s [Teaching and Learning Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit), for example, provides user-friendly summaries of the research supporting such pedagogical approaches as [individualised instruction](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/individualised-instruction), [mastery learning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning) and [peer tutoring](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring).

But there is a downside to the current enthusiasm for evidence-based education. Too narrow a focus on *how* to facilitate learning diverts our attention from the equally important, and equally vexed, question of *what* learning we should facilitate. Empirical evidence can tell us many useful things about how to ensure that all pupils have access to the curriculum. But it can tell us precisely nothing about what should be on the curriculum in the first place.

\_\_\_

Education can and should be based on evidence, but it is necessarily based on values too.

\_\_\_

Questions about the content of education are questions of *value*. They are questions about the kinds of knowledge and understanding, and the range of skills, competences, attitudes and commitments, we judge to be worth having. And they are questions on which people sharply disagree. Some think schools should focus on vocational preparation, on equipping young people with the specific and transferable skills needed to earn a living. Others favour an education that prepares pupils for the life of the mind, acquainting them with ‘[the best which has been thought and said](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Culture_and_Anarchy/Preface)’. Yet others take what matters to be the state of children’s souls, so charge educators with cultivating the beliefs and virtues of a particular religion.

The disagreements here are *philosophical*, not empirical. They cannot be settled by carrying out experiments or observing lessons or analysing exam results. To make progress with them we need to engage in rational argument about what is worth learning and what is not, and about the relative importance of different kinds of knowledge, competence and commitment. We need to do some [philosophy of education](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/education-philosophy/).

There is no avoiding these questions of value. They are foundational to the enterprise of education – arguably *more* foundational that the questions of pedagogy that preoccupy the champions of evidence-based learning. They demand serious and sustained attention from teachers and parents, from policymakers and academics, and from the citizens whose taxes pay for public education. Education can and should be based on evidence, but it is necessarily based on values too. We must not shrink from the task of working out what those values should be.

There are two ways of trying to evade this responsibility. The first is specific to teachers. It is sometimes suggested that teachers can be spared the trouble of attending to questions of value because their job is to deliver the curriculum, not design it. The government stipulates [what should be taught in schools](https://www.gov.uk/national-curriculum) and employs teachers to get on with teaching it. Teachers are not paid to second-guess the values on which the curriculum rests.

But this is a hopelessly impoverished view of what it is to be a teacher. Teachers are professionals, not technicians – which is to say that their expertise is not limited to the technique of teaching. A hallmark of the professions is some degree of autonomy, some measure of control over the ends of a professional activity as well as the means of pursuing them. In the case of education, what teachers bring to the table is privileged insight into the aspirations and aptitudes of pupils, into the nature of schools and the unpredictability of classroom interactions, and into the challenge of converting abstract aims into concrete lesson plans. It is certainly not for teachers alone to work out the guiding values of education, but they are both distinctively qualified and professionally obliged to participate in that endeavour.

Moreover, it is not possible to point to the National Curriculum and claim that it somehow obviates the need for teacher reflection on educational aims. For one thing, the National Curriculum is a statutory minimum: it purports to provide only ‘[an outline of core knowledge](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-framework-for-key-stages-1-to-4/the-national-curriculum-in-england-framework-for-key-stages-1-to-4)’, not a comprehensive account of the learning schools should facilitate. For another, academies and free schools, which now account for some [43% of primary schools and 82% of secondary schools](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/school-pupils-and-their-characteristics) in England, do not have to follow the National Curriculum. For another still, Ofsted judges each individual school on ‘[the scope of the curriculum, including how carefully the leaders responsible for the curriculum in the school have thought about what end points the curriculum is building towards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-handbook-eif/school-inspection-handbook-for-september-2023#evaluating-the-curriculum)’.

The second way of trying to evade responsibility for judgments of educational value is to reclassify them as matters of consumer choice. Governments, and the citizens they represent, need not decide what it is worthwhile for children to learn. They can, instead, provide public funding for schools of various different kinds, founded on different configurations of values, and invite parents to choose the kind of school they prefer. The vexed question of what learning we want to facilitate can be sidestepped, rather than tackled, through the creation of a public education market.

I do not wish to deny that there is room for more than one type of school in a well-designed education system, or that there is a role for parental choice in the allocation of children to schools. But, as a general strategy for dealing with the question of what should be on the curriculum, an unregulated education market has little to commend it. The question is too important – the stakes are too high – for public deliberation to be set aside in favour of consumer choice. Of the sharply differing views people hold on the aims of education, by no means all are defensible.

Consider, for example, the view that educators should cultivate in children the beliefs and virtues of a particular religion. The difficulty here is that religious beliefs are matters of faith, not knowledge, so imparting them to others cannot proceed by the usual methods of teaching. Children cannot be presented with compelling evidence or decisive arguments for the truth of a religion. Instead, the beliefs must be imparted by non-rational means of persuasion – by some form of manipulation, pressure, deception or coercion. But schools prepared to resort to these means are institutions of indoctrination, not education.

Or again, consider those who favour a traditional, knowledge-based curriculum, with no room for such new-fangled, ‘soft skill’ subjects as [Citizenship](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-citizenship-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-citizenship-programmes-of-study-for-key-stages-3-and-4) and [Relationships, Sex and Health Education](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education). The Gradgrindian imperative to ‘[teach these boys and girls nothing but facts](http://www.literaturepage.com/read/dickens-hard-times-1.html)’, and the drily academic curriculum to which it gives rise, leaves children woefully ill-equipped for the demands of adult life. We neglect at their peril and our own the skills and attitudes they need to be engaged citizens, industrious workers, attentive partners and responsible parents.

So hard thinking about the aims of education is neither something teachers can be spared nor something consumer choice can replace. All of us with a stake in public education must attend as closely to its value base as we do to its evidence base. For teachers, that means organising as a profession and making their voices heard in debates about the curriculum. For parents, it means refusing to be cast as consumers in a public education market and demanding a school system in which every child acquires knowledge, skills and attitudes worth having.

Questions of value are by no means easy to answer, but nor are they immune to rational examination and discussion. Progress can be made, and there are few questions on which progress matters more than the question of what children should learn in school.
