# Trump tariffs have pros and cons, like everything else

*We need to be more realistic about Trump tariffs*

**By Dmitry Grozoubinski** | *5th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,599 words*

![Trump tariffs have pros and cons, like everything else](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/trump-tarrifs-piece.webp)

---

*Trump's tariffs have shaken the West to its core. They have been met with widespread condemnation as economically illiterate, but tariffs can work, as can free trade, and this story is as far from black and white as possible. Dmitry Grozoubinski, former Australian trade negotiator and diplomat, argues that politicians and journalists have done us a disservice by simplifying tariffs and trade deals into meaningless sound bites. In trade, there are no universal truths, and if we are to have a grown up discussion of global trade, lofty ideals and easy lies can only take us so far.*

As a young and naive government official just starting out in public service, I kept expecting to stumble across what I thought of as ‘the Room’. In my mind, somewhere in the machinery was a glorious chamber full of experts, synthesizing information from a hundred sources, with the latest academic findings at their fingertips and data scrolling on their screens. This hypercompetent chamber is where I imagined policy was crafted, debated, refined and honed - steered by the strategic vision of elected leaders.

To be sure, there are policy areas where this isn’t too far from the truth. The people balancing the electrical grid or planning military operations seem to have rooms like this. It just turns out that as soon as a policy area becomes politicised, nuance and expertise take a back seat. Half the time the government is just kind of doing stuff, chasing the media cycle, responding to squeaky wheels or effective lobbyists and generally making it up as they go along. There are certainly very serious and highly capable people involved, but they aren’t always heeded and often get shouted down if the public conversation is flowing in a different direction.

I say this not to dampen enthusiasm for government or build cynicism - but to stress that how we talk about things, and how we allow our political leaders to frame questions, matters far more than it should - even outside election years or referendums.

This brings us to trade policy and the current chaos. Let’s recap where we are and how we got here. [Candidate](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/02/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-imposes-tariffs-on-imports-from-canada-mexico-and-china/) Trump campaigned and won promising to slap 10-20% tariffs on all imports into the US from anywhere in the world, and 60% tariffs on imports from China. To put those figures into context, trade geeks will generally say that a 5% tariff is a nuisance, 5-20% is trade distorting (will change purchasing decisions and impact prices) and anything above that is basically trade prohibitive - no one is buying something that comes with a 60% markup at the border.

\_\_\_

At every stage of this rollercoaster he has vacillated wildly between vowing that the tariffs were permanent and intended to be revenue raising and job-creation tools, and bragging that they are merely negotiating leverage to bludgeon concessions out of his counterparties.

\_\_\_

 Upon actually being sworn in as President, he ordered the Secretary of Commerce to prepare a report on why the US keeps having trade deficits (buying more from some countries than it sells to them) and what he can do about it, which is due on April 1st. In the interim, he proceeded to threaten and then rapidly withdraw [tariff](https://www.ft.com/content/4a09c580-055b-4252-9994-8961bcba53e9) threats against (in rapid succession) Colombia, Mexico and Canada, while actually introducing more modest tariffs of [10% on China.](https://www.google.com/url?client=internal-element-cse&cx=015872801169957995706:fv8zq1se9vo&q=https://iai.tv/video/trading-with-the-enemy&sa=U&ved=2ahUKEwi8ytrBtqyLAxV1XEEAHU6pPAwQFnoECAMQAg&usg=AOvVaw0EGkC_4JrKFwFwuqPeXQDw)

At every stage of this rollercoaster he has vacillated wildly between vowing that the tariffs were permanent and intended to be revenue raising and job-creation tools, and bragging that they are merely negotiating leverage to bludgeon concessions out of his counterparties. There has been no reliable way to predict, at the onset of a phone call between President Trump and the leader of a tariff-threatened nation, whether the result of the call would be an end to the tariffs, a suspension of them, their implementation, or their escalation.

When challenged on any of this, the President and his supporters argue - not without cause, that he is simply doing what he promised to do during the campaign. Unlike some of his other recent moves, like apparently deciding to dismantle USAID, tariffs were a frequent theme of his electioneering and not something he is springing on the country out of the blue. Presumably, the argument goes, if the American people had an issue with their leader imposing tariffs and weaponising US trade policy, they’d have voted for someone else.

This is where we return to language, and lies. Consider this thought experiment: would the current situation be happening if ‘tariffs’ were called ‘taxes’. The US President has said that ‘tariffs’ are the “most beautiful word”. I’m sure he means it. A simple quirk of trade policy nomenclature has allowed him to consistently describe a tax increase on Americans as if it were a levy on foreigners. He was able to run an entire campaign with a tax increase as its centerpiece, without ever using the word tax (except to claim that tariff revenue would let him cut various other taxes).

There is a real discussion to be had about tariffs - just as there are about optimal settings for any taxes, regulations and procedures. Tariffs are not magical or sui generis. If you charge people for bringing in certain products from certain countries then that raises their price. If consumers continue to buy them, it brings in some revenue for your treasury while decreasing the amount those consumers have to spend on other things. If there are domestic or lower tariff foreign alternatives available, it makes those more attractive to your consumers. It’s all boring, dry economics stuff - choosing between competing interests.

\_\_\_

Safety versus cost, efficiency versus rigor, a thousand things to balance and always, always choices to be made.

\_\_\_

The same is true in just about any area of trade, and I suspect (though they aren’t my field) most areas of public policy more generally. More heavily regulating your industries can improve their environmental footprint, but raising prices hurts their international competitiveness and could drive jobs overseas. Subsidising an industry helps it grow, but means taking money away from other projects to focus on a governmentally selected champion. Safety versus cost, efficiency versus rigor, a thousand things to balance and always, always choices to be made.

The challenge we’re facing is a political climate in which governments loathe to admit their policy choices *are* choices, and a media environment too easily distracted toward horserace punditry over how a message will play - in lieu of explaining the policy choice that message is obscuring.

This is not, it is important to say, a uniquely Trumpian phenomenon. Well before he rode down a golden elevator to announce his first candidacy, political figures all over the world peddled oversimplified nonsense, often on both sides of a given trade issue. Scroll back through the archives and opinion pages of your favourite newspaper and read what was being said about literally any trade agreement - each somehow painted as simultaneously an economically transformational miracle and a calamitous act of serfdom doomed to impoverish the nation and leave it at the mercy of foreign villains.

\_\_\_

What should have been a detailed debate about the choice facing the country was instead largely a spectacle of personalities and bravado, with wildly exaggerated claims exchanged back and forth with increasing disregard for reality.

\_\_\_

The UK itself experienced this phenomenon on steroids during the Brexit debate, and somehow even more so during its long, agonizing implementation phase. The decision to leave the European Union was a complex one, but there were tradeoffs to be made and trade is perhaps where they were clearest: regain those independent legislative and regulatory powers pooled as part of the Union at the cost of losing frictionless and guaranteed access to European markets.

There is no ‘right’ answer there because, like most policy choices, it is fundamentally a question of balancing competing and ultimately subjective priorities. The public put its trust in national institutions, national leaders and the fourth estate to present the choice on offer and the tradeoffs it entailed - and were let down. What should have been a detailed debate about the choice facing the country was instead largely a spectacle of personalities and bravado, with wildly exaggerated claims exchanged back and forth with increasing disregard for reality.

\_\_\_

It also seems unfairly ambitious to expect leading scholars who have devoted their lives to studying data or modelling economic phenomena to moonlight as brilliant communicators able to go toe to toe with professional polemicists who practice sophistry for a living.

\_\_\_

For our representative democracies to function, the public must have some idea of what the policy choices facing their leaders are - so that they can evaluate the direction chosen against their values and interests. In complex and counterintuitive policy areas like trade, so easily demagogged or misrepresented, the onus falls on everyone from academics to journalists to help the public navigate the confusion.

That’s a tall order. No one has quite figured out how to effectively deliver nuance and complexity in a media environment where attention spans are measured in TikTok increments and YouTube shorts. It also seems unfairly ambitious to expect leading scholars who have devoted their lives to studying data or modelling economic phenomena to moonlight as brilliant communicators able to go toe to toe with professional polemicists who practice sophistry for a living.

Perhaps a place to start is just to remind the public, again and again, that all policy is about choices and to demand at the top of our lungs that our leaders, past, present and prospective, spell out the choices their ideas entail.
