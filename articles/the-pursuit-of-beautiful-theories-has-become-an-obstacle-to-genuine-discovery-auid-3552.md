# The pursuit of beautiful theories is an obstacle to discovery

*Science must pursue ugly theories, not beautiful ones*

**By Jane Forsey** | *16th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,324 words*

![The pursuit of beautiful theories is an obstacle to discovery](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-pursuit-of-beautiful-theories.webp)

---

*Our reverence for beauty encodes a fantasy that reality is ordered and familiar, fully accessible to reason and observation. Philosopher **Jane Forsey** here argues that letting ugliness and disorder into our theories and aesthetic experiences means confronting the world’s strangeness and our irredeemable disconnection from it. And, Forsey argues, it also gives us better ways of understanding reality and intervening in it.*

Consider the blobfish. Named the [ugliest animal in the world by the Ugly Animal Preservation Society](https://uglyanimalsoc.com/), it is safe to say that many people find the creature to be hideous. It is also safe to say that the appellation “ugly” is the result of an aesthetic judgement about the blobfish, or the product of an aesthetic experience of its features. Why then does philosophical aesthetics have such a difficult time incorporating experiences of the ugly, the horrific, the unpleasant, and the banal within the scope of its theories? [Investigating this question](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/aesthetic-question-9781350552319/) has led me to consider the long tradition of my discipline that has made beauty, and with it aesthetic pleasure, the “default setting” for aesthetic theory at large. And I have sought an alternative way of considering what aesthetic experience amounts to, and how it can be expanded.

But first, beauty and pleasure. From the early Greek identification of [beauty with truth](https://iai.tv/video/truth-beauty-and-other-scientific-misconceptions-lisa-randall) and goodness, there has been a strong—if implicit—moral dimension at the heart of aesthetic theory. Beauty has been a call to order that has the power to console, heal, or reconcile us in an attempt to achieve harmony with the world and each other. It is in the contemplation of beauty that we are said to escape the horrors of the world and rise above them in a sort of pleasurable freedom—as though beauty shows us that we can be better selves, or offers us a [glimpse of some kind of transcendence](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44477/ode-on-a-grecian-urn) that we yearn for. But the history of aesthetics brings with it a superficial reassurance, a false tranquility, and an empty hope. Let us instead reconsider: freedom, as we have learned thanks to the Existentialists, can be disconcerting, even terrifying. [The absurd is just around the corner](https://iai.tv/articles/camus-and-the-absurdity-of-freedom-auid-2629). And these days, the idea of a transcendent order must surely be seen as little more than a fantasy.

\_\_\_

Clarity and familiarity may be pipe dreams that we need to overcome.

\_\_\_

I want to suggest that other-than-pleasurable experiences and judgements are not rare, problematic, or otherwise poor cousins to the beautiful and the pleasing: they, quite possibly, account for the majority of the aesthetic experiences of each of us, unless we have been extremely fortunate, or unless we have stopped our eyes and ears and refused to take in the greater part of our surroundings. But in aesthetic theory, the ugliness of a toad, the banality of an exhibit of insipid watercolours, the horror and pity of Picasso’s *Guernica* have been considered as either non-aesthetic (because only aesthetic properties and experiences produce the pleasures of beauty), or as included in the aesthetic domain merely as its negative image: deformed, degenerate and transgressive.

The art world moved well beyond the notion of beauty in the twentieth century; it is aesthetic theory that has not been able to keep up. But beyond fine art, I am interested in aesthetic experience at large, whether of artworks or natural objects, and beauty or aesthetic pleasure have remained ascendant in our theories of such experience. Karl Rosenkranz, [who wrote the first treatise on ugliness](https://philpapers.org/rec/ROSAOU-2), argued that as biology is also concerned with illness, ethics with evil, and theology with sin, so too should aesthetics consider the ugly, which he saw as the *negation* of the beautiful. But to condemn the ugly as somehow perverse retains this implicit hope that the world needn’t be this way, that it isn’t *really* this way, and that these “flaws” can be overcome, as we seek out the tranquil shores of the pleasures of beauty.

The Canadian philosopher [Arnold Berleant has written passionately](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Sensibility_and_Sense.html?id=H4C7BAAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y) about what he calls “negative aesthetics” and has itemized a number of instances of it he finds in most of our lived urban environments. He talks of “aesthetic intrusions” of noise from cars and music; “aesthetic pain” from noxious emissions; “aesthetic distortion” from smog and the strident colours of neon signage; and “aesthetic deprivation” from cramped spaces and inadequate natural lighting. These are no doubt all negative, but Berleant writes of them with a sense of loss and offense, as ways that we have been impoverished against a harmony he seeks to regain. What he really seems to mean is an intrusion *against* the aesthetic, a distortion *of* the aesthetic, a deprivation *of* the aesthetic, and so on. The aesthetic, for Berleant, and for so many in the discipline, is an instrumental value in the service of a greater good.

The discipline’s [valorization of beauty](https://iai.tv/video/the-betrayal-of-beauty) and its pleasures may instead have blinded us and led us astray. There remains the blobfish, swimming in its depths, immune to our ever more frantic attempts to find order and meaning in a world that may have none. The German physicist Sabine Hossenfelder has noted that [notions of beauty have influenced scientific theories to their detriment](https://iai.tv/articles/physics-isnt-pretty-auid-1494). Our nostalgia for harmony is perhaps responsible for the failure of physics to progress since the Seventies, she has said. [Physics is “stuck” because physicists are looking for beautiful solutions to problems](https://iai.tv/video/truth-and-beauty) when the universe may not conform to human notions of beauty at all. The world may instead be ugly, irreducibly complex and messy, and a willing blindness to this possibility equally blinds the sorts of theories that physics, epistemology, metaphysics—and aesthetics—can produce.

What can we gain from admitting the ugly to the realm of aesthetic properties and values in its own right? Perhaps we can come to realize that the relation between subject and object—between us and the world—can be one of disharmony, or acrimony, or incomprehension, or [disorder](https://iai.tv/articles/chaos-theory-and-the-end-of-physics-tim-palmer-auid-2708). I have argued that aesthetic experience is a way of engaging with the world through disinterested perception. In aesthetic experience, we lack a “stake” in—or need to control, use, buy, understand, categorize, etc.—what we are viewing. If this is right, then we may just become more honest with ourselves, and about the world, when we acknowledge that this disinterested attention can be turned toward the ugly as well as the lovely. The world can be beautiful—but it is not, all the time, and we are not all the time either. The discipline of philosophical aesthetics may just be the best hope we have of coming to grips with that precisely because it is not attempting to understand the world as it truly is—as though ordered and harmonious—but rather is concerned with the ways we experience it as it appears to us, or impinges upon us, at any given moment.

As [Albert Camus has written](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Myth_of_Sisyphus.html?id=zaPoAQAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y), “the mind’s deepest desire, even in its most elaborate operations, parallels man’s unconscious feeling in the face of his universe: it is an insistence upon familiarity, an appetite for clarity.” But what is absurd is “the confrontation of this irrational [world] and the wild longing for clarity whose call echoes in the human heart.” Clarity and familiarity may be pipe dreams that we need to overcome. The absurd can well manifest itself in an instance of aesthetic attention, when we are confronted with something that can elicit awe, disgust, revulsion, pain, or boredom. These are also, undoubtedly, parts of human lived experience, and aesthetic theory would be enriched by acknowledging them directly rather than dismissing them as extra-aesthetic or non-aesthetic because of its long ties to pleasure and beauty. As Theodor Adorno has noted, beauty “[is only a moment in the totality of aesthetic reflection](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Aesthetic_Theory.html?id=NGxSnig-u3wC&redir_esc=y),” and a frank confrontation with those aspects of the world which we might prefer to ignore or deny is a way to free the aesthetic from its residual liberatory connotations and naiveté, and to focus on how the world actually reveals itself to us, rather than how we might wish it would be.
