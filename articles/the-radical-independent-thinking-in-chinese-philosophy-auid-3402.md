# The radical independent thinking in Chinese philosophy

*How the West gets Confucianism wrong*

**By Justin Tiwald** | *29th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,237 words*

![The radical independent thinking in Chinese philosophy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-radical-independent-thinking-in-Chinese-philosophy.webp)

---

*We tend in the West to think of Chinese Confucianism as dominated by obedience, conformity and tradition. But this is a misleading caricature. As Hong Kong philosopher Justin Tiwald argues, many Confucian philosophers celebrate independent thinking and moral autonomy. Indeed, Zide–“getting it oneself”—is a core Confucian value, a call to think and act for oneself. Western observers have been blindsided by China’s tech boom, surprised by its reliance on decentralization and local initiative. But once we abandon the cliché of Chinese culture as inherently deferential, China’s entrepreneurial dynamism may be less of a surprise.*

I was going to wear a t-shirt celebrating my favorite rock band to my grandfather’s funeral, but my aunt tells me that it’s inappropriate for the occasion, so I change to a dress shirt with a tie. My child is getting married, and after consulting a book about wedding etiquette, I volunteer to pay for the rehearsal dinner. I don’t know why my aunt and the etiquette book are right, but I know that they are much more likely to be right than I am, and I follow their advice. We generally believe it is good for people to “think for themselves,” but when is it right to have faith in the advice of other people and texts? These sorts of questions—questions about *moral deference—*have been at the heart of Confucian philosophical discourse for most of its history.

Confucianism is often taken to be in favor of deference to tradition and authority (customs, teachers, elders, etc.). In fact, the truth is more complicated and more interesting. Historical views about moral deference among Confucian philosophers have varied enormously. Early (“classical”) Confucianism gave rise to the world’s strongest and most sophisticated pro-deference view, a view associated with Xunzi (3rd century BCE, pronounced like “Shoon-zuh”). But his position came to be marginalized, and for the better part of the next two-and-a-half millennia the most notable thinkers were at least moderately pro-autonomy*.* Some were radically pro-autonomy. In fact, there was a term-of-art, roughly translated as “[getting it oneself](https://academic.oup.com/book/44919/chapter/384783625)” (*zide* 自得), that figured prominently in later writings about ethics and moral knowledge. The story of how this came to pass is well worth telling.

We can start with Xunzi’s case for strong moral deference. [As I read him](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11712-012-9282-1), Xunzi thinks that nearly all of us, in nearly all domains of our ethical lives, are better off trusting a time-tested tradition and its expert interpreters than relying on our own judgment. For him, it’s just not realistic that anyone other than a sage would understand all the considerations that make an ethical practice better than its alternatives. And for most intents and purposes, none of us is a sage.

\_\_\_

Part of what makes ethical values good ones is how amenable they are to being grasped and adopted by us.

\_\_\_

Xunzi offers two succinct ways of grasping his basic argument for moral deference. One compares the Confucian ethical tradition to [crafts like cooking and music](https://ctext.org/xunzi/da-lve#n12799). If you want to become a great musician, is it reasonable to expect that every step toward success would seem justified by your own lights? Or is it more reasonable to learn the traditional musical scales and classical compositions—well attested by teachers and by history’s great musicians—and come to see their advantages and values after a great deal of practice?

Another succinct line of argument compares the Confucian ethical tradition to [technology](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Xunzi/Pr7BAwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&pg=PA330&printsec=frontcover). If you want to travel a great distance, does it make sense to build a vehicle based on principles that you can quickly understand for yourself? Or is it better to adopt the best of the available vehicles and work backwards from the assumption that they work well?

Both analogies suggest that there is some sort of *pre-existing technique* whose adoption is warranted and whose operating principles outstrip any one novice’s ability to understand them (within a reasonable timeframe). Accordingly, adopting the pre-existing ethical techniques is much more reasonable than going it alone.

To extend Xunzi’s analogy, we can think of the Confucian ethical tradition as a highly sophisticated piece of social technology, one whose interlocking parts all depend on one another, so that grasping the proper functioning of any one part will require that one grasps the proper functioning of most of the other parts. If you want to know why this piston should turn in this direction at this rate rather than some other way at some other rate, you would need to know a great deal about how the piston works in tandem with many other parts of the machinery. And the machinery is so vast and so carefully calibrated that no one person (other than the sage) can see how it all works together. Similarly, no one person can expect to understand the basis for one part of the ethical tradition without also understanding most of the other parts, and understanding most of the other parts is neither realistic nor desirable.

Over the subsequent two millennia, Xunzi continued to be read carefully even by thinkers who strongly disagreed with him. But eventually, the consensus among the influential Confucian philosophers was that Xunzi was deeply and systematically wrong about [the foundations of ethics](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&chapter=233438#p7). The great 12th-century Neo-Confucian Zhu Xi (1130–1200) regarded him as an outlier to the proper Confucian tradition, and Zhu’s views became orthodoxy across much of East Asia.

From Zhu Xi’s time onward, most of the influential Confucian philosophers started from different assumptions about the nature of ethical values. They aren’t like components of complex social machinery, whose proper function is determined simply by how well they perform some task. Part of what makes ethical values good ones is how amenable they are to being grasped and adopted by *us*. By analogy, the machinery must necessarily have a certain amount of intelligibility and intuitive appeal to us, or else it doesn’t count as good machinery in the first place. Un-intuitive and alien machinery doesn’t do the work that we rightly expect of our ethical values.

\_\_\_

“Getting it oneself” (grasping the reasons why it’s a good practice) is an indispensable requirement for adopting the belief and acting in accordance with it wholeheartedly.

\_\_\_

My way of accounting for the later consensus against Xunzi is to say that these philosophers thought ethical values must be capable of being adopted *wholeheartedly.* Think of the difference between (1) someone who visits his mother in the hospital merely because trustworthy moral experts tell him to do so, and (2) someone who visits his mother because he understands the acute sense of despair and dread that comes from facing a big, impersonal institution all alone while injured and helpless, because he sees it as a proper expression of his deep desire and commitment to repaying a debt of gratitude to his mother for all of her sacrifices, and so on. Both of these sons *know* that they should visit and comfort their mother in the hospital. But the first one merely affirms this belief on the basis of opaque evidence. The second understands and adopts the belief more wholeheartedly. “[Getting it oneself](https://zh.m.wikisource.org/wiki/四書或問_(四庫全書本)/卷33)” (grasping the reasons why it’s a good practice) is an indispensable requirement for adopting the belief and acting in accordance with it wholeheartedly.

How did this way of thinking about ethical values become so much more widely accepted than Xunzi’s? It’s hard to say for certain because so much changed in the thirteen centuries between his time and Zhu Xi’s. One likely factor was that, by Zhu’s time, Buddhism had become wildly popular in much of East Asia, in part by promoting belief in an intrinsic source of enlightenment, a “Buddha-nature.” Another possibility is that the rise of the huge and highly centralized administrative state created an even stronger imperative to have individual Confucian officials who thought for themselves and were willing to speak truth to power. But the most obvious explanation (compatible with the first two) is just that awareness of the values of “getting it oneself” and wholeheartedness [were there all along](http://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28109/chapter-abstract/212225778?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false), even in the thought of Xunzi’s [classical Confucian predecessors](https://ctext.org/mengzi/li-lou-ii#n1713). Confucianism was never monolithic, and its most influential thinkers arose out of periods of great pluralism and ideological diversity, so that fundamental objections to Xunzi’s framework were sure to arise one way or another.

Many of the debates in later Confucianism turn on disagreements about how rigorously we should apply the requirement that our ethical values should be amenable to autonomous understanding and wholehearted adoption. At the more moderate end of the pro-autonomy spectrum are philosophers like the aforementioned Zhu Xi, or the somewhat subtler and more nuanced Dai Zhen (1724–77). Zhu and Dai want to allow for an ethical value-system that has some counter-intuitive parts or processes, so long as there are at least some accessible or intuitive [points of entry](https://neo-confucianism.com/?page_id=1152)into its machinery (continuing the analogy between ethical systems and social technology). Furthermore, they would insist that the strange and unfamiliar parts should bear a certain analogical resemblance to the parts that we understand more naturally, so that we can eventually come to grasp the proper functioning of the unfamiliar parts through fast and frugal cognition that we find compelling and motivational.

At the more extreme end are the radically pro-autonomy Confucians like Wang Yangming (1472–1529), who insist not just that there be intuitive points of entry into the social technology, but that *all* the machinery (all its parts and processes) be readily intelligible and assessable by us. For Wang and his kindred spirits, there is no way to ensure the wholehearted embrace of ethical values, and the robust independence of thought that societies need to function properly, unless all the ethical values are accessible (in some sense) even to the relatively untutored, “natural” hearts and minds of ordinary people.

To my mind, it is difficult to get a handle on problems of moral deference without at least sketching answers to the questions raised by Xunzi and his opponents. To what extent are ethical guidelines like technology or machinery, to be evaluated primarily by their success at effecting good outcomes? How apt is Xunzi’s analogy between adopting well-attested ethical norms and adopting well-confirmed pre-existing techniques like the traditional musical scales (and what would it mean for an ethical norm to be “well attested”)?

There are also important questions about what it means, exactly, to say that someone “gets” the basis of good moral beliefs “oneself.” In my experience, most 21st-century folks assume that this involves some sort of inferential relationship between general moral principles and specific conclusions (e.g., “*because* one should show respect for the recently deceased… *therefore* I should not wear clothing that calls undue attention to me”). But the later Confucians who wrote on “getting it oneself” [had a much richer sort of cognition in mind](https://academic.oup.com/book/44919/chapter/384783625), one that involves seeing analogies between contextualized scenarios, and connecting them in a stepwise manner that makes them more amenable to fast and frugal (“intuitive”) comprehension, so that their ethical significance is easily grasped and compels assent. For example, a politician who wants to “get for herself” what’s wrong with accepting bribes for special political favors could do this by grasping how accepting the bribe [is relevantly similar to accepting food that some rich bully has spat in](https://ctext.org/mengzi/gaozi-i#n1763) and now hands to you to eat for his entertainment. Capturing the relevant similarities in terms of general principles can often help in this process, but it’s not required. Nor is knowledge of underlying general principles sufficient by itself to make one’s moral judgments autonomous.

When I give presentations to colleagues and students on Confucian views about moral deference and autonomy, they always want to know which of the various positions I think right. Actually, I am not in a great hurry to decide. In my view, the more urgent task is to get a better handle on the range of reasonably coherent and plausible views, and the reasons for preferring some over the others. But if pressed to plunk down for one side, I would plunk down for the moderately pro-autonomy views like those described by Zhu Xi and Dai Zhen. I think they are probably right that ethical values are supposed to serve many purposes, some of which (probably the most important of which) require that we “get it ourselves” and adopt them somewhat wholeheartedly. And they are probably right that this requires relatively intuitive starting points. But I am tempted to part ways with them by making allowances for the fact that some parts of the ethical machinery may never be fully grasped autonomously, may always feel somewhat alien and counter-intuitive to us, even to a sage. If it turned out that we could only avoid nuclear annihilation by adopting values that most of us will never fully “get ourselves,” then we should carve out some space for those values too.

Even so, I don’t think there is much point in being an ethical person if such alien and ungraspable values were the central ones, if we were expected to make them the guiding lights in all that we do. If the pro-autonomy Confucians are right, they aren’t supposed to be guiding lights at all, but rather integral parts of our moral psychology and moral cognition, the tissues and bloodstream of the ethical heart and mind. On most days, it seems obvious to me that our core ethical values must be capable of this sort of integration, and so I tend to think that the later critics of Xunzi had the better approach to moral deference.
