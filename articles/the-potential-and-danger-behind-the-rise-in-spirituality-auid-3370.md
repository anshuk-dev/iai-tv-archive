# The potential and danger behind the rise in spirituality

*Spiritual complexity, science and meaning making in polycrisis*

**By Anna Halafoff** | *24th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,932 words*

![The potential and danger behind the rise in spirituality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-potential-and-danger-behind-the-rise-in-spirituality.webp)

---

*Spirituality is no longer a fringe pursuit but a force shaping health, ethics, and politics in late modern societies. Once dismissed as private and unserious, it now permeates wellness industries, youth culture, and ecological movements, offering new ways to live and heal in an age of polycrisis. Yet its growing influence also carries risks: from exploitation and misinformation to conspiracy and abuse. Understanding spirituality’s complexity is crucial to navigating its role in shaping the future of care, community, and planetary survival.*

Significant studies have focused on the [decline of Christianity, and the rise of religious diversity](https://archive.nonreligionproject.ca/) and of the [non-religious](https://nonreligionproject.ca/), in so-called Western societies over the past 50 years. Far less scholarly attention has been paid to parallel trends of increasing interest in spirituality, and nature-connection as often, but not always, intersecting worldviews. Scholars argue that spirituality has been taken less seriously than religion, given it is [associated with women and the private sphere](https://www.routledge.com/Gender-and-Power-in-Contemporary-Spirituality-Ethnographic-Approaches/Fedele-Knibbe/p/book/9781138845077?srsltid=AfmBOoq_xy2b_XVIKAeU-BmPKBWtMYZWEXQ1hbusCvsHX5aeNO8_0T0R). Yet this is changing, due to the mainstreaming of holistic spirituality through the [global wellness industry](https://globalwellnessinstitute.org/industry-research/2024-global-wellness-economy-monitor/) and in response to global polycrisis.

Research reveals the salience of spirituality in contemporary societies. [PEW recently found](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/12/07/spirituality-among-americans/) that 48% of US adults identify as spiritual and religious, and 22% as spiritual but not religious (SBNR), equalling 70% spiritual in total. In Australia, the [Spirituality and Wellness (SWell) Survey](https://www.abc.net.au/religion/the-meaning-of-spirituality-among-australian-adults/104695486) found 29% of adults describe themselves as SBNR, and 23% are spiritual and religious, 52% spiritual combined. In both societies, these are substantial percentages, and it is important to note how large numbers of people, and much higher in the US, can be both religious *and* spiritual, engaged in practices such as prayer, contemplation, and retreats in natural settings. Indeed, scholars note that spirituality can be Indigenous, religious and/or holistic/alternative, in the Australian and other contexts.

While [religion is often framed](https://www.routledge.com/Spiritually-Integrated-Psychotherapy-Understanding-and-Addressing-the-Sacred/Pargament/p/book/9781609189938) as more serious, institutional, and problematic in terms of abuse of authority, spirituality is typically viewed as more individualised and benign, synonymous with [authenticity, personal choice and freedom](https://www.galenwatts.ca/thespiritualturn). Research reveals, however, that spirituality is not always so different to religion. Many spiritual practices and movements are deeply social, can be prescribed by spiritual leaders/influencers at the head of movements, organisations or companies, and can also [pose some serious risks](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00377686231162005). This is evident in commercialisation and [cultural appropriation](https://www.traumasensitiveyoganederland.com/yoga-and-the-roots-of-cultural-appropriation/) of spiritual practices, [abuse by spiritual leaders](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0048721X.2025.2538957?src=exp-la), and the uptake of conspiracy theories (‘[conspirituality](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13537903.2011.539846)’) during the Covid pandemic. Both [‘religious complexity’](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-55678-9) and [‘spiritual complexity’](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00377686231162005#bibr28-00377686231162005) are evident in late modern societies, with some religious and/or spiritual institutions declining, while others flourish, and with an ambivalence, pertaining to their contributions to wellbeing and possible harms.

While there is no agreed definition of religion or spirituality, contemporary spirituality can be described as entailing a sense of relational interconnection to something greater than oneself, be it to a deity/ies, other humans and/or the natural world, which informs ethics and (non)actions for self and planetary care.

\_\_\_

It seems that motivation plays a significant role in determining whether spirituality can be beneficial or dangerous.

\_\_\_

Many spiritual persons turn to various forms of spirituality when first confronted by their own or other’s health, grief, economic or environmental crises. It is then when they often begin questioning not only their own life’s meaning and purpose, but also modern processes of agriculture, medicine and governance perceived as causes of such ills. Research shows that spiritual women, in particular, feel dissatisfied by the lack of care and respect shown to them by biomedical doctors, and frustration at Western medicine’s inability to cure ailments without significant negative side-effects. They take up alternative, holistic health and wellness practices as a result, which they feel are more caring and beneficial. The efficacy of some of these alternative practices, such as Chinese and [Ayurvedic](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34553463/) traditional medicines, to relieve symptoms of menopause, for example, are increasingly being investigated and proven by scientific studies. Most spiritual people end up [combining both holistic and biomedical practices](https://www.emerald.com/books/edited-volume/17153/chapter-abstract/94143482/The-Body-Complex-Con-spirituality-Wellness-and?redirectedFrom=fulltext), as needed, finding that they can complement one another and posing little cause for concern.

Young people are also experiencing many economic stresses and pressures in fast-paced capitalist societies, with precarious working conditions. They are deeply aware of the harms of social and political injustices, of direct and structural violence, and ecological crises. They, are asking the ‘big questions’, how can we address these issues, be better people and live better lives together with one another and the more-than-human world? While religions or secular humanist traditions used to provide the answer to these questions, many young people find the answers now in spirituality, inspired by Indigenous, Asian, African American and Ecofeminist knowledges, on self and planetary wellbeing.

Spirituality’s relevance in late-modernity is further evidenced by the high and wide uptake of holistic spiritual practices and beliefs, such as yoga, meditation, reincarnation, tarot, crystals and astrology, [particularly among Millennials and Generation X](https://theconversation.com/38-of-gen-z-australians-identify-as-spiritual-and-half-of-them-believe-in-karma-why-is-spirituality-so-popular-223203), and the way they permeate pop-culture’s lexicons and aesthetics, such as [Taylor Swift’s song ‘Karma’](https://www.abc.net.au/listen/programs/soul-search/good-karma-taylor-swift-and-popular-buddhism-in-pop-music/103430304). Spiritual ideas from Asia spread to Western culture through Theosophy and Spiritualism at the turn of the 20th Century. The 1960s Counter Culture movement saw a broader uptake of so-called Eastern spirituality in music and fashion, as did the 1990s New Age, which drew inspiration from Indigenous and Asian traditions. But even then spirituality was associated with fringe, alternative movements. By 2025 spirituality has become deeply embedded in a [$6.3 trillion wellness industry](https://globalwellnessinstitute.org/press-room/statistics-and-facts/), and in everyday lives of younger generations, the children of Boomers and Gen Z hippies and New Agers.

\_\_\_

As Aboriginal scholar and author of Sand Talk, Tyson Yunkaporta shares, ‘Indigenous thinking can save the world’.

\_\_\_

Research reveals that young people make ethical choices on the basis of their spirituality, what they think, say and do, and what they eat and what they wear. Some young spiritual people are [vegan or vegetarian and involved in environmental activism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00377686231162005). Many spiritual fashion brands also have ecologically sustainable practices. Practices of self-care involve spending time slowing down and re-connecting with oneself, with others and with the natural world. This includes sharing social media posts of [‘girl mossing’,](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/4/447) resting in nature dressed in fairy fashion, rejecting the hustle of ‘girl boss’ culture. Affordable urban social bathing, is another current wellness trend where you can lounge in magnesium pools, sweat in saunas, and plunge in ice baths before or after work. Most people do this with friends or partners, and while some venues stress the need for quiet, while others play music and encourage social connection. [Conscious dance](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13537903.2025.2480923), such as 5 Rhythms and Ecstatic Dance, has also had a resurgence as another affordable spiritual and healing modality where people dance, free of substances, to release tensions and gain new insights and life direction. The fact that spiritual and wellness practices can be fun, and shared online, [does not make them shallow](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00377686231162005#bibr67-00377686231162005). Young people can engage with and post about tarot cards with friends, socially on a Friday night, and still be deeply guided by their tarot readings.

Recent Australian research has also noted a deep engagement with ‘Buddhish’ practices, for self-care but also in death-care, including mindfulness, and Pema Chodron’s books on how to cope *When Things Fall Apart*. This and her less well-known, but equally profound book, *Start Where You Are*, were also featured in Mike White’s *White Lotus* most recent third season, which was [replete with Buddhist and spiritual themes and fashion](https://www.abc.net.au/religion/white-lotus-season-3-buddhism-spiritual-complexity-west-malaise/105181464), mixed in with elite five star wellness travel.

Spirituality’s relationship with neoliberalism has been the subject of substantive critique, and it is certainly complex. Scholars observe that the types of worldviews which are flourishing in contemporary societies are ones that are [aligned with capitalism](https://www.routledge.com/Religion-Modernity-Globalisation-Nation-State-to-Market/Gauthier/p/book/9781032089133?srsltid=AfmBOoqUsimWNafotcoeMmhfF5bAYc0kgv7AKN1aqLI2mpGeVBsoP4qo) – including harnessing social media – or ones that are highly critical of it. Contemporary spirituality is both, and also has a similarly ambivalent relationship to science, in that spiritual influencers use scientific evidence to prove the benefits of their practices and products, and also highlight the failures and limits of modern science, notably modern agriculture and medicine. This kind of ‘sciencey spirituality’, including biohacking and conspirituality, seems to be drawing more men into contemporary spirituality. Given both capitalism and science are the dominant ideologies of the 21st century, harnessing and critiquing them gives spirituality increased markedness in current times

Most studies conclude that spirituality has both its genuine benefits and risks. Those who reject biomedicine entirely based on misinformation, such as conspiritual anti-vaccination campaigners, are a vocal minority who nonetheless pose serious threats to broader society. [They are emboldened](https://journal.equinoxpub.com/JASR/article/view/22810) by a spiritual exceptionalism, seeing themselves as holders of true knowledge, involved in a spiritual war against elites. This can lead to spiritual bypassing of actual suffering and negation of the need to protect more vulnerable people from harms, such as dangerous viruses. Spiritual risks can also result from abuse of authority, where followers can be harmed economically, emotionally, physically and/or sexually as a result of unequal power relations. Spiritual and wellness influencers and businesses can also be self-serving and profit driven, pushing their own products, which lack scientific credibility and that can at times be dangerous. Extreme critique of biomedicine from such influencers can also lead others to reject biomedical cancer and other treatments that can save lives. This has all been recently illustrated by the [*Apple Cider Vinegar*](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-02-20/apple-cider-vinegar-belle-gibson-lies-rage-science/104954630) series, centred on Belle Gibson.

But not all spiritual and wellness influencers are out there peddling misinformation. Podcaster and author of *I Quit Sugar and This One Wild and Precious Life,* [Sarah Wilson](https://sarahwilson.com/), [bravely critiqued conspirituality](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2020/sep/15/the-wellness-realm-has-fallen-into-conspiritualism-i-have-a-sense-why) at the height of the Covid-19 pandemic.Once again, spiritual complexity must be highlighted here, rather than tarring all spiritual influencers or persons with a conspiritual brush.

It seems that motivation plays a significant role in determining whether spirituality can be beneficial or dangerous. If the intention is self-serving, exploitative, power and profit driven, and the engagement is shallow, what Wilson describes as [‘spirituality lite’](https://www.emerald.com/books/edited-volume/17153/chapter-abstract/94143482/The-Body-Complex-Con-spirituality-Wellness-and?redirectedFrom=fulltext), or purposely peddling misinformation, it may well result in harm. Yet there is deep and respectful engagement with spirituality, which acknowledges its origins in and influences from Indigenous and diverse religious worldviews. This spirituality genuinely seeks to learn from wisdom traditions and contemplative practices, how to live and die well together with human and more-than-human others, and can and is playing a significant role in self-care, healing and decolonial planetary wellbeing to address modern ills. As Aboriginal scholar and author of [*Sand Talk*](https://www.textpublishing.com.au/books/sand-talk), Tyson Yunkaporta shares, ‘Indigenous thinking can save the world’.

Pop-culture forms of spirituality, such as the ‘girl mossing’ phenomena, Swift’s song ‘Karma’ and TV series *White Lotus* can be easily mistaken for simply being the former, commercialised ‘lite’ versions of spirituality, but closer inspection shows that they can be far more nuanced, complex and sincere. Importantly, by harnessing popular culture, they point to spiritual and ‘Buddhish’ concepts using [‘skilful means’](https://tricycle.org/beginners/buddhism/skillful-means/), which may activate deeper awakening and ethical (non)action, just by sharing a glimmer of them.

Given that we have now entered a time of [polycrisis](https://polycrisis.org/lessons/where-did-the-term-polycrisis-come-from/), with growing economic, political and environmental ills, it is not surprising that people are increasingly turning to spirituality for guidance of how to address these threats. The [science that spiritual persons are most engaged in](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-edit/10.4324/9781003476580-18/sciencey-spirituality-neoliberalism-eco-anxiety-australia-anna-halafoff-ruth-fitzpatrick-andrew-singleton), is that which is proving the wisdom of pre-modern knowledges, and being applied to rectify modern harms, healing bodies and the planet, and re-connecting us with one another and the natural world, from [Indigenous theories of relationality](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14744740211029287) and [fire management](https://www.klc.org.au/indigenous-fire-management) programs, to multiple meditation and sleep apps. These knowledges, combined with the latest technologies, may just save us from the ultimate ignorance and cause of suffering – that we are somehow separate from each other – resulting in a caring and compassionate responsibility to oneself and for all.
