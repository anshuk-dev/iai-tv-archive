# The universe evolves like a life form

*The laws of physics are not fixed, they are evolving*

**By Timothy Jackson** | *11th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,206 words*

![The universe evolves like a life form](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-universe-evolves-like-a-life-form5.webp)

---

*We tend to think of reality as made up of things, governed by fixed laws that determine how they change over time. But biochemist **Timothy Jackson** argues that this is back to front: fundamental reality is a lawless flux, a chaos of unpredictable change, and what needs explaining is not chaos but the stability and order that emerge from it. The “laws” of physics are not eternal truths but descriptions of patterns that have persisted long enough to look permanent. Darwin’s central insight, Jackson suggests, was to show how such order might emerge, via natural selection—a principle which can explain, but never predict, the patterns that make up the world.*

**From physicalism to “biologism”**

From panpsychism to “Platonic Space,” assertions that we need to move “beyond physics” are increasingly common among scientists and philosophers. Whilst it’s nothing new for different sciences to be associated with distinct worldviews, current conversations seem to be more explicitly metaphysical than has traditionally been the case among scientists. It seems that the disavowal of metaphysics as a meaningful pursuit hasn’t really expunged it from modern science, so much as hidden it. The common-parlance term for the dominant—and largely *tacit*—metaphysics of modern science is “[physicalism](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-9991.2008.00163.x),” and it is this that so many are arguing we need to move beyond.

I’m a biologist, working on molecular evolution and chemical ecology—the ways in which organisms mediate their relationships with one another using bioactive molecules—so it may be unsurprising if I agree that a metaphysical paradigm which seems to match the intuitions of physicists isn’t “enough.” Like many biologists, I use physics-derived models to help me understand the systems I study. However, a key difference between physics and biology—at least methodologically—concerns the relative status of variation and invariance. Physics tends to operate via a search for invariant principles or “laws” that govern the behavior of variables. Invariant parameters are embedded in physical models in ways that generate a predefined “state space,” which corresponds to the possible trajectories of the basic elements the theory applies to. These invariants can also be understood as the theory’s basis of reduction or principle of “coarse-graining”—all meaningful variation is understandable in terms of this reduction base and the rest is noise.

\_\_\_

Molecular biology and the “Neo-Darwinian” paradigm have become notorious for attempting to mould biology in the image of physics.

\_\_\_

The situation in biology, as I see it, [is almost the opposite](https://iai.tv/articles/biology-not-physics-holds-the-key-to-reality-auid-3163). Indeed, Charles Darwin’s major insight was to see that in biology, *everything* varies. All of an organism’s character states are potentially plastic—this variation is “random” in the sense that it is intrinsically unpredictable by recourse to any dimension of reduction, i.e., any model, theory, or “law.” Darwin realized that, given the extent of the variation exhibited by the character states of the organisms he painstakingly observed, some principle was required to explain why that variation was, nonetheless, not completely continuous, but exhibits evident order. You cannot *start* by defining invariant properties, or you’ll just end up with a never-ending string of exceptions that seem increasingly unlikely to prove the rule. But variation *is* patterned, clumpy—where one taxonomist and the next draw the boundary between two species might be *somewhat* arbitrary, but it’s nonetheless the case that there are two distinct populations being observed. Enter the principle of selection (“natural” or otherwise) as a way of explaining why some variants proliferate and others do not, and why those that proliferate end up forming moderately stable groupings.

Now, it’s certainly true that biological systems are subject to physical invariants—enabling constraints that provide them with “[order for free](https://www.jstor.org/stable/193076).” Thinking in biological terms, these exceptionally stable properties constitute an environment to which all biological systems are necessarily “adapted.” But, precisely by virtue of their generality, an appeal to these shared physical constraints is unlikely to provide an explanation of the *particular* adaptations that characterize a biological lineage. In the specifically biological strata of relations, there are very few true invariants, and even those which exist—for example the genetic code—are understood to be contingent “achievements” of an historical, evolutionary process. They just happen to be “synapomorphies” (shared derived characteristics) of all life as we know it.

As such, biological “invariants”—for example the synapomorphies that define any given clade, such as a family, genus, or species—turn out to be properties that are simply more stable than others, i.e. varying on a longer time scale. This stability is explained by appeal to a principle of selection. This is a markedly different situation from the traditional one in physics, because in evolutionary theory the “invariants” are internal to the process described by the theory, i.e. products of evolution. In physics, the evolution of a variable’s state is typically described or “explained” by appeal to invariant dynamical laws (and the system’s boundary conditions), which are not themselves the product of that evolution. This may seem like a subtle difference, or perhaps a *merely* methodological one, but it turns out to be a major dividing point amongst metaphysical frameworks that goes back at least to Plato, and before him Parmenides and Heraclitus, i.e., well over 2,000 years.

**The view from chemical ecology**

Just as devotion to physical or biological “lenses” may lead to a difference in worldview, these sciences are not themselves homogenous, and physicists or biologists working in distinct areas within each science may also differ in their intuitions. Molecular biology and [the “Neo-Darwinian” paradigm](https://iai.tv/video/why-dawkins-is-wrong-denis-noble), for example, have become notorious for attempting to mould biology in the image of physics by identifying the gene as a privileged causal reduction base. All variation in biological systems, the claim goes, can be explained by the variation of genes. Despite its rather ironic designation, this reductive conception is rather more *non-*Darwinian, inasmuch as it is a return to the “[hylomorphic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-metaphysics/#SubsHyloComp)” worldview that Darwin’s theory specifically repudiates. In hylomorphism—a doctrine attributed to Aristotle, though his account was more nuanced—predefined forms imbue passive matter with their characteristics in a manner strikingly redolent of the claim that organisms are merely the “vehicles” of the genes which determine their every trait.

It turns out that, in identifying variation as a “first principle,” Darwin was rather more perceptive than this, and chemical ecology—a field very close to molecular biology, given its shared concern with biomolecules—provides ample evidence for the failure of Neo-Darwinism. In chemical ecology, we are principally concerned not merely with a molecule’s *structure,* but with what it *does*—its activities and function(s). We are concerned with [the ways in which organisms *use* molecules](https://iai.tv/articles/organisms-are-musicians-not-machines-auid-3436) in their relations with one another. Sometimes organisms make these useful molecules themselves, and sometimes they steal or borrow them from other organisms—a form of nascent tool use. What is key about biomolecules is that their activities are radically context-dependent. Their effects are not “deterministic,” not guaranteed by their structure, but fully dependent on their “ecology”—i.e. the range of interaction partners (other molecules) they encounter in a given context. This turns out to be key to understanding the ways in which *novel functions* evolve at the molecular level and requires an appeal to forms of variation that radically exceed that of the primary structure of the genes themselves.

To understand this point, it’s important to note that “activity” and “function” are not synonymous in biology. There are devils in the details here, but activity is a context-dependent *property*—simply what a molecule (or a “population” of molecules) does, given a certain environment and range of partners to interact with. A molecule’s “function” concerns the activity it has been *selected* to perform in the life of the organism that produces (or steals) it. Functions are *purposeful*, directed; activities are not. The capacity for *activity* to vary according to context, therefore, is one form of biological variation that enables novel functions to arise. When an “accidental” activity turns out to be useful to the organism, selection can act to increase the likelihood of that activity recurring.

**Indeterminacy at the origin**

Molecules end up in unspecified contexts doing unspecified things due to at least two more forms of biological variation—stochastic gene expression and the “non-stereospecificity” of molecular interactions. Whilst gene expression is regulated in ways that generally ensure there’s enough (and not too much) of a molecule in any given cell or tissue, this regulation is not absolute and there is always a margin of “noise” that escapes it. Molecules are always showing up in places they have no (predefined) “reason” for being. Similarly, whilst the structures of molecules are fine-tuned for specificity—and some molecules are far more specific in their interactions than others—specificity is equally “non-perfectible.” Molecules turn out to be sticky, promiscuous, prone to “off-target” interactions. Both gene expression and molecular interaction exhibit margins of *indeterminacy*, which not only contribute to falsifying Neo-Darwinism’s genetic determinism but are also grist for the mill of selection, enabling the open-ended evolution of novel functions via the formation of novel relations.

\_\_\_

Whilst it is undeniable that physics has offered a great deal to biology in terms of theoretical tools, it may yet be the case that biology has much to offer physics in return.

\_\_\_

Chemical ecology is a discipline particularly well-suited to illustrating these forms of variation and cultivating intuitions about the role of variation in nature. We try to understand how a molecule that had a potentially well-defined ancestral function in the body of the organism that produces it ends up being “co-opted” for a different function in the body of a different organism. And we’re not solely concerned with *organisms*, but also with “the ecology of molecules”—the way in which molecular activities and functions depend on the availability of interaction partners. Indeed, some of the same principles apply to both organisms and molecules. For example, the principle of “escape and radiate” describes a situation in which an organism moves from one environment to another, escaping the constraints of the previous environment and entering into novel relationships with novel degrees of freedom. This often triggers a burst of evolutionary change. It turns out something very similar happens with molecules when *they* enter novel “environments,” like the bodies of other organisms into which they may have been “accidentally” introduced via a bite. In this situation, molecules fulfilling a digestive or antimicrobial function may unintentionally exert a toxic *activity* (which may later become a function, i.e., an *intentional* effect of a bite) in the body of the bite victim.

**Metaphysics again: the primacy of flux**

The tacit metaphysics associated with the invariance-first approach of physicalism arguably does not serve the biologist. Various interesting theoretical options—in particular a return to and elaboration of Darwin’s mode of reasoning—become available when we cease seeking modes of explanation based on the identification of privileged bases of reduction.

The question remains: what metaphysics *is* best suited to a worldview which takes variation as a first principle? Such a worldview doesn’t simply turn physicalism on its head, but charts different territory altogether. It is a worldview based on “operations” and “functions,” [not on (invariant) *substances*](https://iai.tv/video/organisms-are-processes-not-things-with-wahida-khandker), and is thus a genuine alternative to much of the history of metaphysics since Plato (and some of the revivals of “Platonism” in contemporary science). Furthermore, whilst it is undeniable that physics has offered a great deal to biology in terms of theoretical tools, it may yet be the case that biology has much to offer physics in return. Arguably this is already happening, as we see increasing appeal to models that posit primary variation, such as “[Entropic Gravity](https://iai.tv/video/newtons-fall),” and [evolving laws](https://iai.tv/articles/lee-smolin-the-laws-of-the-universe-change-auid-2174) or constants, in attempts to solve outstanding questions in fundamental physics.

Evolving laws would not, of course, merely change in “random” ways—they would be just like the (relatively) stable patterns of biology and geology, which have their origins in arbitrary variation but derive their stability from non-random selection. The primary distinction (itself in need of explanation) between the “invariant” laws of physics, and the ancient properties of, for example, terrestrial environments—which no one doubts have an historical genesis—would be the time-scales at which they are stable.

There are a series of ancient arguments against this mode of thought, which insist that (for example) if the laws change, there must be a law that governs the change of the laws. Such arguments demonstrate the existence of an “infinite regress,” which is considered threatening to the reasonableness of any counter-position. This is a cherished argumentative strategy of theologians and (broadly speaking) “rationalists.” However, it is predicated on the assumption that there *must be* a “first cause,” or that the fact of intelligibility in nature requires an *a priori* guarantor of intelligibility. Causation, laws, intelligibility—so the argument goes—entail a *first* cause, a first law, ultimately a *law-giver*.

The evolutionary thinker rejects this assumption via recourse to the empirical domain of biology, in which intelligibility apparently emerges from unintelligibility all the time. That this emergence of the definite from the indefinite is “real” and not *merely* a matter of “appearances,” turns out to be the unexpectedly (and unintentionally) generic kernel of Darwin’s thought. Numerous thinkers, from logician-metaphysicians like Charles Sanders Peirce and Alfred North Whitehead, to epistemologists like Karl Popper (who considered Darwin’s intervention the invention of “situational logic”), have explored the consequences of this insight in metaphysics, epistemology, and elsewhere. Old habits die hard, however; the ancient invariants that constrain our thought, even when their obvious historical contingency is acknowledged, continue to make the idea of an evolutionary metaphysics derived from a generalization of biological principles a tough pill to swallow.
