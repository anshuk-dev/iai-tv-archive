# Europe's last hope in the AI race

*Europe must gain AI sovereignty; here's how they can do it*

**By Bernardo Kastrup** | *5th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *3,075 words*

![Europe's last hope in the AI race](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Europes-last-hope-in-the-AI-race.webp)

---

*The global AI boom is built on staggering inefficiency: repurposed videogame chips, vast energy waste, and escalating costs. While the US and China race blindly ahead, philosopher of mind and the Founder and CEO at Euclyd, a Dutch company developing chips and systems for core AI datacentre compute, **Bernardo Kastrup**, argues that Europe has an overlooked opportunity to redesign AI hardware itself, and in doing so reclaim technological sovereignty.*

As a philosopher of mind and computer engineer, AI is the one topic that connects both of my professional aspirations. And since this powerful new technology is bound to shape the future of civilization, either supporting or undermining our ways of life, the survival of European values so consistent with my own idealist views—such as personal liberty, liberal democracy, human rights, equality of opportunity, consumer protections, distribution of power, etc.—will largely depend on how we manage the ongoing technological transition. To ensure the survival of its way of life, Europe must thus have the means to control the deployment of AI in its territory, so it happens on our terms.

In this context, it is with sadness that I observe how accepted it has become, among European high-tech industry leaders, that the continent’s AI chips will continue to come from Asian fabrication plants for the foreseeable future. After all, Asia is now so many generations ahead in the development of chip fabrication technology that to think of more than just obsolete chips being made in Europe is to indulge in fantasy; or so the story goes. Many believe that our only hope to secure a [palatable AI future](https://iai.tv/video/scott-aaronson-on-consciousness-and-ai-safety) is to develop our own homegrown AI software models to ensure their alignment with European values and regulations—that is, to ensure that AIs applied to government systems, healthcare, industry, the military, etc., reflect what Europeans consider fair and appropriate.

Here, I shall argue that such thinking is wrong on both counts, and that true European AI sovereignty can only be achieved through homegrown hardware—that’s the bad news. The good news is that, if our problems and strengths are correctly understood, European AI sovereignty can be achieved not in twenty or thirty years, but before this decade is out.

**AI alignment is not about models**

Industry leaders understand that, for Europe to catch up with the leading chip fabrication facilities, or “fabs,” in Asia, an investment of decades and tens of billions of Euros would be required. Therefore, it is understandable that many should seek refuge in software: no manufacturing is needed, and development cycles are measured in months, not decades. This way, Europe could [ignore its manufacturing lag](https://iai.tv/video/europe-after-the-reign) and still lead in at least one salient segment of the AI ecosystem. Sovereign models—many believe—are crucial to ensuring that our AI future is aligned with our laws and way of life.

This is baseless. By and large, the value-alignment of an AI model is not an intrinsic property of the model itself—that is, of the software algorithm—but instead an emerging outcome of its training and parameter fine-tuning. This is analogous to the human mind: our values are largely defined by what we learn through education and feedback from our social milieu, not brain anatomy. Trying to address the need for alignment with homegrown software models is like prescribing brain surgery to address improper education.

Moreover, the models themselves aren’t a sovereignty bottleneck to begin with. There are dozens of powerful open-source models out there—the vast majority of models are in fact open-sourced—which can be fine-tuned to satisfy local alignment needs. More become available regularly. It is true that the best parameter sets—where the results of training a model are encoded—are proprietary, since training costs immense amounts of time, energy and money; but the algorithms themselves are often described in academic papers and open-sourced. Europe stands a much better chance of success by leveraging the competitiveness of this open worldwide ecosystem than by artificially restricting itself to a couple of limited homegrown alternatives.

The real problem is this: to train an AI model and thus ensure its alignment, one needs access to vast amounts of hardware resources; that is, to the very AI chips we believe we can’t manufacture in Europe. Focusing on alignment brings us squarely back to the very issue we were trying to circumvent in the first place: if Europe doesn’t have a sovereign AI chip supply chain and can’t access foreign GPUs in sufficient numbers, it cannot have aligned AI even if the underlying models are homegrown.

Finally, certain key elements of alignment can only be guaranteed with so-called Hardware Enabled Mechanisms, or HEMs, which—as the name indicates—are permanently baked into the chips, not written in hackable software. All roads to true AI sovereignty lead us back to hardware.

But how plausible is it that Europe, with its dated chip fabs, could make usable—let alone competitive—AI hardware? To answer this, we must first understand why Europe’s chip-making ecosystem lost so much ground to Asia.

**Europe’s automotive focus**

The automotive market has, for a long time, been the focus of the European semiconductor industry. Our chip-making ecosystem has thus adapted to the requirements and constraints of cars. Unlike phones, tablets, and laptops, which are launched with new features almost every year, innovation cycles in the auto industry are long, as vehicles are too expensive for consumers to upgrade as often as they upgrade phones. This drives less demand for aggressive development in semiconductors.

Moreover, chip reliability in cars trumps cost and energy efficiency. The total cost of semiconductors is a relatively minor item in the bill of materials, where big metal—engine, chassis, gearbox, etc.—dominates. And the total energy consumed by a car’s chipset pales in comparison to the energy it takes to make the car move. As a result, Europe’s chip manufacturing industry has placed relatively little emphasis on the cost and energy consumption of the semiconductors it produces.

In contrast, in the mobile devices industry—which drives chip manufacturing in Asia—the need for low cost and energy consumption is overwhelming: chips dominate the bill of materials of phones sold at mass-market price points, and those chips are fed by small batteries. Moreover, rendering devices obsolete as quickly as possible, so consumers upgrade to new models, is what drives revenue. There is thus relentless pressure to develop new chips that perform more functions at lower energy consumption and cost. In a nutshell, this is why European fabs lag Asia’s.

The problem for Europe’s automotive-centric chip industry is not only its chip efficiency lag, but the fact that its own fortress—the automotive industry—is now being besieged by Asian makers of electric cars. This threat highlights the need to diversify the customer base of Europe’s chipmakers beyond automotive. And the explosive growth of AI makes it the obvious alternative.

But then again, how could Europe make competitive AI chips, given its lagging fabs? To finally answer this question, we must realize that a competitive chip is not only one made in the most advanced manufacturing nodes, but also one *cleverly designed*. And Europe’s lag in chip design capability is far less pronounced than that in manufacturing.

**AI is not videogame graphics**

Over the past five decades or so, it has become common wisdom in the chip design industry that shorter time-to-market and lower engineering costs are financially more important than efficient chips. As a result, chips are now routinely developed with commodity off-the-shelf building blocks and generic architectures. These building blocks—which include everything from processors such as ARM and RISC-V to interface blocks such as USB and PCIe—can be licensed from vast commercial libraries and applied in just about every chip.

The advantages are obvious: instead of doing everything from scratch, engineers can simply drop generic building blocks into an equally generic architecture, which is faster and less costly to do. The price, however, is that the final design is invariably suboptimal: none of the building blocks or architectures are customized to the specific needs of the application at hand. In extreme but regrettably common cases, if one needs only a metaphorical elephant’s tail to finish a design, one simply drops the entire elephant into the chip.

Absurd as it may sound, this has made good economic sense for decades. Getting to market only a few months late hands over a large chunk of the pie to the competition. And engineering hours are often the highest cost item in a chip design company’s budget. So generic, re-usable “Lego” blocks have been the name of the game for a long time.

NVIDIA’s greatest advantage—the fact that they happened to have something usable and ready to go when AI became commercially attractive—is also their Achilles’ heel: they are now stuck with an entire paradigm and ecosystem that is catastrophically inefficient.

\_\_\_

But now, with the AI boom, the calculation has suddenly become quite different: it is the efficiency of chips—in terms of their cost and power consumption per task performed—that has become the key barrier to scaling the deployment of AI. In the USA, the construction of nuclear power plants is being planned next to large datacenters to keep vastly inefficient GPUs supplied with the hideous levels of energy they require to operate. Moreover, it costs billions of dollars to train a large AI model, a cost that increases sharply from one generation to the next. The reason? GPUs so inefficient that many thousands are required to do the job.

If AI is to fulfil its promise in society, this cannot continue. Designing efficient AI chips from the ground up—tailored to the characteristics of AI workloads—now takes precedence over time-to-market and engineering costs. *AI is not videogame graphics*; acknowledging this glaring fact is the first step to grasping Europe’s opportunity.

**AI needs precision engineering**

Today’s chip design giant, America’s NVIDIA, has catapulted to previously inconceivable heights on the back of serendipity: they originally made chips for videogame graphics, not AI. That’s why their chips are still called “Graphics Processing Units,” or GPUs. When transformers—the enabling algorithms behind AI chatbots—were developed, it turned out that one could program them on existing GPUs, instead of waiting for tailor-made hardware. From that point on, the explosion of the AI market has relentlessly gobbled up ever greater numbers of GPUs, and NVIDIA never looked back. Why would they? It is plausible that they make a lot more money selling vast numbers of inefficient parts than economically rational numbers of carefully considered solutions—as long as there are customers willing to foot the bill. This is why we now live in a world where AI datacenters pretend that a transformer—with its intrinsically local, distributed data and control flows—is a giant videogame operating on global data; just about the worst thing conceivable when it comes to efficiency.

NVIDIA’s greatest advantage—the fact that they happened to have something usable and ready to go when AI became commercially attractive—is also their Achilles’ heel: they are now stuck with an entire paradigm and ecosystem that is catastrophically inefficient. The word ‘catastrophically’ isn’t an exaggeration: according to the International Energy Agency, the total electricity consumption of AI datacenters will be 945 TWh in 2030, or about 3% of the entire world’s electricity consumption. Imagine what it will be a mere 15 years from now, if we keep on accepting inefficiency.

Europe’s long and celebrated precision engineering tradition means that we are well-positioned to design far more efficient alternatives. While the USA and China are locked in an often-irrational race—both believing that they have no time to take a step back and reconsider what they’re doing, lest they lose the race—Europe can disrupt the status quo by doing something surprisingly lowkey: adequate chip design. In an increasingly irrational market, old-fashioned competence can be a superpower.

Take my own company, Euclyd, as an example: we reimagined the entire AI stack from the ground up, with an eye for efficiency. Instead of re-using off-the-shelf processors such as ARM or RISC-V, we designed our own, specifically for AI workloads. Instead of deploying a generic system architecture with a global operating space, we developed our own to capture the local data and control flows present in AI workloads. The result? A system up to a hundred times more efficient than NVIDIA’s in terms of cost, energy consumption, and physical footprint. Europe has plenty of engineers who can do the same, if only they are given the right goals, resources and strategy.

There is more than one way to leverage higher design efficiency, such as Euclyd’s hundred-fold advantage over current GPUs. The obvious one is to start with fab parity—that is, to make the chips in the same advanced Asian fabs that NVIDIA uses—and leverage Euclyd’s efficiency advantage purely as a market differentiator. Another way is to think of Europe’s latent design superiority as a kind of “budget.”

For instance, a company like Euclyd could hypothetically have its chips made in Europe and spend, say, 50% of its hundred-fold advantage to compensate for the lower efficiency that results from less-aggressive European manufacturing—in other words, better design balancing out manufacturing lag. The remaining 50% could then be leveraged against incumbents in the marketplace. Even if Euclyd were to spend 100% of its design advantage merely to compensate for Europe’s lagging fabs, we could perhaps still have a sufficiently competitive product to kickstart Europe’s sovereign AI chip supply chain.

**Europe’s manufacturing strengths**

Even when it comes to chip manufacturing alone, Europe isn’t [a barren land](https://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-the-west). It’s true that we never made the transition from planar transistors to FinFET technology—the latter being a more efficient transistor architecture—but the transition itself represents a trade-off.

In FinFET transistors, a thin and tall structure called the “fin”—think of a shark’s dorsal fin—poses a thermal challenge and is prone to manufacturing defects. Fins are mechanically fragile and tend to bend or collapse during manufacturing steps such as chemical-mechanical planarization (a glorified form of sanding). During operation, heat tends to form at the top of the fin, so to keep the structure from melting the heat must be forced down the tall and narrow body of the fin, which isn’t as easy as cooling a good-old planar transistor. As such, the latter—which Europe excels at making—has advantages when it comes to manufacturing yield, device cooling, and robustness, all of which are critical for AI datacenters.

Moreover, unlike Asia, Europe has kept on developing planar technology for the past couple of decades. [A coalition of European partners led by France’s CEA-Leti is now working on 10 and 7nm planar nodes](https://www.leti-cea.com/cea-tech/leti/english/Pages/What's-On/Press%20release/cea-leti-announceslaunch-of-FAMES-pilot-line-as-part-of-EU-chips-act-initiative.aspx)—not leading edge, but not laggards either. [Another European coalition is developing highly advanced 3D chip packaging technology](https://www.leti-cea.com/cea-tech/leti/english/Pages/Industrial-Innovation/Demos/3D-Integration-HPC-AI.aspx), which is a key enabler of both present and future AI systems. [Even more leading-edge developments are taking place at Belgium’s IMEC](https://www.imec-int.com/en/what-we-offer/development), a world-leading research institute for advanced semiconductor manufacturing technology. And on the memory side of things, [a new German initiative on fast non-volatile storage](https://www.ferroelectric-memory.com/) can have significant impact on AI, which is an extremely memory-intensive application. The list goes on.

The problem is less that Europe doesn’t have competitive chip manufacturing, and more that its efforts are fragmented and lack the focus of a continent-wide push like a “Manhattan Project” for AI, or what the Center for Future Generations has called a “CERN for AI.” We admittedly lack cohesion and sheer hunger, not latent technological prowess.

**A short-term blueprint for true European AI sovereignty**

Through [world-class AI-specific chip designs](https://euclyd.ai/) such as the Netherland's Euclyd's, [advanced planar nodes](https://www.leti-cea.com/cea-tech/leti/english/Pages/What's-On/Press%20release/cea-leti-announceslaunch-of-FAMES-pilot-line-as-part-of-EU-chips-act-initiative.aspx) such as those now being developed by France’s CEA-Leti and partners, [advanced packaging technologies](https://www.imec-int.com/en/expertise/cmos-advanced/connect/3d-integration) such as those being developed at Belgium’s IMEC, [advanced power electronics technology](https://www.infineon.com/applications/information-communication-technologies/hyperscale-computing) such as Germany’s Infineon’s, [advanced non-volatile memory technology](https://www.ferroelectric-memory.com/) such as that being developed by Germany’s Ferroelectric Memory Company, [advanced “cross-over” MCU designs](https://www.nxp.com/products/processors-and-microcontrollers/arm-microcontrollers/i-mx-rt-crossover-mcus:IMX-RT-SERIES) such as the Netherlands’ NXP Semiconductors', [emerging European server-class CPUs](https://sipearl.com/) such as those made by France’s SiPearl, etc., Europe can make a credible push towards AI hardware sovereignty.

Admittedly, this won’t make European chips competitive in the consumer/hyperscale datacenter market—there, the volumes and margin pressures are just too high for anything but bleeding-edge technology across the entire value chain. But in key sovereign application spaces, such as government, corporate, military, and financial systems, there are real opportunities for European hardware autonomy. These spaces are less price-sensitive and require significantly less volume, reducing the impact of Europe’s manufacturing handicap. At the same time, they entail extremely sensitive data—citizen databases, corporate trade secrets, military technology, economic data, etc.—and are crucial for Europe’s ability to remain out of foreign control. Precisely in these spaces, where the need for sovereignty is highest, current European handicaps have the least impact.

\_\_\_

European AI sovereignty across the entire value chain *can* be achieved before this decade is out.

\_\_\_

To achieve sovereignty, we will need proper policy and strategy. Although there is much funding available for AI-related technology in Europe, this funding is usually sprinkled across hundreds of budding companies, which then each get but a little. *This ensures that not one of them has a realistic chance to succeed*, for developing deep-tech for AI datacenters requires hundreds of millions of Euros per company, not ten or twenty. We need to focus our investments on the most promising players—regardless of which particular country they happen to be in—despite the risk that we might choose wrongly. And we need to make these investments without requiring companies to produce paperwork instead of products.

We then need to develop hunger for independence, belief that we can achieve it, and the courage to try by deviating somewhat from traditional markets, strategies, and ways of thinking. The world has never been a more dynamic, changing place. To *not* change along with it is to guarantee a bleak future. As much as the automotive industry is and—I strongly believe—will continue to be Europe’s stronghold, diversification is possible by leveraging technology across markets. There is no world in which this isn’t a good thing.

Finally, we need continent-level alignment across all relevant players, which can only be achieved if we take the time to talk to each other and take each other seriously, without hiding in the corner of our individual, pre-approved, time-honoured business strategies and goals.

With all this in place, European AI sovereignty across the entire value chain *can* be achieved before this decade is out. Not only is this feasible, it is also the only way to safeguard our way of life and ability to choose our own destiny in a world increasingly hostile to our values, and which now—uniquely in recent history—threatens us from both east and west.
