# An anthropological guide to the elections

*Why we get the leaders we do*

**By Harvey Whitehouse** | *2nd July 2024* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,523 words*

![An anthropological guide to the elections](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Why-we-get-the-leaders-we-do.webp)

---

*With a year of elections and the rise of populist parties across Europe, politics appears to be changing fast. But some things always remain the same. Anthropology can give us insights into how to manage our evolved psychology to avoid polarization and extreme ideologies. Harvey Whitehouse argues that we need to harness the inheritance of our evolutionary history more wisely, to produce leaders capable of increasing human flourishing rather than dividing and exploiting us.*

As we look back on 14 years of Tory misrule, we must consider the role of leadership in the incoming Labour government. The Tory party which we once associated with fiscal responsibility has played havoc with the economy. The people we once thought cared about Britain’s standing in the world have weakened our voice in Europe and the world. How we now treat the poor and destitute is a blight on our national character. However, turning things around will require more effective leadership. Now that Starmer no longer leads a party in opposition, we need him to become a different kind of leader.

But where should Starmer look for inspiration? Contemporary pundits may wish to point you in the direction of Thatcher or Blair who also experienced landside victories. Or they might point to models our grandparents admired: Churchill our war leader or Attlee who presided over the creation of our welfare state. But as an anthropologist, I would argue that such comparisons are very superficial. We need to look deeper into our evolutionary past.

For nearly four decades, I have been studying the evolved psychology of leadership and followership and the ways in which this has been harnessed and extended over thousands of years of world history. In my new book – [*Inheritance: the evolutionary origins of the modern world*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/gp/product/1529152224?tag=prhmarketing2552-21) – I describe innumerable experiments my colleagues and I have conducted, cross-cultural surveys we have carried out, fieldwork we have undertaken, and vast databases we have constructed and analysed. Based on all this research, I humbly offer him the following advice.

There are basically two kinds of leaders: [big men and chiefs](https://www.jstor.org/stable/177650).

\_\_\_

\_\_\_

Big men (and historically they have usually been men but of course they could also be women) are largely self-made. They fight their way to the top by persuading people to get behind them. In return for the support and loyalty of their factions, big men offer in return protection and resources. But they are also statesmen who manage the group’s relationship with outsiders – including the leaders of potentially hostile groups. The success of such leaders depends on striking the right balance between internal popularity and external renown. The more the big man pours resources into wars and alliances, the more his faction will be exploited. But the more he prioritizes the faction, the weaker will be his position as a statesman.

Big men succeed by finding the sweet spot. But it never lasts. Eventually, they lose their grip or make some fatal error. Then they come crashing down. Since their ascent to power depended all along on personal achievements, nobody could inherit their position once they have vacated it. Every big man is therefore doomed to be usurped by another big man who must create a faction of his own. Following this model, it is well-nigh impossible to build a foundation for heritable institution building and the types of cumulative advancement this can offer to society at large. Big men no doubt dominated much of our collective prehistory, when human societies remained relatively small-scale and cooperation across groups was fragile and limited.

Chiefs are a very different kind of leader. They derive legitimacy from qualities that are ascribed to them by virtue of holding office. Rather than having to persuade or bully us into following them, we trust them because deep down we feel they have the right to rule. Why?

The answer lies partly in research my colleagues and I conducted in a psychology lab with preverbal infants. When our babes-in-arms were shown videos in which an agent with supernatural powers lost out in a standoff with an agent lacking such powers, [they expressed surprise](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-89821-0) by staring at the screen for longer. This intuitive response has been exploited by many kinds of leaders over the millennia – from divine kings to popes and ayatollahs. But our innate trust in the chief does not need to take the form of ideas about living gods, it is much more often manifested in the idea of some kind of ascribed charisma – what some Polynesians describe as *mana* or my fellow Brits as the ‘blue blood’ of royalty.

\_\_\_

The world could easily be destroyed by big men – think Trump, Johnson, Berlusconi, Gaddafi, Netanyahu

\_\_\_

Democratically elected PMs are obviously self-made big men or women to a large extent but they would be foolish to ignore the value of ascribed mana as part of their leadership toolkit. The best way to tap into mana is through ritual – especially the kind of rituals that link to ancestral spirits of the past via time-honoured tradition. A word of warning to Starmer: if you abolish the monarchy and the House of Lords, you will remove two of the most effective pathways to legitimacy for yourself and for future heads of state.

The world could easily be destroyed by big men – think Trump, Johnson, Berlusconi, Gaddafi, Netanyahu… But some of the most powerful regimes in today’s world are also led by more chiefly figures – the Putins and Xi Jingpings of this world whose ascribed right to rule makes them relatively invulnerable to ousting via democratic means. At my college in Oxford, we have created what we call [the ‘deadly despots’ project](https://callevacentre.org/current-projects/) aimed at predicting which of the world’s leaders are most likely to use mass violence to achieve their ends, based on [award-winning research](https://anthro.web.ox.ac.uk/article/work-predict-extreme-violence-amongst-online-users-wins-mrs-presidents-medal) we previously developed to detect violent extremists before it’s too late. We have discovered a [deadly cocktail](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1057610X.2022.2109244) of psychological ingredients that could help predict commitment to acts of terrorism based on signals hidden in what we say – the things we write and the orations we deliver. Another word of warning to Starmer: we will be analysing your speeches closely too.

\_\_\_

In a world where military technologies are increasingly deadly and intergroup grievances ever more toxic, barrier-bound leaders pose a serious threat to regional and global cooperation.

\_\_\_

Many leaders – whether big men or chiefs – are dedicated to the advancement of their own groups at the expense of others. I refer to these as ‘barrier-bound’ leaders. These are people who regard their in-group – often their own country but more commonly now in polarized democracies some coalition within it – as all that matters. Foreigners or those who align with the opposing party can go to hell in a handcart for all they care. In a world where military technologies are increasingly deadly and intergroup grievances ever more globalized, barrier-bound leaders pose a serious threat to regional and global cooperation. What we need instead are barrier-crossing leaders. Think of those people – from heads of state like Clinton and Blair to local politicians like Trimble and Hume – who worked tirelessly to establish the peace process in Northern Ireland. Or Mandela and Tutu who struggled to rebuild South Africa in the wake of apartheid. What is special about such individuals?

To find out, my colleagues and I [ran a study with leaders of embattled groups in diverse environments](https://scholarworks.umb.edu/nejpp/vol34/iss2/7/) – Muslims in London, Travellers in Ireland, African-Americans in Louisiana. Roughly half our sample were barrier-bound leaders and half were barrier crossers. How did they differ? The key difference lay in their ability to recognize shared suffering on both sides of the intergroup conflicts they knew best. Awareness of this made them better able to work with outgroups to achieve win-wins. We have shown that this works also for the population at large. Merely drawing people’s attention to shared suffering on both sides of the Israel-Gaza conflict could move the dial towards more peaceful outcomes even among those strongly aligned with one side or the other. This way of thinking can be [scaled up also to humanity at large](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsos.231632) – something that may be vital in addressing global cooperation problems, such as the climate crisis.

In sum, my advice to Starmer as our incoming leader is threefold. If you want to be *popular*, you need to become an effective big man by achieving a reputation for being supportive internally and strong externally. If you want to be *enduringly popular*, you need to become a chief with ascribed authority (*mana*) so that we can trust you and accept deep down that you have the right to rule. And if you want to be *popular for good reasons*, we need you to be a barrier-crosser – a leader who can truly serve an entire country rather than just a party or coalition, and who can act in the interests of the world as a whole when required. We need such a leader now more than ever.
