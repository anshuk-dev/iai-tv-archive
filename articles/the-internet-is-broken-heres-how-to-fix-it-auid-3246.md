# The internet is broken, here's how to fix it

*The real internet revolution starts now*

**By Maurizio Ferraris** | *3rd July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 23 mins* | *4,568 words*

![The internet is broken, here's how to fix it](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-internet-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it.webp)

---

*AI and the internet have given us more access to data than ever before. Yet we sit on this wealth of knowledge and do nothing with it. [Italian philosopher Maurizio Ferraris](https://maurizioferraris.eu/) argues for a radical reform of the internet grounded in his new realist philosophy. He proposes a system of "webfare" where institutions cash in on the data troves they posses and redistribute profits to those in need. The digitalisation of everything in the modern world can often feel like a curse. Yet Ferraris offers hope for a future in which the internet can become the basis for a decentralized and more equitable society.*

**Let’s start with the metaphysics behind your theory. What is a social ontology?**

It is an ontology that takes this interview, for instance, seriously. An interview is not something that we can touch. It exists only if it is recorded. If the recording of an interview does not work, the interview disappears.

It always works this way. What applies to an interview also applies to a marriage, a bank account, even a promise. We, for instance, promised to see each other today at 6pm. This is not something that merely circulates in our minds. It is something that exists in the world. For instance, if my train were delayed, I would have had to call you, reach you in some way to tell you, sorry, my train is late. It would have been unacceptable for me to simply disappear without saying anything.

This shows us that often philosophers misunderstand the nature of social ontology. For instance, a great philosopher like [David Hume said that a promise is the expression of our will](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/2/5). But it’s not simply an expression of our will. Because once I make a promise, that which I promised no longer depends merely on my will – I could obviously decide to be a liar, to not keep my promises and so on. But the fact remains that that which I promised remains recorded, as banal as it may be. Let’s go to the cinema tonight. If you change your mind and no longer want to go to the cinema, you must inform the other.

So how is it that we are so bound by invisible things? They are invisible and intangible, though the bases on which our actions (actions which become objects) are then recorded are neither invisible nor intangible.

\_\_\_

As we know, we have never produced as many documents as we do now. The main characteristic of the digital age is the fact that everything that passes through the digital realm gets recorded.

\_\_\_

Let’s take the example of the promise. I make a promise. This is an act. This act has value only if it is recorded. To be precise, in the mind of the person to whom I make a promise. If the person did not hear, if she or he did not understand, then the promise is imperfect. A perfect promise – let’s take the case of a marriage. Two people formally promise to be together, something they generally won’t keep (but that’s a different story), and they create an object: a document, a marriage certificate. We should also note that in events like marriages and graduations, we often use witnesses. And now many people take photos. Why do we take all those photos? To give the sense that the act has become an object. To fix an act. It’s like all those photos we see of two political leaders shaking hands before a group of journalists. They have already shaken each other’s hands, they will shake them again, but the truth is that they shake them for a surreal amount of time to allow for everyone to take a photo of that which has become an act. It is as if they signed a contract.

So these are often invisible things, but not immaterial ones. For a long time, documents were made of paper and they occupied a huge amount of space. Now, they are concentrated in terabytes that hold all our memories – and they continue to be recorded.

As we know, we have never produced as many documents as we do now. The main characteristic of the digital age is the fact that everything that passes through the digital realm gets recorded. In the analogue era this was not the case. In the analogue era, you could speak and then forget what you had said, or not leave any trace of it.

In the digital world, every message we send is recorded. Even our voice becomes a form of writing because voice notes, for instance, have the characteristic of iterability that is unique to writing. Writing is that which I can read every time I want, while the spoken word is a unique act. The moment that the spoken word is recorded, it can be indefinitely repeated, just like writing.

**So the recording of an act is done to give it meaning, or to make it real?**

The recording gives existence to things. The thing exists or the act exists only if recorded. We often think only of the act and not of its registration.

In this sense I am speaking of a social ontology – because it deals with things that actually exist. A social ontology is comprised of social events, such as a battle, and social objects, such as a mortgage or a bill. And the definition of a social object is, firstly, that it must be an act involving at least two people – or else it is something we do merely in thought. If I take a ten pound note from my left pocket and move it to my right pocket, I am not lending anyone ten pounds. But if I take a ten pound note and I give it to you, who are another person, an *alter ego*, then this is a loan. So at least two people are necessary, though a million people could be involved. And, secondly, the act must be recorded on something of any kind: paper, stone, papyrus, parchment, the digital, or even simply in the mind of another person, because most of the things or commitments we take in life live in the minds of others.

Interestingly, the more authority a person who does an act has, the more difficult it is to ask for more than their word to substantiate it. For instance, if I go to the King of England or to the Pope and I ask to borrow their car, and the Pope tells me: yes, sure, you can use the Popemobile next week, it’s free – it would be very impolite of me to ask for a document testifying to this fact. It’s the Pope, no one should doubt his words, they have authority and are an act in and of themselves. Whereas, on the other hand, if I’m in a taxi and I forget something and need to get out to fetch it, telling my taxi driver I will be right back, the driver might ask me to leave a ten pound note as I am a stranger, I don’t count for anything – it is necessary for a document to back me up.

Here we return to the definition: a social object is the result of a social act involving at least two people which is recorded on something. As the digital records everything, this has resulted in a frightening explosion in the number of social objects since the digital took over.

Social objects have two functions: they document something that happened, and they simultaneously give you the power to do something in the future. Money gives you the power to receive a service, a passport gives you the power to cross a border, etc.

**But what if we have a fake passport or a diary full of lies – are these still real, social objects?**

They are simply a fake passport, a fake pound coin, a fake diary. It is obvious that any real thing can be falsified. But we tend to assume that things are real. Evidently, an inflation in fake things is always seen negatively. It brings social disorder. We now speak of fake news. But the fake has always existed, now it has just quantitatively increased because the ways to disseminate it have multiplied. But we can look, for instance, at the war, when the Germans printed an enormous amount of fake pound notes. Why did they do this? To create social disorder. Because when you begin to doubt the value of the pound note you have, you enter into a state of uncertainty and of social disorder. It was an astute move. So you can use false documents to create real social effects.

**With the explosion of documents and recordings on the internet, has there been an expansion of reality? Or what effect has this had?**

Not an expansion, but a *capitalized* reality. In other words, you can do something with reality. You can do something with the human life form. In the past, most things disappeared like, as *Bladerunner* says, [tears in the rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tears_in_rain_monologue). Now everything is recorded, all our conversations, our books, the enormous amount of acts that human beings execute. These acts can be tied to our biology. For instance, my watch keeps track of the steps I take and so on. All this is recorded and creates an enormous archive – the number of [data collected in 2024 is enormous](https://explodingtopics.com/blog/data-generated-per-day), and they predict even more for 2025. These numbers are so hyperbolic that they do not mean anything. We can say *very many* and it would have the same effect.

\_\_\_

Why is our AI now so very good? Because in the last 20 years we have deposited such an enormous amount of data online.

\_\_\_

Now we look at AI as if it were some kind of miracle. But [this AI is actually not what Turing thought](https://iai.tv/articles/the-turing-tests-of-today-are-mistaken-auid-2790), because Turing wanted to train a machine that could think like a human being. But what we actually have is just an enormous archive that answers our questions. In fact, before today’s excellent AI, we had translation systems that worked wondrously, while before then they worked quite badly. Why do they work so well now, and why is our AI now so very good? Because in the last 20 years we have deposited such an enormous amount of data online.

**Has AI or the internet then enabled the maximum of human potential or of human nature?**

No, AI has simply recorded with a never-before-seen accuracy and automation the human life form. Because it records *only* the human life form. I have recently begun a research project called *Hybrid Intelligence*, which is meant to imply that human intelligence is constitutively hybrid. That which differentiates us from non-human animals is not our brain mass, dolphins have a bigger brain than us for instance, but the systematic use of technical apparatuses.

In the 1900s there was a psychological and philosophical doctrine called behaviorism. The idea is that we can’t know the mind of others introspectively. Introspectively I can only understand myself and I know that I often fool myself, which is why I have to go to the psychoanalyst and so on. On the one hand, introspection can thus be false. On the other, I can’t open someone’s cranium and look inside to know what they’re thinking. I won’t find anything, just neurons. I understand how people think from the way they behave. Behavior is what makes us know things.

Another point – the professors who studied behaviorism made experiments on their own students. Their students were all men, these were the 1950s, from the same social class, etc. – the result is thus totally distorted. We already know that even in more seemingly objective fields such as medicine, most literature is written about white men.

What is interesting is that now that we have zillions of data points at our finger tips, we can really achieve a granular understanding of the human without precedents in history. We are used to seeing AI and the internet from another point of view: that of social control. This is more the case in China than in the US. In China, online platforms are in the hands of the state and control the citizenry.

\_\_\_

Why should we leave such a profound and radical understanding of humanity in the hands of only those who want to sell products, and not those who want to understand human beings?

\_\_\_

Rather than seeing only the negative side of social control, we should see that the door is opening to a [new and richer human science](https://www.scienzanuovainstitute.com/intelligenza/maurizio-ferraris-on-scienza-nuova): the possibility to know humans through a dataset of their behaviors that is richer and more detailed than any we’ve ever known. And it doesn’t only concern 20-30-year-olds, but also children, the elderly, the most disparate ethnicities – because this technology has reached every kind of culture.

We should not remain simply paralyzed before this fact, allowing this wealth of knowledge to be utilized only by Bezos. For instance, Amazon has experimented with sending some customers products they did not order. And the rate of return is around 0.1%. This means that the customers were profiled so well that Amazon knew perfectly well the product they wanted.

Why should we leave such a profound and radical understanding of humanity in the hands of only those who want to sell products, and not those who want to understand human beings?

**What potentiality do *you* see in all this data then?**

We have the opportunity to have a descriptive psychology, a comparative anthropology. Every social science could be remade in light of this new archive that did not exist merely 20 years ago.

If AI and computer programs, moreover, are not created by prejudiced nerds – who are also quite ignorant – but by philosophers, psychologists, and competent people of all stripes, we could find the truth of human beings in that data trove.

**What about the ever-increasing number of bots online and in algorithms? Doesn’t their production of data and fakes jeopardize the human archive we are creating?**

I don’t think so. Because even when a robot produces something, it produces following a human impulse or instruction to do so. So it reproduces human prejudices and behaviors. It doesn’t reproduce the beaver’s prejudices and behaviors, for instance, because beavers don’t instruct computers.

More importantly, the enormous number of data allows for forms of auto-correction. Even on the basis of a million data points you can err. But on the basis of zillions and zillions of data points, and with computers advanced enough to process them, and with researchers who ask the right questions and make the right programs, the social sciences can be completely transformed.

Obviously there are enormous problems. For instance, who can access the data? This is a relatively simple problem. European citizens, for instance, can request a copy of their data from the platforms they utilize. Nobody does this because it is not interesting on the individual level. But if we put together millions of people asking for their data, and we put their data together… It is now possible to anonymize all the data collected. Not just for privacy issues, but also for speed, to make the data more synthetic.

**What’s an example of how this new social science would look like?**

It would mainly impact psychology and anthropology. They have always had difficulty being taken seriously from the other sciences because they relied on interpretation.

Traditionally, nature was predictable and humans were unpredictable. Now, nature is no longer so predictable, as [quantum physics has introduced uncertainty into the natural world](https://iai.tv/articles/zizek-quantum-physics-shows-reality-is-incomplete-auid-2940). On the other hand, humans are becoming extremely predictable precisely because of all the data we now have access to. This is proved by Bezos sending products to people who accept them.

\_\_\_

Freedom is born from knowing oneself. If there’s one thing we need as humans, it is to know ourselves.

\_\_\_

When I say these things, people assume I want to restrict people’s freedom. But freedom is born from knowing oneself. If there’s one thing we need as humans, it is to know ourselves.

And to have access to such an incredible and omnipotent data source changes everything. It is, moreover, incredibly authentic. Because when people speak, they often lie, either intentionally or because they are deceiving themselves. Tallyrand said that speech was given to man to conceal his thoughts. But concealing our actions is much harder. To conceal our thoughts, we need only not speak or tell a lie. But if, for instance, I want to eat an ice cream – sooner or later, they will catch me with it. It’s an undeniable action. And now the proof remains on our phone: which ice cream parlor, how much it cost, etc.

**And this in your opinion should inspire optimism?**

Theoretical optimism, yes. Knowledge is always good, that is my base assumption. So ignorance is always bad, knowledge is always good. Sometimes, it would be better not to know, but if I am in this profession, and I spend time reading books and speaking with people, it is because I believe that knowledge is a good thing.

Of course, we need to find the tools and the ideas to make use of this data. But it is definitely a positive thing. There are fields in which this is already obvious, such as medicine. Previously, data was dispersed – now medical professionals can immediately collect them. You go to a doctor, and now they know everything about you, all your behaviors and so on. You want to be cured, and so you are happy the doctor knows everything. If you were going to your bank for a loan, you might prefer they did not know anything, but that would be a different situation.

Here I am speaking about welfare, or *[webfare as I term it](https://www.transcript-publishing.com/978-3-8376-7176-6/webfare/)*. How can we ensure that everyone benefits from this technology? We cannot increase taxes because then people will vote for the right. This is, unfortunately, the banal truth. Even those who would benefit from an increase because they would actually be taxed less and receive more services, will vote right. So we can’t take money away from people, because that’s not what they want.

\_\_\_

If we combined all our data, we could invest the money in social questions, for instance assisting those displaced by automation.

\_\_\_

What we can do is to leverage the data that people produce, but do not use. Only about four or five people in the US use it, as well as Xi Jinping and his coterie in China. No one else touches them. If instead we managed to make use of them, we could capitalize on this data and the profits could be redistributed to those in need. This would involve, for instance, each individual requesting for their data from platforms and then uploading it on a different platform we could term *[communist, a platform you contribute to voluntarily](https://www.otto.unito.it/it/articoli/comunismo-digitale-di-cosa-parlo-quando-parlo-di-webfare)*.

If we monetized only the data we produced, there would be very little. We would get ten pounds a year.  That is not worth it. But if we instead combined all our data, we could invest the money in social questions, for instance assisting those displaced by automation.

Let’s take the example of a hospital or health center. Let’s say the hospital gives its patients the opportunity to consent to its collection of their data (the data they provide the hospital and also that which they ask from different platforms). The hospital would then have access to all the data, all the preferences and so on that Bezos or Facebook have access to.

But the hospital has an additional edge. It has *structured* data, which platforms like Facebook do not have. The hospital has an organized database. It has your name, your date of birth, the history of your diseases, and so on, all under your well-structured medical profile.

\_\_\_

Knowledge makes us free. If the access to this knowledge remains restricted to a few people, it will certainly lead to slavery.

\_\_\_

If you cross over this data with that from social media platforms concerning a much larger pool of individuals, you will immediately be able to establish correlations between different lifestyles and, for instance, diseases. After all, medical knowledge is often made up of correlations. This is especially true in the case of psychic ailments. Those who cure depression will often tell you that they don’t know why something works – they are essentially witch doctors. When it comes to the heart or organs like that, it is easy to understand. The heart is like a pump; one knows how to fix it. But a mind, a mind in a body with a history and so on, is much harder to predict.

If you, however, increase the samples on which you can verify trends and hypotheses, you suddenly have access to an enormous wealth of knowledge.

**So we are seeing the Hegelian shift from quantity to quality?**

That is exactly right. The weakness of behaviorism lay in the fact that its database was always quite modest, and therefore its predictions were not wholly credible.

The same goes for the economy, for instance. A planned economy is much more convenient than a market economy, because the market economy produces a lot of waste. But planned economies have not worked because they were unable to understand what was missing, or what was needed. The result was that, for instance, when I visited East Berlin in 1985, the book store sold only Schelling. In 1985, they printed Schelling, and then Hegel in 1986. Schelling is great, of course, but the problem is I would like to find everything in a bookstore – and in fact, people increasingly go on Amazon as bookstores are seen as inefficient.

**How will this bring us freedom?**

Knowledge makes us free. If the access to this knowledge remains restricted to a few people, it will certainly lead to slavery. Because if just a few people know everything about us, we will continue to be ignorant as we are. But the opposite is true if many people know. Science, after all, is precisely this. The difference between science and magic or initiate sects is that modern science is considered a public thing: everyone has access to scientific knowledge. It is thus essential that the knowledge we collect be accessible to all.

The mistake is those who often say: no, we should not access this knowledge because we will be opening a Pandora’s box full of dangers. But the Pandora’s box is already open. Whether or not the hospital from my example sells its data, the large internet platforms will end up knowing our information nonetheless. But instead, with the system I propose, we are the ones selling our data to the big platforms. They will always have use for it – Big Pharma, for instance, will want to develop something. And we take the money, to guarantee, for instance, efficient cures to those without means.

**If I understand your proposal for digital communism, then, we actually remain in this societal and economic structure, but add a digital-based redistributive mechanism?**

Yes, exactly. The great problem with communism was that it required a revolution to be realized. Revolutions are never a good thing because it has been proven that they always result in some kind of animal farm.

The interesting thing about data is that it is a reusable good. I do not have to take it away from someone to have it myself. If I ask Facebook for my data, Facebook gives it to me, but it continues to have it as well. [As a result, I do not have to make a revolution to have Facebook’s data](https://iai.tv/video/democracy-and-the-digital-revolution). If I wanted, instead, Zuckerberg’s money, I would have to start a revolution.

It is also essential that my proposal be carried forward through citizen initiatives. People say that the internet is in the hands of liberalism. But that is not entirely true. One piece, the American piece, is in the hands of liberals. But another big chunk, the Chinese one, is in the hands of Bolshevism. I am speaking of Bolshevism technically, as the people being guided by the party, as is the case in China. The Chinese, unlike the Americans, do see a redistribution of their use of the internet. You don’t find unemployed people in China, or Chinese beggars.

\_\_\_

If you increase the number of those who know something, you democratize the knowledge, and, most importantly, you remove the advantage of those who possess it.

\_\_\_

The problem is that if the redistribution is controlled by the State, you will be controlled in the minutiae of your life. In China, they do everything with their cellphones. They pay with their phones, order food with their phones, communicate on their phones. And everything on their phones is recorded and traced. [This becomes a panopticon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon): the government sees everything you do, and you become a subject. In China, many people are happy about this arrangement, because it is not a failed communism, as was the case in the Soviet Union. Their freedom is removed, but their wellbeing and social security is assured.

How can we restore social security under capitalism without taking away freedom? Through the idea of communist platforms, where the people themselves freely unite and then freely give their consent to the use of their data. And there must be an initial document that guarantees the use that will be made of this data.

**Would this initiative be based around individuals then?**

The best thing would be to rely on existing institutions. These already have a database and social legitimacy. If I go online as an individual and offer to create a platform, no one knows me or will trust me. A bank, for instance, would be different. Most people have a trusting relationship with their bank as they trust it with their money. Or a hospital. Or a university. Universities have an enormous amount of data on the behavior of their students, their professors.

This is a wealth that they do not make use of. Instead, they could be exploiting it.

It is best that there be no centralized organ because it would want control, which would reduce freedom. My proposal would be communism in the sense of *communes*.

**Wouldn’t the platforms who end up buying all our data, the Zuckerbergs or Bezos, ultimately constitute a kind of panopticon in themselves?**

Yes, in a way, but they would not be the only ones to know. We are increasing the number of people who know. We are removing the cognitive advantage of the few and redistributing it to society. The few platforms now have an undeserved cognitive advantage, and one they probably use poorly, because they are not, for instance, trying to find the cure to cancer, but rather using it to make money and buy five star hotels in Venice. This knowledge is being used wrongly. This is also because they have commercial interests, or political ones in China.

If you increase the number of those who know something, you democratize the knowledge, and, most importantly, you remove the advantage of those who possess it. When the conquistadores arrived in Mexico, for instance, the mere fact of having 100 horses and some weaponry allowed them to take over a fantastic empire. Because they had a technological advantage, they had things the inhabitants had never seen, and the inhabitants were astounded. An astronomer, for instance, could easily be taken for a soothsayer if people didn’t know a thing about astronomy.

We need to take away the role of expert in data, the fetishization. And with that, by utilizing that which we *already have*, we have the means to create a more equal society.
