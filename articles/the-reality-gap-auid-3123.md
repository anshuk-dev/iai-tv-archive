# The reality gap

*An Interview on Perception, the Brain, and the Limits of Knowledge*

**By Nadine Dijkstra** | *26th March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,298 words*

![The reality gap](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-reality-gap-nadine-djikstra-interview.webp)

*Our brains do not just passively perceive the world—they construct it. Research shows that imagined and real experiences can produce strikingly similar neural activity, raising deep questions about the nature of reality itself. As the line between perception and imagination blurs, what determines our sense of what is real? In this first HowTheLightGetsIn pre-festival interview, we speak with Nadine Dijkstra, a UCL cognitive neuroscientist whose research seeks to reveal the brain’s “reality threshold,” the role of vividness in hallucination, and what this means for mental health and future technologies.*

At the [HowTheLightGetsIn Festival](http://howthelightgets.org), taking place from May 23-26, 2025, the debate, [Imagination and the Real](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/the-big-ideas/debates-2?_gl=1*ll44aj*_up*MQ..*_ga*MTc0MzEyMjE3OC4xNzQyOTg2MjI2*_ga_FNCHD2V7Q2*MTc0Mjk4NjIyNi4xLjEuMTc0Mjk4NjIzMi4wLjAuMA..), will challenge our assumptions about perception and reality. Most of us assume that what we see and experience is fundamentally different from what we imagine. However, recent advances in neuroscience suggest that the distinction may not be as clear-cut as we think. Studies show that brain activity in the visual and sensory cortex can be just as strong during dreams and imagined experiences as it is during actual perception. Some neuroscientists even argue that our perception of reality is a form of “controlled hallucination,” an idea that resonates with the German philosopher Immanuel Kant’s claim that “imagination is a necessary ingredient of perception itself.”

This raises profound questions: Has neuroscience uncovered a fundamental truth about the role of imagination in shaping reality? Could researchers discover a brain mechanism that definitively separates imagination from perception? Or is it misguided to think neuroscience alone can answer such deep philosophical questions? In this interview, Nadine Dijkstra discusses her celebrated work, the historical experiments that inspired it, and the implications for our understanding of perception, hallucination, and reality itself.

**In the upcoming Imagination and the Real debate at HowTheLightGetsIn Festival, the core question seems to be the nature of the gap between reality and illusion. Your research has explored how the brain understands this distinction. Could you share how you came to see this gap, particularly in light of the Perky experiment and the broader philosophy of mind?**

Absolutely. My research has shown that when we imagine something, such as picturing an apple in our mind’s eye, many of the same brain regions activate as when we actually see an apple. There is a striking overlap in brain activity between imagination and perception, and we still don’t fully understand how the brain distinguishes between the two. Since the brain only has access to electrical signals rather than the external world itself, it must interpret those signals to construct our experience of reality. Recent findings suggest that vividness plays a key role in differentiating real from imagined experiences. If an imagined object is vivid enough, some people - healthy participants included - may experience it as real. This suggests that the boundary between perception and imagination is far more fluid than we often assume.

**I remember reading in an interview that during the COVID lockdown, you explored different research areas. What drew you to the Perky experiment specifically?**

The challenge in studying how the brain differentiates between imagination and perception is that, under normal conditions, they are quite distinct. If you compare brain activity when people are imagining versus seeing, any differences might simply stem from the presence or absence of visual stimuli. What you really need is an experimental setup where people mistakenly believe imagined objects are real or vice versa. While reading old research papers, I came across Mary Cheves West Perky’s 1910 experiment, which was the first experimental study of imagination. It has the great name *[An Experimental Study of Imagination](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1413350).* She asked participants to visualize objects while unknowingly projecting faint images of those same objects onto a screen. Participants didn’t notice the projections; instead, they assumed their imagination had become extraordinarily vivid. This was an elegant way to blur the boundary between perception and imagination, and it inspired me to design a more rigorous modern version of her experiment.

**Your work is often described as dealing with “controlled hallucinations” and the reconciliation of imagination with external reality. Given your background in neuroscience, how do you balance this view with the philosophical notion that all of reality is a constructed perception?**

As a neuroscientist, I try to avoid metaphysical speculation—I focus on what can be tested experimentally. My research doesn’t make claims about what reality fundamentally is; rather, it explores how we experience reality and how our brains construct it. I do agree that our experience of reality is a construction, but I hesitate to use the term “hallucination” because it has negative and clinical connotations. Nonetheless, our perception of the world is certainly a creation of our brains rather than a direct, unfiltered experience of objective reality.

**Your research introduces the idea of a “reality threshold.” Could you explain how this concept helps us understand the boundary between perception and imagination?**

The brain continuously gathers and evaluates evidence about whether something is real or imagined. Only when a certain threshold of evidence is met does an experience become subjectively real. This process ensures that we typically don’t mistake our imagination for reality. However, if an imagined experience is vivid and detailed enough, it can cross this threshold and be misperceived as real. Understanding this threshold can help us grasp not only everyday experiences of imagination but also cases where perception is distorted, such as hallucinations in clinical settings.

**Speaking of clinical implications, your work has been linked to understanding conditions like schizophrenia. Do you think we might eventually develop ways to assess and even correct errors in the brain’s reality-evaluation process?**

While I’m not a clinician, I believe the ultimate goal in clinical research is to help individuals function effectively in their daily lives. The challenge is in defining what constitutes a “correct” evaluation of reality—there’s a normative question about how the brain “should” work. However, if someone’s experiences interfere with their well-being or ability to function, understanding these mechanisms could potentially inform therapeutic interventions. This area of research is still in its early stages, but it has significant potential.

**Why is it so crucial to distinguish between reality and illusion? Is it just about individual consciousness, or are there broader societal implications?**

It’s essential for adaptive behaviour. If you mistakenly believe a pink elephant is in the room, you might panic, act irrationally, or be perceived as delusional. The ability to accurately distinguish real from imagined experiences is fundamental to navigating the world effectively. On a broader scale, this research has implications beyond clinical settings—it could enhance virtual reality technologies by helping us understand how the brain decides what feels real. Understanding these mechanisms could also inform fields such as artificial intelligence and cognitive science.

**Given the limitations of current neuroimaging technology, do you think we will ever achieve a complete model of brain activity, or is there something fundamentally unknowable about the brain?**

Technologically, we are making great strides—new methods allow us to scan brains in more naturalistic settings, which improves our understanding of real-world cognition. However, even if we were able to map every neural connection in real time, we might still struggle to interpret what it all means. That said, I don’t believe there is anything inherently unknowable about the brain. The complexity is vast, but I’m optimistic that we will continue making progress in deciphering how it constructs our experience of reality.
