# A world without certainty needs its own grand narratives

*We need replacements for Eurocentrism, not just criticism*

**By Ayşe Zarakol** | *8th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,336 words*

![A world without certainty needs its own grand narratives](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/AdobeStock-1953230853-copy.webp)

---

*From Western-centric visions of the liberal world order to the Enlightenment story of history as Reason’s onward march, we rightly distrust grand narratives. But, argues Turkish international-relations theorist **Ayşe Zarakol**, without them we struggle to make sense of the world. We also risk uncritically slipping back into outdated narratives without realising it. The way forward, she argues, is to build new, better grand narratives, that recognize themselves as products of their own time and biases, rather trying to justify the status quo from a position outside of history.*

We all operate with grand historical narratives in the back of our minds, but these days new ones are hard to find, except in the airport nonfiction section. Social scientists and historians are [not encouraged to produce them](https://iai.tv/articles/after-the-death-of-the-grand-narrative-auid-2965). This creates an odd situation where academics either produce highly nuanced empirical research that is underwritten by an unexamined zombie grand narrative, likely left over from the nineteenth century, or they settle for criticizing existing narratives for being reductive and Eurocentric. Few of us grapple with the question of how to replace the problematic grand historical narratives with better ones.

We need histories which make vivid what Charles Tilly called “[big structures, large processes and huge comparisons](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7758/9781610447720).” There are numerous reasons why such macro-history gets a bad rap these days, some fair, some not. First, there is the fetishization of method. Because doing macro-history inevitably involves synthetic work based on secondary sources, nowadays it is not considered proper academic work, compared to the intellectual manual labor of working in the archives with primary documents. One reason for this (among many) is that in the academy we are often asked to evaluate work that is outside our period or region of expertise. In such situations, methods become the common language, creating a twisted incentive structure for methods and methodology to matter more than substantive contributions. The way to stand out as a scholar has become to invent a new technique, creating methods fads across the social sciences. In such a climate, PhD students cannot take the risk of claiming that their methodology is to synthetically put together narratives from secondary sources. That would sound old hat.

Second, macro-history is often charged with reductionism and oversimplification. Many consider it to be an ahistorical approach to history, failing to take into account historical context, and the contingent and constitutive impact of particular events. My own discipline of International Relations, for instance, desperately needs to make macro-historical claims to support its claim to be a proper social science that can come up with law-like generalizations about human and state behaviour. But it operates most of the time with ossified, essentialised, schematic narratives about the past abhorred by actual historians: e.g. “the Westphalian System,” “the Concert of Europe,” “why the League of Nations failed,” “the bipolar dynamics of the Cold War,” etc. The charge of oversimplification is right, but the solution should not be to stop doing macro-history; rather, the solution is to construct better grand narratives.

The third charge against macro-history, following from the first two, has to do with how it has been used in service of teleological and Eurocentric narratives which legitimize and reproduce the social hierarchies of the present. For example, in International Relations theory, postcolonial scholars charge the mainstream of the discipline with Eurocentrism precisely because of the macro-historical narrative of Westphalia, which holds that the first and only international order was created in the seventeenth century by European states, who were also the first to invent and practice the notion of state sovereignty. Textbooks unproblematically jump from the Peace of Westphalia to the Concert of Europe to the Cold War system to the Liberal International Order in a top-down manner, paying no attention to how the rest of the world experienced these moments. Such narratives contribute to the sense that [history revolves around Western actors](https://iai.tv/articles/there-is-no-objective-view-of-earth-auid-3507).

But while grand historical narratives are often (and often justifiably) charged with Eurocentrism, the reverse can also be true: the fetishization of archival methods and [micro-histories](https://doi.org/10.1086/448699) can also reproduce Eurocentrism. Primary sources are not equally distributed, equally accessible or equally preserved (and the patterns of distribution often reproduce the inequalities and hierarchies of the last century). “[National” archives often reproduce methodological nationalism](https://iai.tv/articles/languages-are-social-tools-not-windows-onto-reality-auid-3349) in ways that are very difficult to transcend unless one is already coming at them with an alternative vision.

To be sure, micro-histories are desperately needed outside of the West to correct the imbalances of scrutiny European thinkers and institutions have received in contrast to their counterparts elsewhere. Yet mountains of such work are not enough to correct the epistemological imbalance we are dealing with, if the grand narratives of the nineteenth century remain undisturbed. This is because readings of archives or primary texts, including those of political theorists, cannot happen in a vacuum, without a bigger-picture background in which the text can be located. If we dismantle the Euro- and West-centric grand histories that have animated our modern world without replacing them with anything but micro-oriented work, those macro-historical accounts that we think we have dismantled through our brilliantly devastating critiques of Eurocentrism will simply live on as zombie common-sense versions of themselves, filling in the blanks wherever there are some­—and every account has blanks. Critiques are not enough by themselves. We need alternative grand narratives, and for that we still need macro-history. So how do we go about constructing better grand narratives?

The starting point has to be avoiding presentism. On the one hand, historical narratives are always interventions in present-day debates, this is inescapable. At the very least, whether they resonate with large audiences depends on whether people are ready to receive them. On the other hand, grand historical narratives that are constructed in ways that make the present seem inevitable, or that make it seem like the culmination of history, are best avoided.

This does not mean the “facts” of history are meaningless or subject to infinite interpretation, but rather that the same “facts” take on different meanings depending on the eventual outcome. Imagine you are wearing a red suit to a job interview. Your day goes well, you get the job: the red suit becomes part of your success story, something that made you stand out. Or your day goes badly, you do not get the job: the same red suit becomes part of the narrative as to why you failed, something that made you stick out like a sore thumb. In both scenarios, you are wearing the red suit, but the story around it changes depending on how your day ends, though it is not even clear if the suit had any causal impact whatsoever. History is always contingent and even—or especially—grand historical narratives should not lose sight of that fact.

Avoiding presentism also means being careful with the concepts that we use and spending some time thinking about whether they travel historically. If we are not careful, it is very easy to deceive ourselves that we found the equivalents of present-day dynamics in history, which confirm our current biases. This does not mean grand historical comparisons are not possible, but that it is better to trust grand narratives where the author has given some thought to historical context and change over time. In other words, even grand historical narratives should have a strong historicist sensibility.

Finally, grand historical narratives should have a degree of reflexivity and be transparent about the limitations of their claims. Because they invariably involve synthesis, they stand on the shoulders of more meso- and micro-level approaches. As granularity increases, so does the intensity of historiographical debates among experts. For some periods, the data is quite scant and open to many different interpretations. All of this should be acknowledged where possible. Macro-historical approaches may not be able to incorporate all such scholarly debates in the main narrative but they should still point the interested reader in the right direction.
