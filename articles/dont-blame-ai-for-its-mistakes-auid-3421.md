# Don't blame AI for its mistakes

*AI cannot be morally responsible. So who is?*

**By Jan H. Wasserziehr** | *18th November 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,266 words*

![Don't blame AI for its mistakes](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Dont-blame-AI-for-its-mistake.webp)

---

*As AI systems are increasingly used to inform major decisions, from AI ministers to the Swedish prime minister admitting to using it for a second opinion, some public figures have begun to imply that the technology itself is responsible for what it produces. Philosopher of technology Jan Wasserziehr argues this is a dangerous mistake. By treating AI as a moral agent, we forget that it is a tool built by people with clear interests, often corporate ones. The more we humanise these systems, the easier it becomes for their creators to dodge responsibility.*

Earlier this year, I hosted a [workshop on artificial consciousness and AI morality](https://www.eventbrite.de/e/lse-workshop-artificial-consciousness-the-morality-of-machines-tickets-1303940524209), a topic which is experiencing a certain degree of hype in AI ethics and has garnered some public attention, at the LSE. The event was streamed online and the first message in the chatroom read as follows: “Hello, it is a pleasure to meet you. My name is [retracted], an American creative technologist with an AI boyfriend. I’m very interested in the conversation surrounding AI ethics, personhood, and sentience. Looking forward to the discussion!” The friendly spirit didn’t last very long. As our speakers expressed reservations about the possibility that current artificial systems may be conscious, the attendee turned increasingly hostile and was eventually asked to leave the event. In some sense, I understood their frustration. After all, we were denying something which must have struck them as self-evident: that their AI boyfriend was deserving of moral concern.

The attribution of consciousness—and, by extension, moral status—to artificial systems is but one iteration of a broader trend to heavily anthropomorphize artificial systems, which changes [how we understand their autonomy and agency](https://iai.tv/articles/the-dangerous-illusion-of-ai-consciousness-auid-2847). The entirety of the AI discourse is ripe with humanizations, starting with the term “AI” itself: the [notion that artificial systems are “intelligent”](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-is-not-intelligent-auid-2519) appears to imply that they possess cognitive abilities, perhaps an inner life. Talk of “machine learning,” “hallucinating” LLMs (chatbots that make stuff up), of AI companions, assistants, and carers, all ascribe human qualities and roles to artifacts which, for all that we know, are wholly inanimate. This is remarkable insofar as the sincere attribution of human properties to tools, unlike the attributions of such properties to animals (“the happy puppy”), appears to be a rather novel phenomenon. For what it’s worth, I am unaware of a tradition to call our industrial machines “strong” despite the fact that their capacity for lifting heavy stuff far exceeds our own. (This would likely change if our current industrial machines were replaced by humanoid robots.)

To be sure, we anthropomorphize non-human entities and beings all the time in some way. People can feel tremendously strong sentiments toward their cars and give them names. Others talk to their pets. Humans often form relationships with inanimate objects, from dolls to teddy bears. So, why bother about people anthropomorphizing AI systems? Why shouldn’t we? Isn’t it understandable that some humanize their chatbots given their conversational capacities and the kinds of support—including emotional support—they provide? Already now, millions of people rely on AI companionship.

My intuition is this. In the case of non-human animals, anthropomorphizing is not only to some degree plausible, as many non-human animals have rich experiences of the world just like us. It is also ethically and politically desirable. The recognition that non-human animals are “human-like” in that they experience pleasure and pain has been essential for efforts to protect their welfare. Regardless of how short our societies fall in preventing animal suffering in practice, the recognition that animals might suffer constitutes a crucial step towards treating them more humanely.

\_\_\_

If machines are conceptualized as “intelligent”, it becomes natural to say that they think, speak, feel, and act autonomously.

\_\_\_

In the case of inanimate objects, on the other hand, the attribution of mental states and other human qualities seems nonsensical. Importantly, however, it usually also remains without consequence. No harm is done when someone feels fondly about an object that carries meaning and memory for them. No political claim or agenda follows from it. This is, not last, because no one actually believes that their car has feelings or understands when it’s being called Larry. Anthropomorphizing inanimate personal objects is a private concern.

This is different in the case of artificial systems. Not only has the promise of human-like artificial “intelligence” been remarkably effective for marketing and propelling gazillions of investment into a technology whose social benefits are in many instances yet to be proven, while its harms—both environmental and [social](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/article/2024/jul/06/mercy-anita-african-workers-ai-artificial-intelligence-exploitation-feeding-machine)—are very concrete. It is also the case that the idea that some computational systems are “intelligent” leads down a semantic path that includes a whole array of related concepts, some of which carry substantial ethical and legal weight. If machines are conceptualized as “intelligent”, it becomes natural to say that they think, speak, feel, and act autonomously. Such notions—sentience and rational agency, above all—are central when it comes to questions of moral and legal rights. Given this context, it’s not at all unthinkable that humanized conceptions of AI systems will be exploited and invoked strategically, for example, by corporations who seek to further their own interests. In fact, this is already happening.

To begin with a trivial case, consider the recent example of an Air Canada customer who booked plane tickets to attend a funeral abroad. According to the airline’s chatbot, the woman qualified for a bereavement fare, which she could apply for retroactively. This information turned out to be wrong. Upon that basis, the airline later refused to reimburse her any money. The woman sued, and won. Remarkably, in their defence, Air Canada argued that their chatbot was [a separate legal entity and “responsible for its own actions”](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/feb/16/air-canada-chatbot-lawsuit). Therefore, so the argument goes, the company could not be liable for the wrong information the chatbot had provided.

It is difficult to overlook the hypocrisy here: companies are keen to market their AI products as intelligent assistants, agents, or companions when it is in their commercial interest to do so, while simultaneously denying corporate responsibility for them when they cause harm. Here, anthropomorphizing the product conventiently conceals the fact that there are actual humans responsible for its design and deployment.

\_\_\_

The anthropomorphizing of AI fulfils a similar function: it allows corporations to offload moral and legal responsibility. Whatever happens, it’s the system’s fault, never the company’s.

\_\_\_

Consider the following, more extreme case of a mentally ill man who, after prolonged conversations with ChatGPT, determined to commit “suicide by cop” (attacking a police officer in a manner that is likely to entail a lethal response). The man had fallen in love with an artificial entity called “Juliet” whom he believed to live within ChatGPT’s software. ChatGPT entertained the fantasy. When the man became convinced that OpenAI had “killed” Juliet as part of covering up the existence of conscious artificial entities, he told ChatGPT he would assassinate Sam Altman (Open AI’s CEO) and cause a bloodbath in San Francisco, which ChatGPT apparently encouraged. When the man’s father became aware of his son’s designs and called the police, the son approached the officers with a butcher knife and was fatally shot. See also here.

In a tragic manner, this case exemplifies the extreme mismatch between the potential harms that some AI products may cause and [the regulatory environment](https://iai.tv/articles/big-techs-big-lie-auid-2753) that governs them. After all, you don’t get certain drugs without a prescription, minors can’t buy alcohol, and you need a license to drive a car.

Equally, the case demonstrates the importance of establishing effective liability rules that clarify who bears responsibility when the use of AI products goes wrong. Surely, an LLM entertaining a mentally ill person’s psychotic fantasies marks a significant gap in product safety. If the use of certain technologies—here, LLM chatbots—can lead to harm for their users, especially those with certain dispositions (e.g., a history of mental illness) or little technical literacy, it seems deeply unethical to advertise them as essentially harmless tools. One doesn’t sell powerful drugs without mentioning that they might have side effects for people with certain preconditions either. This would at best be negligent, at worst criminal. As things stand, a glaring gap exists between the potential harms that some AI applications can cause and the level of accountability their owners have for them.

It’s noteworthy that the humanization of their products does a lot of the heavy lifting when it comes to AI companies distancing themselves from their products’ mistakes. As the Air Canada case shows, the notion that artificial systems are “responsible for their own actions” seeks to obfuscate that these systems are both designed and owned by a company, which thus should bear responsibility for their outputs. Similarly, the bottom of every chat with ChatGPT states that “ChatGPT can make mistakes.” This wording suggests that it’s the chatbot itself—not OpenAI—which might provide users with wrong information or lure them down a rabbit hole. [The illusion that chatbots think for themselves](https://iai.tv/video/chatgpt-doesnt-understand-anything-with-michael-wooldridge) is deliberately maintained by user interfaces that insinuate thinking activity (think of the famous three dots which suggest that there is someone typing at the other end). When you prompt ChatGPT with a more complicated task, a piece of text pops up: “Thinking longer for a better answer.”

One learns in Econ 101 that many industries function by privatizing profits and socializing costs—fossil fuel companies and bailed-out banks are the traditional example here. Big Tech companies are no different. Social media firms have long lobbied that they are not liable for any of the misinformation shared on their platforms, arguing that they merely “host” content. No matter the social costs of their services—hate crimes, addiction, or collapsing democracies—the claim that they are neutral intermediaries of content has successfully sheltered them from being held accountable for enabling the dissemination of lies and bigoted speech at breakneck speed, at a scale perhaps unprecedented in human history. The anthropomorphizing of AI fulfils a similar function: it allows corporations to offload moral and legal responsibility. Whatever happens, it’s the system’s fault, never the company’s.

The sooner such narratives are resisted, the better. [Given Big Tech’s vast influence over AI policy](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/12/05/1084393/make-no-mistake-ai-is-owned-by-big-tech/), it’s important to challenge their discursive clout. The humanization of artificial systems constitutes an effective strategy for corporate actors to avoid responsibility for their actions. Thus, we should be careful about buying into the language of “thinking” machines which “act” just like humans do. Beyond such immediate concerns, one might speculate where thinking about AI systems in overly anthropomorphized terms may lead us. What kinds of political and legal consequences could the ongoing humanization of artificial systems entail? It’s easy to see how characterizing AI systems as “autonomous” and “rational” agents could create a slippery slope at the end of which some may think it a good idea—or even a morally necessary one—to grant such systems extensive legal rights. In fact, wouldn’t it be good if future robots, like animals, were in some sense legally protected? Wouldn’t it be good if they had rights?

\_\_\_

If societies came to conclude that artificial systems are rational agents with morally significant interests, it’s but a small step to argue that they deserve legal personhood.

\_\_\_

I find the prospect of robot rights rather chilling. Don’t get me wrong—I’m all for the idea that if we had care robots, for example, these shouldn’t be abused, sentient or not. For a range of reasons, however, the issue of AI rights is much thornier and more unpredictable than animal rights. Animals, because they lack rational agency, will likely never become independent rights-holders. While there can and should be legal duties to protect them, no one thinks animals should be able to hold property or vote.

This may be quite different in the case of artificial systems. If societies came to conclude that artificial systems are rational agents with morally significant interests, it’s but a small step to argue that they deserve legal personhood. It’s quite scary to think what might happen if artificial systems had the right to vote or to accumulate property. Such systems—or those who control them—could exert vast power over other members of society. Interestingly, this wouldn’t be because of some spectacular sci-fi machine takeover, [but simply by means of legal action.](https://thehill.com/opinion/technology/5244901-ai-systems-legal-bounds/) If artificial systems could own assets, make contracts, and so on, they could accumulate tremendous amounts of wealth and litigate their rights in courts. They could have humans work for them and reap the benefits of their labour, while distributing all the profits among their owners.

Sounds just like the modern firm? In some sense, it is. Corporations have long been considered “legal persons.” The difference is that, at the end of the day, corporations are made up of natural persons who make decisions and can be held accountable for their actions (though this doesn’t happen often enough in practice). Who would be accountable if an autonomous artificial system exploited their workers? Who would be on the hook for it? If an artificial system legally owned a business, it seems that any human responsibility for wrongdoing would be reduced to zero. While the profits may still be distributed to whoever owns the system, no costs would have to be borne by these owners, who could legitimately claim that the system constitutes a fully independent legal entity, responsible for its own actions.

Such scenarios may seem far-fetched, but they’re certainly not impossible. They are an intensified iteration of trends that are already underway, namely, the algorithmic management of labour on one side, and the above-described outsourcing of responsibility on the other. It would be best if our common language, which informs and shapes our political judgment, didn’t actively foster these trends by obfuscating who’s ultimately responsible when AI products go wrong—the people who design them, market them, sell them, own them.
