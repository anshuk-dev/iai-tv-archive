# Eurovision is more important than you think

*Europe needs cohesion*

**By Galia Press-Barnathan** | *15th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,736 words*

![Eurovision is more important than you think](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Eurovision-is-more-important-than-you-think.webp)

---

*On the eve of the Eurovision Song Contest, Europe finds itself in a precarious place. But this contest is more than mere glittery pop escapism, it is the most recent in a long line of events that have influenced politics both within and beyond Europe. Professor Galia Press-Barnathan argues major international events can be the glue that holds political groups together, which in these precarious times is more important than ever.*

Many people think that cultural events like the Olympics or Eurovision Song Contest should be detached from politics – in the same way individuals find refuge in music, or film, or watching football, or reading books, as a way to ‘escape’ the often complicated or boring political life. Do states host or take part in international cultural gatherings as an escape from complex political relations elsewhere? In this view, international cultural events are a social glue holding the international society of states together, even in difficult political times. If that is the case, then international cultural events should be protected from being politicized. This view, however, is mistaken.

\_\_\_

But rather about what politics is and what political agenda should be advanced through such events.

\_\_\_

It ignores both the nature of cultural products like major music and sports events and their potential impact, as well as the complexity of world politics. By better understanding both, it becomes apparent that the debate is not really about politics or cultural events, but rather about what politics is and what political agenda should be advanced through such events. Several characteristics of popular culture make it inherently relevant to politics. These include the nature of these cultural products, their dual impact (whether intentional deployment by states or unintentional, organic impact), and the different ways that the public views and consumes them.

*The nature of cultural products*:

Cultural products offer multiple visual, sonic and other elements that make them an effective medium to transfer ideas and information, and even more importantly, to generate emotions. They can operate within the special category of cultural mega-event which are noted exactly for this huge media exposure. Cultural products can play a crucial role in shaping, re-shaping and challenging multiple identities. This is true at the societal group level, the national level and the transnational level. They can shape all sorts of identities and understandings (much broader than those discussed here), but for those of us interested in world politics, they are important because they shape our geopolitical imagination. Much has been written, for example, about the impact of comic series like Captain America on the Cold War perceptions of young Americans, or on the inspirational impact of American music and film on ordinary people in the Soviet sphere, when they were exposed to them. [Even the Beatles were said to have inspired early dissidence against the Soviet Union.](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2013/apr/20/beatles-soviet-union-first-rip-iron-curtain)

\_\_\_

The Eurovision Song Contest, for example, is a platform to demonstrate self-proclaimed liberal values or to compete with other geopolitically more powerful states.

\_\_\_

*The dual impact of cultural consumption*:

The ability to shape the geopolitical imagination inevitably generates the ability to mobilize interest in these cultural products. It is interesting though that the political impact of cultural consumption can operate in two different ways. Most obvious for those interested in international politics and cultural events is the intentional use of these events to shape external opinions, promote the nation, express certain treasured values (those of the state or those thought of as valuable for the international community) or compete with rivals. Concepts such as soft power and cultural diplomacy are often associated with the efforts of states to use international cultural events as platforms for this purpose. The [Eurovision Song Contest](https://www.euronews.com/culture/2025/05/13/eurovisions-israel-constant-yuval-raphael-expecting-booing-during-this-years-competition), for example, is a platform to demonstrate self-proclaimed liberal values or to compete with other geopolitically more powerful states.

Alternatively, at a global level, the Olympic Games follow similar thinking. Participating is therefore politically important for several reasons – on the one hand, to influence and impress significant others on the world stage, and on the other, influencing one’s domestic audience as well. This being said, cultural consumption has crucial long-term impact that is also unintentional. The banal consumption of various cultural products has an impact on consumers that is not necessarily intended or manageable. While in the case of international cultural events the intentional dimension appears more dominant, for example the [2015 Armenian entry](https://eurovisionworld.com/eurovision/2015/armenia) to Eurovision marked the centenary of the Armenian genocide.

However, with such large events you can never quite predict what will happen. Think for example about the impact of Qatar hosting the soccer World Cup on the enhanced exposure to its internal practices and to issues of human rights abuses, or how the Eurovision contest turned into a magnet for large demonstrations against Israel and the war in Gaza in Malmo. Think also of the celebrities that are born in such events and may become political agents on their own. [The Streisand effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streisand_effect), whereby trying to suppress something only for it to become more popular than it ever would have been, is in full swing in international events.

*The nature of consumption*:

One final important observation about cultural consumption relates to the multiple ways in which cultural products can be consumed. Consumption can take place as a collective – either a national group (e.g., cheering our national soccer team) or another type of group (being part of Live Aid concert) – and it can take place at the individual level, outside the public sphere. Why is this important? To understand this, we should turn our attention to the nature of world politics today.

World politics today is comprised of multiple layers of actors and interactions: global-transnational, international (great power politics), national (mobilizing national body), and societal or individual (individuals playing global roles and experiencing the world). Political processes take place at all these levels, and today, perhaps more than ever, they interact between these levels. Therefore there are more different types of political actor relevant to world politics than ever: governments, great powers, small states, and transnational societal groups ranging from transnational advocacy networks to transnational fan clubs. Individuals also play different roles – as celebrities that gain political power (think of Bono, or Taylor Swift), or as an audience that can play a supportive or disruptive role.

These complex political dynamics are reflected in the workings of cultural mega-events or popular culture more broadly.  Such international cultural events play a role in providing an opportunity for different actors to advance their agenda or voice their discontent. These actors can be states, as noted before, but also other actors, whether participants in the event or protestors using it as a public stage. The more intriguing characteristic of these events is the intersection they often create between the national and the personal. This can generate various types of political behaviors that demonstrate how all sorts of actors become political agents in world politics. During the World Cup in Qatar, for example, some players chose to wear the LGBTQ-associated rainbow armband, and the [German team](https://www.npr.org/2022/11/23/1138967472/german-players-cover-their-mouths-at-the-world-cup-to-protest-fifa) players chose to cover their mouth in a formal group photo, to express their critique of human rights restrictions in Qatar. Some [pubs](https://inews.co.uk/news/pubs-refuse-world-cup-protest-qatar-lgbtq-rights-record-1975364?srsltid=AfmBOopK00Vnwsg2f2vmFiRZNZj5qaS-V2xN1sgDBs90OjNWuvRRurwO) and bars in the UK refused to screen the games for similar reasons.

One could argue that this World Cup competition has probably turned these players into global political actors, in role they may not have assumed otherwise. The Eurovision Song Contest right after the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](http://iai.tv/articles/we-cant-just-blame-putin-for-russias-crimes-auid-3000) also reflected these different levels. At the most formal inter-state level, the most obvious expression was the expulsion of Russia, but also at the societal level the competition became the stage for individuals across Europe to demonstrate their support for Ukraine, symbolically and materially, for example with fundraising campaigns.

 Furthermore, the competition and the dynamics surrounding it was part of an evolving new militarized liberal European identity that was being negotiated at the time. The most recent Eurovision contest in Malmo, Sweden, also clearly demonstrated the inevitable political nature of the competition and its unintended political impact, as it became a magnet for and an amplifier of transnational societal protest [against Israel](https://www.dw.com/en/thousands-protest-israels-eurovision-participation/a-69038273), and against the EBU for allowing its participation in the competition. These situations are inevitably political, as both allowing and banning a state like Israel will produce discourse.

Simultaneously, however, we also saw an intriguing and unusual gap between the jury votes and the popular votes regarding the Israeli song, Hurricane, [with the jury placing Israel 12th](https://www.timesofisrael.com/israeli-fans-pan-eurovision-juries-political-snub-say-it-denied-them-victory/), and the popular vote placing it second. It is both beyond the scope of this essay, nor is it its intention, to analyze what this gap means. It is suffice to say that this gap, as well as the analysis that followed it, demonstrated nicely how a song competition can generate so much political activity across so many dimensions. It also demonstrates quite clearly that such events are not only tools to be used to advance certain political agendas. Rather, they create a new political space where various interactions, intended and unintended, can take place.

\_\_\_

The question is not about separating culture from politics but about which politics one seeks to advance through it.

\_\_\_

These examples, as well as much of the political attention already given to international cultural events, focus on their political role in the context of resistance. While this is unsurprising it is, however, important to stress that such events' [political nature is not inherently conflictual](http://iai.tv/articles/trump-tariffs-and-the-lessons-to-learn-from-machiavelli-auid-3134). I began by noting that various international cultural events serve as a glue of the international society of states. This, in itself, is clearly a political observation. Creating an international infrastructure to enable states to compete ‘peacefully’ in various cultural spheres and create a habit of cooperation, even if competitive, is clearly a political project and a worthy one. Being political is thus not necessarily a bad thing.

Around this cooperative politico-cultural infrastructure, numerous contentious political processes are also taking place, by different actors, as briefly described above. However, the question is not about separating culture from politics but about which politics one seeks to advance through it. This interplay between the stabilizing glue-effect of international cultural events and the desire to use them to disrupt and protest is all political. Providing stability and normality in international relations, as well as challenging it are both clearly political. This interplay is at the heart of the new political space generated by such events, where actors of all sorts can now play.
