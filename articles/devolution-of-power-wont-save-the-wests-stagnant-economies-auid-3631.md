# Devolution of power won't save the Britain's stagnant economy

*Manchester's real lesson*

**By Joel Hoskins** | *27th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,956 words*

![Devolution of power won't save the Britain's stagnant economy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/26-07-10-devolution-wont-save-the-Wests-stagnant-economies-copy.webp)

---

*Britain's growth problem isn't a devolution problem. Handing power down to cities and regions has become the default prescription for stagnant growth. But economist Joel Hoskins argues that Manchester's transformation, the model most often cited to justify this approach, was never really about who held the power. It took three decades, four governments, and a refusal to keep starting over. That kind of institutional discipline has vanished across Whitehall, and no amount of devolved authority can substitute for it.*

Burnham is one of the few politicians with a proven record of creating genuine economic growth. However, the secret ingredients for that growth were political stability and a widespread willingness to deliver a plan. Both of those things are in desperately short supply everywhere you look today. Ask most people who follow economic policy and they will tell you, with conviction, that Manchester is a stand-out success story. The skyline has changed. The jobs numbers have changed. The mood has changed. For a country starved of good economic news, that story feels like a glass of cool water in the middle of the desert.

It is in large part because of that story that its former mayor is now sitting in Downing Street—many people are hoping that he can replicate the success across the UK. However, some key questions remain—how real is Manchester’s success? Why has it done so well?  And what does this tell us about the new Prime Minister’s chances of [changing the growth story?](https://iai.tv/video/the-holy-grail-of-economics)

**Has Manchester really grown?**

On the official figures, Greater Manchester has posted the seventh highest productivity growth of 41 comparable UK regions since 2015, and the City of Manchester itself has overtaken every large UK city outside London and Edinburgh.

However, despite being frequently used to prove Manchester’s success, [that statistic is seriously contested](https://www.productivity.ac.uk/research/the-manchester-model-what-foreign-inward-investment-reveals-about-the-uks-most-productive-city-outside-london/). One issue with the data is that there is a likely data error in Trafford’s legal and accounting sector, which has inflated the numbers for part of the city region. A second issue concerns suspicious revisions to hours worked, which conveniently flatter the North West while doing the opposite to London, which has [raised further questions about whether the underlying data can be trusted at all](https://www.centreforcities.org/blog/is-london-too-successful/). Finally, even researchers highly sympathetic to the Manchester story accept that they cannot yet explain why measured productivity has grown so much faster than wages or household income.

A second caveat concerns who benefits from this growth. Growth that pools in one part of a city while leaving everyone else behind does not provide a compelling model for the rest of the country to follow. The need for its benefits to spread widely is a reasonable concern, and one that is often levelled at growth-focused policymaking. However, [median wage growth since 2015 has actually been strongest in outer boroughs like Oldham, Rochdale and Tameside, not in the city centre](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/labourproductivity/articles/subregionalproductivity/2015-02-19), and real household income has risen fastest in Manchester, Trafford and Tameside together, all comfortably ahead of the typical English local authority. That does not completely remove the inclusion question, but it does mean that the crude story of a [shining centre extracting value from a neglected periphery is not supported by the data.](https://iai.tv/video/the-wealth-paradox)

So—the headline statistic is shaky, and the inclusivity question is unresolved. However, if you zoom out, the overall picture is undeniably positive.

International investors, who have no incentive to flatter the city’s ambitions, spent $13.7 billion building brand new offices, labs and facilities in Manchester between 2013 and 2025. That is nearly twice what they invested in Birmingham and more than two and a half times what they built in Leeds. Lenders have also come to see Manchester projects as markedly less risky than almost anywhere else in the country outside London, and the city has seen one of the sharpest such improvements of any UK region. Demand for office space has grown faster than in any comparable British city bar Bristol. Finally, the share of residents with higher level qualifications has risen faster than anywhere in England except London and the West of England.

Taken individually, each of these data points has issues that mean they are indicative rather than conclusive. Taken together, they provide very strong evidence that the city is building [genuine economic dynamism.](https://iai.tv/video/the-economics-of-almost-everything)

**Why has this happened?**

The story usually told about Manchester begins with a mayor. It should really begin much earlier, with two men that few people outside the city have heard of.

[Sir Howard Bernstein and Sir Richard Leese ran Manchester City Council for the best part of three decades between them](https://manchestermill.co.uk/the-man-who-ran-manchester/), one as chief executive and one as leader of the council. The strength of their collaborative abilities was tested in the run-up to Manchester’s hosting of the 2002 Commonwealth Games, when a serious funding shortfall left organizers and much of the press convinced the Games would end in humiliation. Indeed, one newspaper warned the city would come to regret ever bidding for them. The Games went ahead successfully, on the strength of a partnership between the council, national government and private sponsors that had been built up over years. [Bernstein went on to serve as chief executive until 2017](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-manchester-39455363), a tenure that ran across four different Prime Ministers and three changes of government, while the same underlying partnership with the council leadership stayed in place throughout. That habit of collaboration predates devolution, predates the mayoralty, and long predates Andy Burnham’s arrival in Manchester politics.

The first devolution deal was signed in November 2014. [The first elected mayor took office in 2017](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-manchester-39822225). Full bus franchising, the flagship transport reform, did not complete until January 2025, over a decade after the process began. Greater Manchester’s success, then, was not the product of any individual policy decision. It took nigh on a decade of the same plan being carried through several different stages without being abandoned partway. One former senior council official described the city’s universities, its combined authority, its business community and its council as “joined up at the hip,” which allowed them to act collaboratively and respond quickly to new opportunities.

This collaborative capacity is what investors have actually responded to. Foreign firms choosing to build in Manchester rather than Leeds or Birmingham cite the strength of its “joined up at the hip” structure more often than firms choosing any of the other three cities studied. Those are not the sort of assets that a government can announce on a Monday and acquire by Thursday. They are networks of relationships and trust that accumulate, or fail to, over years and even decades.

Many of the researchers who have studied Manchester’s performance are frank that they cannot fully separate how much of Manchester’s success came from devolution, how much from agglomeration, and how much from simple institutional stubbornness—perhaps because asking what the exact cause of the success is is asking the wrong question. The one thing nearly everyone involved agrees on, whichever explanation they favour, is that whatever worked has been allowed to keep working for the better part of thirty years.

The long, durable improvements did not look like a dramatic transformation at the time. The transport system took the best part of a decade to fully franchise. The housing fund needed years to prove its model before anyone would consider scaling it up. These improvements are the least newsworthy kind of governing imaginable, which may be why they have worked so well.

**Can this be replicated?**

The lesson from Manchester is an uncomfortable one for anyone familiar with the patterns of national politics. Real progress turned out to be slow, often ambiguous in its short-run results, and dependent on people continuing to pull in the same direction long after the initial excitement had worn off and long before the ultimate benefits were visible. Can the national political system manage the same kind of improvement?

At first glance, the omens are poor. At a national level, the instinct to treat every contestable decision as a scandal has calcified into standard practice. Any minister who makes a defensible but difficult judgment call on issues such as greenbelt housing, net-zero timelines, or high-speed rail routing now has to defend not just the decision but their own good faith. The UK has a press and a political culture primed to expect villains, and so it treats an ordinary trade-off as evidence of wrongdoing. This means that the safest response for anyone in office is to avoid making a judgment call at all. The corrosive effect of this incentive structure makes the government much slower to deliver real change.

\_\_\_

The cardinal sin of British politics is a philosophical one.

\_\_\_

[The dangers of sticking to an old plan](https://iai.tv/articles/democracy-and-the-delusion-of-longtermism-jonathan-sumption-auid-2605)—and risking being blamed for any teething issue you have to deal with—can be avoided by simply abandoning it and launching a shiny new one. [This underlies one of the great pathologies of the British State](https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/publication/report/all-change), where new governments constantly relaunch new initiatives to address the same underlying problem every few years, since continuing someone else’s program rarely wins headlines the way announcing a fresh one does. The project of giving England’s city regions a coherent framework for growth has been announced anew at least four times in fourteen years, first as a round of Devolution Deals, then a Levelling Up White Paper, then an English Devolution White Paper, then a fresh Bill, each pitched as a new beginning rather than the next chapter of the last one.

The cardinal sin of British politics is a philosophical one. It is the belief that arriving at the correct policy, and then obtaining the undiluted power of the premiership, is sufficient for success. This is a seductive view, and a fatal one. Durable achievement depends on building consensus, working out who needs to be kept on board, and maintaining the relationships that keep delivery consistent long past any single politician’s time at the top. Manchester’s success was never authored by one mayor, minister, or official acting alone. It was authored by a council leadership, a business community and a set of universities that all kept turning up to the same table long after the novelty of doing so had worn off. Westminster’s failure was authored by a succession of deeply intelligent individuals, who cared deeply about the problems they wanted to solve, and who lost power as quickly as they took it.

The philosophical trap is [perfectly illustrated by the recent downfall of Keir Starmer](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/ng-interactive/2026/jun/22/the-rise-and-fall-of-keir-starmer-where-did-it-all-go-wrong). His tight, centralized, managerial style of government alienated his colleagues and potential collaborators, and he burned through their patience within two years. We can hope that Burnham’s time in a very different political culture has socialized him into governing in a very different way.

Collaborative policymaking, of the kind practiced in Greater Manchester for three decades, can sound like an empty platitude when promised from a podium. Indeed, it is easy to dismiss as a vibe rather than a serious governing strategy. But if genuinely delivered at national scale, it would represent one of the most significant changes to the British state in decades.

None of this guarantees Burnham will succeed as Prime Minister. The scale is different, the pressures are different, and the temptation to reach for the same command and control levers that undid his predecessor will be considerable. He will have to build a collaborative engine from scratch, when he was previously able to inherit one in Greater Manchester. Nonetheless, we can say that Burnham is likely the best hope this country has had for genuine economic growth in a long time. Burnham is still facing a task that has defeated better-resourced governments with clearer electoral mandates. I wish him the best of luck. He will need it.
