# Climate politics is currently just hot air

*Freeing the environment from its academic prison*

**By Onofrio Romano** | *30th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,894 words*

![Climate politics is currently just hot air](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/hollow-politics-of-climate-science.webp)

---

*The climate has become imprisoned in academia, jargon, and empty rhetoric. In this piece, sociologist Onofrio Romano denounces his own discipline's hollow attempts to make a difference to the planet. He proposes a complete overhaul to its approach, dropping its tendency for hollow claims and demanding a focus on measurement and implementation instead. We should not be afraid of the "boring" sites of change. At the local and national levels, concrete carbon budgets and more must be implemented with severe punishments for those who violate them. Setting limits and brakes should be prioritized over constant innovation.*

Everyone now knows the script. We need new narratives, more‑than‑human perspectives, decentred subjectivities, ecologies of care. In the last decade, Environmental Humanities (EH) has grown fast, institutionalising journals, centres, and syllabi to meet the demand. [The field’s flagship journal (Environmental Humanities) launched in 2012](https://read.dukeupress.edu/environmental-humanities) with a programmatic invitation to “[think through the environment, unsettle the humanities](https://read.dukeupress.edu/environmental-humanities/article/1/1/1/8085/Thinking-Through-the-Environment-Unsettling-the).” The Anthropocene itself—[popularised by atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-26590-2_3)—has become our default epoch, a shorthand for human planetary agency and responsibility.

And yet a paradox persists. The more sophisticated our conceptual vocabularies become, the more elusive material transformation appears. Concepts proliferate; emissions do too. The discursive field expands; infrastructures, property regimes, and political time keep their own stubborn pace.

This is not an argument for abandoning theory. It is an argument for diagnosing a structural slippage: between epistemic novelties (how we know and say “environment”) and institutional inertias (how standards, ledgers, and public law actually move). The point is simple: semantic innovation is not strategic leverage. Theory that does not interface with the mundane sites of change—indices, permits, asset classes, procurement rules—risks mirroring the very political form it aims to resist.

So the question is no longer whether EH can craft better stories. It is whether it can furnish handles—interfaces with governance, design, and law—that shift incentives, change baselines, and bind future actors. “More-than-human” will remain a posture until it is also a “procedure.”

EH emerged, like many modern disciplines, in response to crisis. Its genealogy runs through deep ecology, animal liberation, ecofeminism, political ecology, Indigenous cosmopolitics, and the ecocritical turn in literature and culture. A partial map includes [Val Plumwood’s “ecological humanities”](https://australianhumanitiesreview.org/2007/08/01/a-review-of-deborah-bird-roses-reports-from-a-wild-country-ethics-for-decolonisation/) developed in dialogue with Deborah Bird Rose; the international journal Environmental Humanities (2012); and synthetic volumes—such as [Serpil Oppermann & Serenella Iovino’s *Environmental Humanities: Voices from the Anthropocene*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358853661_Environmental_Humanities_Voices_from_the_Anthropocene) and [their edited *Material Ecocriticism*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt16gzq85)—that helped stabilize the field’s canon and ambitions.

From the outset, EH positioned itself against technocratic “fixes”, arguing that ecological breakdown is inseparable from the stories, values, and institutions that organise human–nonhuman relations. That wager remains right. But the historical backdrop matters: the early 1970s did not deliver a “green Keynesian” recalibration of the welfare state. They delivered something else—neoliberalism. The same years that consolidated Earth Day and the Stockholm Declaration also registered the Nixon shock that ended Bretton Woods, the New York fiscal crisis that accelerated urban austerity, and the Chilean coup that trialled market shock therapy. The environmental “awakening” was real—[*The Limits to Growth* (1972)](https://www.clubofrome.org/publication/the-limits-to-growth/) modelled overshoot; [*The Population Bomb* (1968)](http://pinguet.free.fr/ehrlich68.pdf) galvanised public anxiety—yet the policy instruments that followed increasingly routed stewardship through markets, price signals, and disclosure regimes. What disappeared from view was the “system”: the capacity and the legitimacy to steer socio‑technical metabolism at scale. “Govern as little as possible” became common sense.

That distinction—between epistemic awakening and institutional retreat—sets the stage for the paradox that concerns us: a field devoted to de‑centring the human finds itself articulated to a regime that “de‑centres decision.”

In recent decades, “new materialisms” have reconfigured social theory and EH. [Karen Barad’s agential realism](https://read.dukeupress.edu/books/book/1068/chapter-abstract/151874/Agential-RealismHow-Material-Discursive-Practices?redirectedFrom=fulltext) dissolves the nature–culture binary; entities emerge in intra‑action, matter is active, discourse material. [Rosi Braidotti’s posthuman critiques](https://rosibraidotti.com/publications/type/books/) take issue with anthropocentrism while affirming vitalist, porous subjectivities. [Donna Haraway’s Chthulucene](https://read.dukeupress.edu/books/book/27/Staying-with-the-TroubleMaking-Kin-in-the) reframes our epoch as multispecies entanglement, committed to sympoiesis and “making kin.” [Actor–Network Theory](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40878163) (Latour, Callon, Law) asks us to grant symmetry to humans and nonhumans, mapping sociotechnical assemblages as they perform the world.

These moves have done valuable work: provincializing human exceptionalism; tracking infrastructures and devices; revealing the performativity of indices, models, and standards. They have also, at times, encouraged what we might call a “flat political ontology”: the migration of disciplinary humility (“we cannot assume the human stands above the nonhuman”) into a normative form (“we should not assume any privileged site of collective decision or control”). From there, three slippages follow.

First, the “scale of agency” dissolves. If everything acts, nothing decides. The political horizon drifts from conflict and institution‑building to care and voluntarism. [Care is ethically serious](https://iai.tv/video/a-world-that-cares); but when it becomes the political horizon, a politics of obligation risks becoming a politics of personal virtue—easier to perform in cultural spaces than to anchor in statutory mandates, budgets, or grid codes.

Second, the embrace of “contingency” can become a practical fatalism. [Quentin Meillassoux’s celebrated “necessity of contingency”](https://altexploit.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/quentin-meillassoux-ray-brassier-alain-badiou-after-finitude-_-an-essay-on-the-necessity-of-contingency-bloomsbury-academic_continuum-2009.pdf) is a metaphysical intervention, not a policy manual. As a cultural mood—hypercontingency—it can read as: “no one controls anything; everything is flux.”

Third, “life”—vital, hybrid, autopoietic—can drift towards [techno‑enthusiasm](https://iai.tv/video/the-communication-crisis) precisely where markets thrive. [Melinda Cooper](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvcwnt6d)’s genealogy of the bioeconomy shows how neoliberalism re‑sited accumulation at genetic, microbial, and cellular scales—turning life itself into surplus.

Neoliberal governmentality works by shaping environments, not dictating ends. It installs rules for competition, builds markets for externalities, and sets metrics that convert ecosystems into asset classes. It governs not by commanding, but by tilting choice architectures—what [Foucault called “governing the rules of the game.”](https://criticallegalthinking.com/2014/12/02/governmentality-notes-thought-michel-foucault/) [Hayek’s defence of competition as a discovery procedure](https://cdn.mises.org/qjae5_3_3.pdf) makes disequilibrium a virtue; the price system is an epistemic machine that outperforms planning. Ordoliberalism adds the legal layer: constitutionalizing economic freedom and the order of competition itself.

Here is the crux. When EH embraces “horizontality”—flattened agency, distributed entanglement, suspicion of “vertical” coordination—it mirrors the neoliberal form. Not its values, but its regulatory architecture. The “system” disappears from view; limits become privatized as individual ethics (recycling, veganism, offsetting), or outsourced to markets (cap‑and‑trade, biodiversity credits). The sites where collective power actually resides—indices, standards, permits, procurement, public finance, maintenance—slip into the background. Meanwhile, neoliberal policy moves precisely there.

If that sounds abstract, consider how sustainability has been institutionalized in our time: through taxonomies of “green” investment, disclosure standards, and reporting directives. It is “metrology as politics.”

Is there a way to keep EH’s strongest insights while regaining political traction? One path comes from [Kohei Saito’s re‑reading of Marx](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/marx-in-the-anthropocene/D58765916F0CB624FCCBB61F50879376). Saito embraces “ontological monism”—humans are nature—yet insists on “methodological dualism”: we must analytically distinguish between natural processes (which operate whether or not we exist) and social institutions (which can be redesigned). That distinction restores the legibility of intervention. Ice melts at a given temperature regardless of culture; emissions accumulate according to carbon budgets; and those budgets can be enforced (or not) by law, standards, and finance. Saito’s more recent popular work argues for “degrowth communism”—re‑organising production and reproduction around sufficiency, equality, and metabolic repair.

Two further resources help: [Walter Benjamin’s image of revolution](https://monoskop.org/images/e/e4/Benjamin_Walter_The_Arcades_Project.pdf) as the “emergency brake” on a runaway train; and Karl Polanyi’s analysis of how nineteenth‑century societies were torn apart by treating land, labour, and money [as “fictitious commodities”,](https://keywords.sites.ucsc.edu/2025/03/29/fictitious-commodification/) then to be re‑embedded by collective counter‑movements. The implication is not nostalgia but “design”, in order to establish “limits” and “rights.”

If the goal is leverage, EH can retool without abandoning its ethos. Five shifts follow.

> 1. From “awareness” to “metrology.” Change what counts by changing “how” we count. Standards, indices, and taxonomies are political instruments. Examples: “carbon budgets” embedded in law and procurement; “biodiversity standards” that privilege habitat integrity over offsetting; “critical infrastructures” recoded as public–ecological commons, with maintenance as a first‑order category rather than a cost center. This is precisely where ordoliberalism has long operated—embedding economic rules into constitutional orders. EH can contest that terrain with counter‑standards oriented to “limits, repair, and equity.”
> 2. From “care ethics” to “instituted care.” Care must be budgeted, scheduled, and staffed. That means labour law, public finance, and service design. Translate care into occupational health, toxic exposure thresholds, and just‑transition agreements in carbon‑intensive sectors.
> 3. From “more‑than‑human” to more‑than‑market governance. [Sympoiesis (see Haraway)](https://clarkbuckner.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/HARAWAY-SYMPOIESIS.pdf) needs institutions. If EH is serious about nonhuman claims, then administrative law and representational innovations (guardianship models, trustee regimes for rivers and ecosystems) are the path, not just metaphors. Haraway’s insistence on “making kin” gains teeth when rivers get standing and budgets, not just stories.
> 4. From “innovation” to ex-novation and de‑licensing. Set binding phase‑out schedules (coal, petrochemicals, industrial meat), then use innovation to solve the coordination problems of withdrawal (jobs, grids, land use). The emergency brake is not anti‑modern; it is a design principle.
> 5. From “planetary” to place‑bound leverage. Global talk is cheap; rule changes are local. Interface with municipal procurement, public land trusts, building codes, and zoning—boring sites where emissions, extraction, and exposure are materially determined.

“Isn’t this a call for top‑down technocracy?” No. It is a call for “democratic verticality”: the capacity to set and enforce rules collectively, with transparency and contestation. Neoliberalism did not abolish vertical power; it repositioned it—in central banks, trade agreements, and standards bodies. EH should contest those verticalities, not pretend they do not exist.

“Doesn’t flat ontology (i.e., neoliberalism) prevent human hubris?” As metaphysics, perhaps. As politics, it can disable agency at the right scale. The choice is not between hubris and humility; it is between institutionalized responsibility and diffuse ethics.

“Aren’t markets adaptable—why not price our way out?” Pricing can help at the margin; it cannot found a regime of limits. Hayek understood this: competition is a discovery device when we do not know the ends. Ecological governance begins by “setting ends” (caps, bans, phase‑outs).

“But disclosure and taxonomies are progress!” They are—“if” they anchor into binding pathways. [The EU Taxonomy](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/sustainable-finance/tools-and-standards/eu-taxonomy-sustainable-activities_en) is a step forward; it must be paired with prohibitions and public investment or it will end up as a labelling exercise.

This is not an argument against post-humanist ethics, Indigenous cosmologies, or multispecies storytelling. Nor is it a plea for technocracy. It is a claim about sequence and interface. Without binding rules, diffuse care becomes performative. Without institutions, symmetrical agency becomes irresponsibility. Without maintenance, innovation becomes entropy. The wager is that EH can keep its ethical breadth while acquiring institutional depth.

If “theory with handles” sounds dull, remember that handles are how we move heavy things. They are not ends in themselves; they allow better ends to be pursued reliably.

EH promised to dislodge a civilisation built on mastering nature by redrawing the boundary between humans and everything else. It has succeeded, often brilliantly, in reopening our sense of who “we” are, and of what worlds we inhabit. But as an operating system for political action, much of EH has flattened the very terrain where change happens. In the name of humility, we have mislaid the handle.

Saito’s monism‑with‑method restores it. Benjamin’s emergency brake names its urgency. Polanyi’s re‑embedding sketches its institutional path. And the mid‑century ordoliberals—our adversaries here—teach a sober lesson: those who write the “rules of the game” rule the future. If EH is to avoid fuelling the crisis it opposes, it must move from “theory about limits” to “theory with handles”—handles that turn in parliaments, ministries, standards bodies, utilities, and courts. Not less theory. Different interfaces. Not fewer stories. More statutes. Not a smaller “we.” A “we” that can pull the brake.
