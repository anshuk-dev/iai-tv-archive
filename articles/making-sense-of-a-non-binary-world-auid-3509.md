# Making sense of a non-binary world

*Humans think in binaries, but reality is more complicated*

**By Kevin Richardson** | *3rd March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,579 words*

![Making sense of a non-binary world](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/non-binary-world.webp)

---

*For centuries we’ve divided the world into tidy pairs: good and evil, male and female, gay and straight. But what if the need to draw such sharp lines tells us more about ourselves than about reality? **Kevin Richardson**, Associate Professor of Philosophy at Duke University, argues that the binary lens through which we view sex and gender — and many other domains — is a conceptual shortcut that distorts more than it clarifies. Rethinking these boundaries, he suggests, may be the first step toward understanding a world that was never meant to be simply divided in two.*

“You can’t just be a *little* gay,” my friend Quinton said, shaking his head. “I don’t have anything against people who are, you know, gay. But let’s be real: you’re either gay or straight.”

This was the kind of conversation you only have with old friends. Quinton and I grew up in the same place: the Southern United States, in the church, surrounded by what we were taught were traditional values. We weren’t taught to hate anyone. But we were taught to believe that the world came neatly divided into two kinds of people. Two genders. Two sexual orientations. Male or female. Gay or straight.

\_\_\_

What’s breaking down isn’t reality, but a way of *constructing* reality.

\_\_\_

For Quinton, those lines in the sand are obvious and natural. Anything in between—bisexuality, pansexuality, non-binary gender—looks like confusion or deception. People, on this view, are really one thing or the other; the rest is just reluctance to admit it.

Our conversation had been sparked by something Quinton found troubling: [Gen Z. Increasingly, young people don’t describe themselves as gay or straight at all.](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/ipsos-pride-survey-2024-gen-zers-most-likely-identify-lgbt#:~:text=This%20significant%20societal%20shift%20looks,followed%20by%20Millennials%20at%2011%25.) They identify as bisexual, pansexual, queer—or they avoid sexual orientation labels entirely. Alongside this shift in sexuality is a parallel shift in how people think about gender. Instead of identifying as male or female, many people now identify as non-binary—often meaning neither male nor female.

“I’m sorry,” Quinton said, “but the kids are confused.”

In my book, *The End of Binaries*, I argue that the kids aren’t confused at all. What’s breaking down isn’t reality, but a way of *constructing* reality—one that has always required enormous effort to maintain.

Binary thinking has a powerful grip on us because it feels simple, stable, and safe. If there are two genders and two sexual orientations, then social life is easier to navigate. We know what to expect. We know how to act. We know how to classify people.

On the traditional view many of us were raised with, sex, gender, and sexuality form a tight package. You’re born with certain physical characteristics—particularly reproductive anatomy—that determine your sex. [Your sex determines your gender.](https://iai.tv/video/the-science-of-sex) Your gender determines your social role. And your sexual orientation is defined by which gender you’re attracted to.

This picture is often presented as a matter of biology, or even divine design. Nature made us this way. God intended it this way. To question the binary, on this view, is to deny reality itself.

\_\_\_

Gender itself is not a pair of boxes into which people must be sorted. It is a multidimensional space structured by bodily traits, social roles, norms, and self-interpretations.

\_\_\_

But this picture assumes something rarely made explicit: that gender and sexuality must be carved into discrete categories, rather than understood as continuous and multidimensional. That assumption is exactly what I challenge.

To see what has gone wrong, it helps to rethink how categories work in the first place. Consider a simple question: *Where do you live?* Depending on the context, you might say your city, your state, your country, or even your continent. None of these answers is false. [They simply locate you at different levels of granularity](https://iai.tv/articles/on-the-ultimate-nature-of-reality-auid-3408). Beneath them all, however, is a more fine-grained reality: your precise location in physical space.

Gender, I argue, works in an importantly similar way. Rather than thinking of gender categories as basic facts about the world, we should think of them as socially recognized regions within an underlying gender space. Gender itself is not a pair of boxes into which people must be sorted. It is a multidimensional space structured by bodily traits, social roles, norms, and self-interpretations.

Each person occupies a particular location in this space. Some people fall close to the center of a socially dominant region like *woman* or *man*. Others fall near the edges. Some occupy positions between regions. Some occupy areas that our existing categories do not capture very well at all.

This way of thinking helps explain why gender norms extend far beyond biology. Gender governs how we dress, how we speak, how we express emotion, how we move through public space. A woman crying is often seen as understandable; a man crying is often seen as weak. A woman being threatened triggers an expectation of protection; a man being threatened is expected to handle it himself. Dresses, makeup, hairstyles, voice pitch, posture—these are not biological facts. Yet they are central to how gender is read and enforced.

On the spatial view, this is not surprising. These traits help locate people within gender space. They are not peripheral to gender; they are part of how gender is socially structured. Seen in this light, non-binary, trans, and gender-nonconforming people are not confused about biology. They are responding, quite appropriately, to the fact that [gender categories are coarse-grained shortcuts imposed on a far more complex underlying reality](https://iai.tv/articles/we-do-not-discover-reality-we-create-it-auid-3475).

This also helps explain why disputes about “what counts” as a woman or a man are so intractable. When categories are treated as sharp and exhaustive, boundary cases become crises. On a spatial view, boundary cases are not anomalies; they are inevitable.

The same framework applies to sexuality. The claim that you “can’t be a little gay” assumes that sexual attraction must be all-or-nothing, fixed across time, and directed exclusively toward one gender category. But this assumption does not match many people’s lived experience.

Some people experience attraction differently at different points in their lives. Some experience attraction that isn’t well captured by “same-sex” or “opposite-sex.” Some are attracted to people rather than to genders. Others find that desire, behavior, fantasy, and identity come apart.

Sexual orientation, on the spatial view, is best understood as a multidimensional sexual space, structured by factors like desire, behavior, emotional attachment, fantasy, and self-identification. Categories like *gay*, *straight*, and *bisexual* mark socially important regions of this space, but they do not exhaust it.

This is why it can make sense, in ordinary language, to say that someone is “mostly straight,” “kind of gay,” or “gay in some contexts but not others.” These ways of speaking are often treated as imprecise or socially awkward. Strictly speaking, however, they may be more accurate than rigid labels.

\_\_\_

When societies double down on binary enforcement, the result is not safety. It’s surveillance.

\_\_\_

[If these changes were merely about personal identity,](https://iai.tv/articles/beam-me-up-scotty-auid-1794) the backlash might be mild. But binary thinking plays a deep structural role in social life. Categories don’t just describe people; they organize institutions, norms, and expectations.

We see this clearly in contemporary politics. In my own country, the government has declared—by policy—that there are two sexes, full stop. This is presented as a simple scientific truth, beyond dispute. At the same time, scientists are increasingly barred from receiving funding if they study non-binary sex or gender at all.

[This is not the calm confidence of settled science](https://iai.tv/articles/scientific-consensus-needs-dissent-auid-2926). It is the anxiety of a worldview under pressure. Much of the fear centers on safety, especially the safety of cisgender women. Critics worry that recognizing non-binary or transgender identities will undermine the norms that protect women from harm. If gender categories blur, who gets access to which spaces? Who is vulnerable? Who is responsible for whom?

These concerns should not be dismissed out of hand. But they rest on a mistaken assumption: that safety depends on sharp categorical boundaries, rather than on social norms of care, accountability, and respect.

When societies double down on binary enforcement, the result is not safety. It’s surveillance. People are scrutinized, policed, and punished for failing to conform. And this doesn’t only affect non-binary or queer people.

Women who don’t look “feminine enough,” men who don’t look “masculine enough,” queer people who don’t fit stereotypes, intersex people whose bodies don’t align with expectations—all become targets. The harder we insist that everyone fit into two boxes, the more people fall outside them.

\_\_\_

Our categories are tools—useful in some contexts, harmful in others—and that they should answer to human lives, not the other way around.

\_\_\_

From the spatial perspective, this makes sense. When we mistake regions for reality itself, anyone near a boundary becomes a problem to be managed. My goal is not to deny that differences exist. Bodies differ. Experiences differ. What I argue against is the idea that these differences must be organized into two exhaustive, mutually exclusive categories.

Gender, in reality, is expansive. Sexuality is multidimensional, context-sensitive, and often graded. Maintaining the binary requires constant work: medical classification, legal enforcement, cultural policing, and moral pressure. When people invoke Nature or God to justify this work, it’s worth asking whose interests are being served.

Change is unsettling. When long-standing norms shift, people worry about losing moral footing. They worry that if the old rules no longer apply, anything goes. But abandoning rigid binaries doesn’t mean abandoning norms altogether. It means recognizing that our categories are tools—useful in some contexts, harmful in others—and that they should answer to human lives, not the other way around.

You can be a little gay. You can be a lot of things that don’t sit comfortably at the extremes. And recognizing that fact doesn’t confuse reality—it helps us see it more clearly.
