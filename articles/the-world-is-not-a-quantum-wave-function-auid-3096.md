*Why reality is non-local and the wave function isn't real*

The world of our immediate experiences, its manifest image, shows us flowing rivers, wandering clouds, and rainbows shining. To understand these phenomena and predict new ones, we have proposed scientific theories. They give us an image of the world, a scientific image, which may differ from the manifest image. For instance, in classical mechanics, everything is modelled as being made up of point-particles. A scientific image is required to explain why we perceive something not as it truly is.

\_\_\_

Classical mechanics was superseded by quantum theory for its great success in making predictions with unprecedented accuracy and precision. However, quantum theory does not provide a clear scientific image.

\_\_\_

Indeed, classical mechanics can explain the river’s motion, cloud formation, the colors of the rainbow, and more in terms of particle motion. Nonetheless, there is the (often unspoken) assumption that one should not accept radical departures of the scientific image from the manifest image unless there are no alternatives, all other things being equal. This, among other things, allows us to keep at bay, views encouraging us to doubt everything we believe about the natural world, such as ‘I live in three-dimensions’. I will argue, one such skeptical view to be kept at bay is what philosophers of physics call ‘wavefunction realism’.

**Quantum Mechanics: A Hazy Scientific Image**

Before launching into the topic of wavefunction realism, it is first helpful to understand the ‘scientific image’ of its parent theory, quantum mechanics. Classical mechanics was superseded by quantum theory for its great success in making predictions with unprecedented accuracy and precision. However, quantum theory does not provide a clear scientific image. In quantum physics particles can be described as being in a ‘superposition’ of states. These particles can, for example, be described as being both in one position and another at the same time. A ‘wave’ function is the mathematical representation of the ‘state’ of some quantum system, it contains the information of the possible positions, spins, momentums etc. of a particle. This conception came after the discovery that particles on the quantum scale exhibit wave-like behavior.

\_\_\_

The probability that a particle will be found in one position or another can be calculated prior to observation from the wavefunction. Referring to ‘observation’ is, however, contentious; why isn’t ‘observing’ considered a clearly-defined physical process?

\_\_\_

To explain why we see a classical-looking world with chairs in just one position *or* another (and prevent having dead-*and*-alive cats), the theory postulates that the act of observation randomly and instantaneously ‘collapses’ the superposition into one of its terms. The probability that a particle will be found in one position or another can be calculated prior to observation from the wavefunction. Referring to ‘observation’ is, however, contentious; why isn’t ‘observing’ considered a clearly-defined physical process?

Luckily, there are other quantum theories immune to this problem. They do share with the ‘standard’ quantum theory, the postulate that any physical object is described by a wavefunction. In the standard theory though, as we have seen, the wavefunction produces macroscopic superpositions (dead-and-alive cats) because there is no mechanism to describe the transition from superpositions of particles to classical-looking ‘things in one place’. Thus, another theory, known as ‘spontaneous collapse’, modifies the wavefunction dynamics so that macroscopic superpositions spontaneously collapse as a matter of law, not observation. Another proposal, the many-worlds theory, instead accepts that all terms in the superpositions exist simultaneously– every possible outcome does occur but in different worlds, each unable to interact with another. Finally, another alternative, the ‘pilot-wave theory’, assumes that the world is made of wave-guided particles, rather than the wavefunction, and are never in superpositions.

**The Wavefunction**

What is the scientific image these theories provide? Setting the pilot-wave theory aside, the many-worlds and spontaneous collapse theories have only the wavefunction in their formalism. This is a field, namely a mathematical object, that assigns to every point a number. Take a topographic map of a region of Central Italy, say. One will observe several concentric closed lines representing altitudes, indicating planes and mountains. This ‘altitude field’ is not a physical object; rather it allows a two-dimensional description (a map) to show a third dimension (the altitude). However, other fields, for instance electromagnetic fields, are more than just effective descriptive tools: light is understood as the radiation produced by the oscillations of the electromagnetic fields.

![Wavefunctions 1 map of italy2](assets/Uploads/Wavefunctions-1-map-of-italy2.jpg)

*A topographic map of Central Italy, representing a 'field' in our analogy.*

Should we think of the wavefunction field like this? According to a view called wavefunction realism the answer is yes: the wavefunction is the matter field, just like how electromagnetic fields are the radiation field. However, the wavefunction is not a field oscillating in three-dimensional space like an electromagnetic field. To account for the strange features of quantum mechanics such as entangled particles, the wavefunction needs to oscillate in the so-called configuration space, a high dimensional space (of dimensions of the order of ). Configuration space is just the space in which a value can be assigned to each possible configuration of some number of particles. In 3-dimensions one particle’s position can, for example, be characterised by three numbers, but for a system of N particles, we would need 3N such numbers to characterise a possible arrangement of particles.

![Wavefunctions 2 configuration space](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzgwMCw1NDhd/Wavefunctions-2-configuration-space.jpg)

*3-N dimensional configuration space. One point in configuration space corresponds to one arrangement of N particles. Diagram adapted from Roger Penrose's 'The Emperor's New Mind'.*

This seems to be a shortcoming of wavefunction realism, as it makes the scientific and manifest image very distant – we are used to our physical objects existing in 3-dimensions, not in a high-dimensional space. This seems especially contentious given that there are alternatives in which the world is still fundamentally three-dimensional, among which is the so-called ‘primitive ontology’ approach [which I defend](https://academic.oup.com/book/32659/chapter-abstract/270604695?redirectedFrom=fulltext). For instance, one can assume that matter is fundamentally composed of particles, like in the pilot-wave theory. Or, in the other theories, that it is described by, say, a three-dimensional matter field. Is there a good reason *not* to endorse these views?

**Wavefunction Realism: Preserving Locality?**

[Alyssa Ney has argued](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-just-a-quantum-wave-function-auid-2024) that wavefunction realism is the only account which makes the two images close in another respect: locality. Locality says an object is only affected directly by what is in its immediate surroundings. That is, interaction between physical objects travels at finite velocity and is never instantaneous. However, it is a fact (theoretically [‘predicted’ by John Stewart Bell](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysicsPhysiqueFizika.1.195) in the 1960s and [experimentally confirmed by Alain Aspect and collaborators](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.49.91) in the 1980s) that the world is nonlocal. That is, an object’s motion can immediately influence an arbitrarily distant object. This is extremely counterintuitive and contrary to what has been assumed in the past, assumptions which have led to successful science.

\_\_\_

In contrast, wavefunction realism aims at avoiding nonlocality. The view is holistic: the whole universe is described by the wavefunction field, and physical objects of our experience are to be suitably found within it.

\_\_\_

We haven’t used the notion of magical ‘spooky action at a distance’ in any of our science before. Even if in classical electromagnetism forces appear to act instantaneously, one can always find a way to avoid this nonlocality by using the notion of fields instead. In electromagnetism, electromagnetic fields mediate the electromagnetic interaction, and changes in the field propagate at the velocity of light. Thus, the effect of electromagnetic fields can be seen as that of a water wave in a pond reaching a floating boat; it takes time for the wave to reach and affect the boat.

![Wavefunctions 3 locality](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzgwMCwzOTZd/Wavefunctions-3-locality.jpg)

*Locality: Only when the ripples from the pebble reach the boat do we see the boat rock. Similarly, changes in the electromagnetic field can only propagate at a finite speed, the speed of light.*

Moreover, physicists have always assumed that one can cut up the world into suitably small regions and study them independently. When physicists perform an experiment in the lab, they control the relevant parameters in the lab, ignoring the outside. Also, nonlocality is in direct contrast with the theory of special relativity that sets the velocity of light as the maximum velocity anything can travel at.

The ‘three-dimensional’ views mentioned above essentially embrace nonlocality. In fact, in these views, particularly the primitive ontology approach, the wavefunction is not a matter field. Rather, at the fundamental level matter is understood in terms of three-dimensional entities which compose the Lego-style objects we experience in the manifest image. This view is particularly suited to describing the pilot-wave theory, as it already completes the quantum mechanical description with (three-dimensional) particles. Nonetheless, one can extend it to spontaneous localization theories and many-worlds, by specifying which three-dimensional entities one should think matter is made of (three-dimensional matter fields, or spatiotemporal events, or the like). In any case, in all these theories the wavefunction describes the interaction between (three-dimensional) physical bodies. It should be seen as the field mediating interaction, just like electromagnetic fields. However, electromagnetic fields propagate in three dimensions, while the wavefunction does not. Because it is defined in configuration space, it connects arbitrarily distant bodies instantaneously. That is, the interaction field the wavefunction represents, is nonlocal.

In contrast, wavefunction realism aims at avoiding nonlocality. The view is holistic: the whole universe is described by the wavefunction field, and physical objects of our experience are to be suitably found within it. In other words, physical objects do not individually exist at the fundamental ‘higher dimensional’ level: their mutual independence and interaction suitably emerges non-fundamentally in three-dimensions. That is, while in the three-dimensional views one constructs macroscopic entities from the microscopic fundamental entities, in wavefunction realism there is another step: first, non-fundamental microscopic three-dimensional objects, non-compositionally, emerge from the wavefunction, then they construct macroscopic objects. In any case, importantly, since interaction among objects happens in the derivative three-dimensional world, locality is restored in the more fundamental, high-dimensional world.

![Wavefunctions 4 emergence](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzgwMCw0MDVd/Wavefunctions-4-emergence.jpg)

*The emergence of the macroscopic world according to Wavefunction Realists.*

So, the dilemma is the following: either we accept the ‘three-dimensional’ views which are, however, nonlocal; or we go for wavefunction realism, which is local but in which space is not three-dimensional. Independently of the choice, the manifest and the scientific image are considerably distant.

\_\_\_

I think that the problem with wavefunction realism is that the intuitive notion of locality is not the notion which wavefunction realism preserves.

\_\_\_

The two views have different motivations. As discussed, wavefunction realism is motivated by keeping locality, because that is part of the manifest image. Instead, the ‘three-dimensional’ views favor keeping the number of dimensions of the two images the same. This is part of the manifest image as well, because it allows us to think of macroscopic objects as composed by microscopic fundamental entities, Lego bricks style. Can one justify keeping one aspect of the manifest image over the other?

**Choosing a Manifest Image: Wavefunction Realism's Mysterious Locality**

I think that the problem with wavefunction realism is that the intuitive notion of locality is not the notion which wavefunction realism preserves. The intuitive concept of locality has to do with the interaction between three-dimensional objects. In fact, locality is the idea that there is no instantaneous action at arbitrary distance. We do not see these effects, and it is mysterious how these effects would be brought about. We think of events as having causes which are nearby because we think that the cause-effect interaction travels at finite velocity: it takes time for some object to ‘feel’ another object’s presence. As already mentioned, physical systems can be successfully studied in isolation from their environment, which would not be possible if an arbitrarily distant object could instantaneously influence the object under study.

\_\_\_

The real quantum revolution is that we were all wrong: interaction can be nonlocal.

\_\_\_

Electromagnetism is local: electromagnetic fields travel at the velocity of light between one charged object and another, acting as mediators of the electromagnetic interaction. Locality was a crucial ingredient in Einstein’s development of his theories of relativity, and in his rejection of quantum mechanics. On the one hand, special relativity sets a velocity which nothing can surpass. And on the other hand, general relativity eliminates the nonlocality of Newton's gravitational force by suitably introducing the spacetime curvature. Moreover, [Einstein’s paper with Podolsky and Rosen](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.47.777) famously argued that quantum theory had to be wrong because the wavefunction collapse is nonlocal. Instead, the real quantum revolution is that we were all wrong: interaction can be nonlocal.

Wavefunction realism restores locality in configuration space, ‘high-dimensional locality.’ In what sense is high-dimensional locality intuitive? It is not the intuitive concept which physicists were trying to preserve, which has to do with the properties of interaction among physical objects. Indeed, it is unclear how to connect it to the notion of interaction at all, since in configuration space there is only one thing, the wavefunction. That means, at least, that we need to change our idea about what ‘interacting’ amounts to.

**Final Thoughts**

Summing all up, the primitive ontology approach and the other ‘three dimensional’ views give up on locality. This is not ideal, but at least we preserve compositionality. Instead, wavefunction realism wishes to ‘save’ locality by doing away with three-dimensionality and therefore (fundamental) compositionality. However, to justify preserving high-dimensional locality they end up revising the notion of interaction. Thus, wavefunction realism ends up looking like a skeptical scenario: we are deceived about the dimensions of the space we live it, we are deceived about the nature of objects, and we are deceived about the notion of interaction.  And like all skeptical scenarios, why should we believe it to faithfully represent reality, if there are non-skeptical alternatives? That is, based on the manifest image we believe that there are fundamentally individual objects moving and changing and interacting locally in a fundamentally three-dimensional space, but we are wrong.

Why should we accept a theory according to which we are being deceived about *everything* when there is a view such as the primitive ontology approach according to which *most* of what we think, given the manifest image, is actually true? In this approach, it is *really* the case that there are fundamentally individual objects moving and changing in a fundamentally three-dimensional space. What we need to figure out is what it means to say that there is nonlocal interaction. But wavefunction realism needs to figure it out as well, together with all the rest.
