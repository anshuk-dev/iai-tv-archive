# Life defies the second law of thermodynamics

*Order out of chaos*

**By Dorothea Olkowski** | *1st July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,830 words*

![Life defies the second law of thermodynamics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/life-defies-the-second-law-of-thermodynamics.webp)

---

*The second law of thermodynamics says disorder always increases. But life looks like an exception, since living things are ordered and create further order. Physicists usually explain this away: order here is balanced by extra disorder elsewhere. Philosopher **Dorothea Olkowski** doesn't dispute the law itself. Drawing on Ilya Prigogine's work, she argues that order is not a debt repaid elsewhere, but the natural result of open systems exchanging energy with their surroundings. The real question, she suggests, is not whether the second law is wrong, but whether its classical, closed-system version ever described the reality of life at all. Through natural selection, life's order overcomes reality's chaos.*

**Language, Logic, and the Limits of Formalization**

I would argue that I have been working with the idea of complex systems since grad school, although I certainly would not have been aware of that term nor of the multitude of scientists, philosophers, and others who engaged with what became complexity theory. My first introduction to the term “science of complexity” came from the work of the mathematician John L. Casti in his book *Complexification: Explaining a Paradoxical World Through the Science of Surprise*. With Casti, we encounter the concepts of catastrophe, chaos, lawlessness, irreducibility, emergence, and surprise, but he begins with the simple question of whether our language accurately reflects reality.

When I say “desk,” I could be referring to the flat surface in the kitchen where I occasionally work, and when I say “counter,” I could be referring to an ad hoc table sitting in a corner or the app on my phone that keeps track of the number of steps I walk each day. The logical structure between language and the world is never as clear as we assume—and it’s not only ordinary language that suffers from this. If we look at the mathematical proof for 1 + 1 = 2 in [Bertrand Russell](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russell/) and Alfred North Whitehead’s *[Principia Mathematica](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/principia-mathematica/)*, we are always surprised that it takes up over 360 pages. And the light shining on that work dimmed when [Kurt Gödel’s incompleteness theorem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/goedel-incompleteness/) revealed that mathematical proofs cannot be both consistent and complete. These discrepancies become even more problematic when we are trying to make predictions about the future based on past states of the world—since this is the business of science by means of rule-following leading to formalization.

**Dynamical Systems and Strange Attractors**

One such set of rules is that of a dynamical system. If you’ve ever played “Treasure Hunt,” you know the rules of this game. Beginning from an “initial state,” players start at different locations and move from location to location on a trajectory guided by instructions or clues given at each location. The end point, where the winner finds a prize, is called an attractor and it governs the motion of the entire game. Fixed point attractors are definite domains of attraction, such as the point where the winner finds the prize. If the game’s creator were a bit devious, they might set up another attractor—a limit cycle—and send one or all the players in a fruitless round of movement retracing their steps over and over.

Outside of this, something else can occur. The devious game maker can give one player a particular initial condition whose rules are iterated at each point, but each repetition cannot be carried out precisely as directed. This creates a trajectory that sends the player on a route that amounts to a tangled mess in which that poor player passes by all the others over and over, endlessly cycling through the game space without ever repeating the exact same trajectory—confused and lost, totally unstable. They have been dealt a strange attractor, and so they go on and on with no end in sight. They endlessly follow the rules from point to point, but never reach the prize. This sensitivity to initial conditions and the existence of strange attractors occurs all the time, as the rule and not the exception.

But has our poor player really been abandoned to wander randomly, or could something else have occurred? Suppose our player had a fixed trajectory but noticed that other players are following very different kinds of stable trajectories leading them through what appear to be nicer parts of the park. The player may abandon their own path and follow another player. But even then, there could be a problem if the player cannot decide between the two different but equally enticing trajectories of two different players. Stretched between them, so to speak, the player becomes unstable until undergoing a catastrophic “falling out” into a new stable manifold—most likely by leaving the game and going home.

These simplifications of complex mathematical ideas nonetheless give us insight into how easily processes become chaotic or unstable. Minimally, chaos is the study of complex nonlinear dynamic systems. But if our subject is complexity, then why spend time explaining chaos?

The non-linear systems we have been discussing are linguistic and mathematical systems that fascinate insofar as their input and output are incommensurate; they don’t match up. Nevertheless, it has been frequently noted that many natural systems that are thermodynamically open to new matter and energy are nonequilibrium systems, and their evolutionary processes of emergence and development are nonlinear. A whirlpool fed by a constant stream of water in a bathtub or at the bottom of a natural falls reveals this process very clearly. This brings us out of the linguistic and mathematical and a bit more in the direction of nature. It also opens the way to [complexity](https://iai.tv/articles/nancy-cartwright-physics-cant-deal-with-reality-complexity-auid-2269).

**Order Out of Chaos**

A great deal of amplification of these questions came from the Nobel laureate [Ilya Prigogine](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1977/prigogine/biographical/). In *[Order Out of Chaos](https://www.versobooks.com/en-gb/products/390-order-out-of-chaos)*, co-written with philosopher of science Isabelle Stengers, they argue that if “in the classical view, the basic processes of nature were considered to be deterministic and reversible … today we see everywhere the role of irreversible processes, of fluctuations.” The classical model does not take into account sensitivity to initial conditions—the reality that even two very proximate initial conditions will follow very different trajectories.

Prigogine and Stengers reformulate the world as open, complex, probabilistic, and temporally irreversible, particularly as it applies to the macroscopic scale of atoms, molecules, and biomolecules. New, dynamic states of matter may emerge from thermal chaos when a system interacts with its surroundings—for example, the Great Red Spot, a vortex on planet Jupiter which has continued for centuries. These new structures were given the name dissipative structures to indicate that dissipation can in fact play a constructive role in the formation of new states.

They argue that the equilibrium structures of thermodynamics fail to account for various structures encountered in nature. Once formed, equilibrium structures may be isolated and maintained without interaction with the environment. By contrast, biological cells or cities are open systems feeding on the matter and energy of the world, without which they would die off. Far-from-equilibrium systems are instances of the threshold where fluctuations lead to unstable behavior.

The Roman poet and philosopher [Lucretius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lucretius/) (99–55 BCE) referred to this as the clinamen, a spontaneous, unpredictable deviation that describes turbulence—irregular on the macroscopic scale, but highly organized microscopically. For a long time, turbulence was dismissed as noise, but it was eventually recognized as macroscopically chaotic yet microscopically coherent and highly organized. This transition—from what appears to be merely chaotic to highly organized, coherent behavior—is now viewed as self-organization, and is evidence for what computer scientist [Christopher Langton](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/016727898690237X) called “complexity at the edge of chaos.” When a system interacts with the outside world, exchanging energy and matter, these nonequilibrium conditions may be the setting for dissipative structures that become sources of order—that is, order out of chaos.

As Prigogine and Stengers point out, this process has become apparent in molecular biology. “Auto-catalysis”—in which a chemical reaction produces an enzyme whose presence then encourages further production of the same enzyme—is prevalent in a small number of basic biomolecules and helps to explain how lumps of DNA develop into complex living organisms. Molecular biology explains the transmission and exploitation of genetic information as a feedback loop, a nonlinear process. This leads to the question of whether there are objective ways to characterize complexity.

Casti contrasts complex systems with simple systems: simple systems have predictable behaviors, few components, few feedback loops, centralized decision-making, and weak interactions among components. By contrast, complex systems display unpredictable and surprising acausal behavior, in part due to the multiplicity of variables and feedback loops, the diffusion of authority, and irreplaceable components. Sensitivity to initial conditions implies that minor uncertainties, errors, or disturbances can amplify into large subsequent uncertainties with respect to location and trajectory.

Symmetry breaking offers a homely illustration of the same principle. Simply heating water in a pan on a stove breaks the random profile of molecules vertically, as bottom layers heat up and pass the heat upward to the cooler layers—breaking the original symmetry of the liquid through conduction. Increase the heat to a rolling boil and this form breaks further still, through convection. In each such symmetry breaking, the order and complexity of the system increases. Complex systems also bifurcate due to structural instability—the famous case of the [collapse of three balconies in a Kansas City hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyatt_Regency_walkway_collapse) illustrates this on a darker register, where a small engineering change in the placement of nuts tripled the load on one support, causing it to split open and the balconies to collapse. Bifurcation is the condition for self-organization as the system moves to a form that is either ordered and/or more complex.

**The Source of Order**

Where does all this order come from? Among the scientists and philosophers who have asked this question, one who interests me greatly is [Stuart Kauffman](https://iai.tv/articles/biology-not-physics-holds-the-key-to-reality-auid-3163). Trained as a medical doctor, Kauffman became a theoretical biologist and an early member of the [Santa Fe Institute’s complexity project](https://www.santafe.edu/research/overview). For Kauffman, the answer begins with Darwin and the concept of natural selection—but for Darwin, natural selection was largely accidental, whereas for Kauffman, “laws” of complexity themselves generate spontaneous order, after which natural selection refines and moulds.

The second law of thermodynamics proposes that the universe is moving in the direction of disorder driven by entropy, yet as Kauffman argues, we are surrounded by “extraordinary surges towards order.”

As he writes, “[t]he biosphere around us sustains us, is created by us, grafts the energy flux from the sun into the great web of biochemical, biological, geologic, economic and political exchanges that envelopes the world.” Kauffman grounds this in two basic conditions: first, the reality that any small change in a chaotic system can and typically does have large amplifying effects; and second, quantum mechanics, which ensures the fundamental indeterminism of particles at the subatomic level.

With regard to the question of the origin and nature of life, both Prigogine and Stengers and Kauffman cite the philosopher who has most influenced my work in this area: the Polish-French philosopher [Henri Bergson](https://iai.tv/articles/time-is-not-made-of-distinct-present-moments-auid-2972) (1859–1941). For many years the scientific community overlooked or outright condemned Bergson under the influence of criticisms levied against him by Bertrand Russell, who characterized Bergson’s philosophy as a dualist opposition between life and matter, derived from the distinction Bergson drew between the tendencies of instinct or intuition and those of intellect. Bergson insisted that each of these tendencies includes some aspect of the other, so that they are not at all opposites. However, since for Bergson time as duration and change arises with instinct or intuition, intuition does not separate the world into distinct entities, while space as intellect does precisely this.

His target was always determinism. His version of novelty requires heterogeneity—“multiplicity without divisibility and succession without separation.” Prigogine and Stengers cite Bergson’s critique of scientific rationality precisely for its reduction of time and change to a sequence of instantaneous states linked by deterministic laws. For Bergson, time is invention and nature is change, the continued elaboration of the new, open to development without a preestablished model.

Kauffman argues that life is a natural property of complex chemical systems—a position Bergson also maintained—that is, that dead molecules organize if each molecule’s formation is catalysed by some other. Thus, when the number of different kinds of molecules in a chemical soup passes a certain threshold, an autocatalytic metabolism—a self-sustaining network of reactions—will appear, and life emerges complex and whole. What is remarkable here is the claim that this is not a Watson-Crick pairing, not a double helix, but something deeper, something simpler, something based in chemistry, and originating with “dead molecules.”

This was, in fact, [Hans Driesch’s](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/theories-biological-development/) conclusion decades earlier, by a different route. A brilliant experimentalist, Driesch separated a frog embryo into two and discovered that each half developed into a fully normal frog. Rather than falling back on the already discredited theory of homunculi—the idea that preformed entities are nested inside the egg—Driesch surmised that there must be information making this possible in each cell, and that the order in question is emergent.

For Bergson, evolution does not follow a single course but fragments like a shell bursting from a cannon, and each fragment bursts again and again. Life meets resistance from inert matter, but as he writes, it succeeds “by dint of humility, by making itself very small and very insinuating, bending to physical and chemical forces, consenting even to go a part of the way with them.”

The extent to which Bergson’s speculations about the origin of life prove to be precisely those Kauffman is discovering may be debated, but the fact remains that, as Bergson insists, the evolution of life is a tendency creating divergent directions in time, heterogeneous and surprising, adapting itself to the accidents of the ground and not the realization of a plan.

**Complexity Itself**

Kauffman argues that “the reason complex systems exist on, or in the ordered regime near the edge of chaos, is because evolution takes them there.” Natural selection tunes their parameters until they arrive at the region between order and chaos where complex behavior lives. In terms strangely reflective of Bergson’s, Kauffman states that if life arose as collectively autocatalytic sets, then the first life was already exploding and struggling to control the explosion. This “supercriticality” continues until limited by factors such as food supply and energy, but also by the fact that we metabolize our food rather than simply merge with it.

Kauffman cites several limitations to selection, even with the assistance of mutation. Living things may mutate too slowly and remain trapped at a certain optimum, or they may mutate too fast and fail due to increasing poor fitness—an “error catastrophe” not unlike the fate of our lost player in the treasure hunt described earlier. Hence, self-organization is the essential pre-condition of evolution, not its replacement. For life, Kauffman asserts, natural selection has always sifted for useful variations, but what makes order possible in a far from equilibrium universe is that life itself—self-organization—is a natural expression of tendencies toward order.

Let us conclude by taking this into an area of investigation that is extremely relevant to my work: the physiology of perception. Much of my philosophical research is grounded in Bergson’s analysis of affectivity and cognition, as well as the phenomenology of perception of [Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/), who spent several years working in a psychology research lab to ground his ideas.

In the interests of moving forward, I want to briefly give a more recent neurobiological account, that of Walter J. Freeman. Freeman concludes, based on 30 years of research, that perception cannot be understood as the activity of individual neurons—the common approach in neuroscience. Rather, perception requires “the simultaneous, cooperative activity of millions of neurons spread throughout expanses of the cortex.” Like Bergson, who insists on the unbroken heterogeneity of perceptual images, Freeman states that to grasp choral music, one must hear the performers modulate together in response to one another. This, he argues, is all part of the chaos-complex behavior of vast collections of neurons that shift abruptly and simultaneously from one complex activity pattern to another in response to the smallest inputs. Bergson refers to this as ontological memory—the manner in which multiple dimensions of memory enter any present perception and constitute it, eventually making it possible for the individual to formulate new ideas and thoughts.

For Freeman as well, the chaotic behavior of collections of neurons is what makes it possible to respond to perception at all, to generate novel activity, and—significantly—to experience fresh ideas. Other researchers, neuropsychologist [Mark Solms](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/london/big-ideas/speakers/2026) and neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp in particular, focus on the limbic system as the site of consciousness and the source of information passed on to the memory system and the cortex.

To discuss all of this in detail would require another paper, if not a book, but let me simply conclude by saying that for neuroscience as for theoretical biology and philosophy, complexity is now.

It is what we must think about when we think about life, when we think about creative activity, and when we create concepts.
