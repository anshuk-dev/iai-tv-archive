# Morality is about practicality, not principles

*We should apply the scientific method to morality*

**By James Lizska** | *17th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,276 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Morality is about practicality, not principles](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/moral-2.webp)

---

*For hundreds of years, we've been told that science and ethics are fundamentally separate - that the scientific method cannot tells us how we ought to live and that morality has no bearing on scientific facts. But New York philosopher **James Liszka**, leading authority on Charles Peirce's ethics, argues this is a mistake. Unearthing the work of America's greatest forgotten polymath, he argues that a deeper understanding of the scientific method reveals how a pragmatic, experimentalist approach can resolve our ethical problems too.*

After many years of work on the logic of science, Charles Sanders Peirce realized that it needed an ethical grounding. The founder of pragmatism wrote a letter in 1902 to William James, his longtime friend and compatriot, confessing how “stupid” he was in failing to recognize this. In his last, unpublished work on logic, he wrote that he had “spared no elbow-grease” in polishing its “lens” with the ingredient of ethics, “showing much that was not discernible before.” What Peirce saw was a rather novel approach to ethics. Instead of relying on ethical *principles*, Peirce argues for a *process* by which ethical problems can be sorted out. Scientific inquiry itself can serve as the model for that process. But to understand this, we first have to understand his work on the logic of science.

**Pragmatism and the scientific method**

Peirce certainly had scientific street credentials. He did his graduate work in experimental chemistry at the innovative Lawrence Scientific School at Harvard. He was well-trained in mathematics, his father being the greatest American mathematician of the time. After graduation, he worked at the Harvard Observatory, making precise measurements of the brightness of stars in order to better map the Milky Way. This was complemented by his work with the US Geodetic Survey to measure the shape of the Earth more precisely.  But he claimed he had been a student of logic from the age of 12.

 In 1877, Peirce presented his logic of science in a series of articles for *The Popular Science Monthly*, entitled “[Illustrations on the Logic of Science.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_12/November_1877/Illustrations_of_the_Logic_of_Science_I)” They formed the basis of a philosophy that came to be known as [*pragmatism*](https://iai.tv/articles/east-of-eden-free-will-and-rewriting-the-myth-of-your-life-auid-3711).

Peirce argued that scientific method, broadly understood, provides epistemic reliability. It is the best way to assuage doubt, settle opinion and fix beliefs. And, ultimately, he came to realize that the same method could settle our ethical beliefs too. This is due to three features of scientific practice.

\_\_\_

Science is reliable because it is a very sophisticated version of trial and error.

\_\_\_

First, experimental science rests implicitly on metaphysical realism. Regardless of what inquirers believe, there exists an order to things independent of those beliefs. When those beliefs are tested in the right way, the order of the world will decide whether those beliefs are in error or not.

Second, experimental design is a very good model for clarifying our concepts and beliefs, all the better to test them. By understanding what something *does* under certain conditions—how it *functions*—that provides a more specific way of understanding what it *means*. Yes, a concept such as ‘hardness’ could be subjectively assessed through touch.  But by using the Mohs test, for example, hardness can be objectively measured. The test is to settle which minerals scratch or are scratched by others, defining their rank order of hardness. Proceeding in this way, we would discover that diamonds are the hardest mineral, even if we thought iron was. This idea became the basis of what is called the *pragmatic maxim*.

But there is an even more important consequence of the pragmatic maxim. Because we understand what things are by what they do, this functional understanding can serve our purposes. A *theoretical* hypothesis is essentially a belief, e.g., that diamonds are harder than glass. And the functional meaning of a belief is the conduct it entails. Believing this hypothesis (that diamonds are harder than glass) means believing that pressing a diamond against glass will result in scratched glass. But that can be translated into a *practical* hypothesis: If scratching glass is your end (with the ultimate purpose of cutting it), then use a diamond. Thus, experimental science is ready-made for functional use.

Thirdly, science is reliable because it is a very sophisticated version of trial and error, with well-designed trials and very good methods for detecting error. Induction, in particular, is adept at sorting out whether the experimental hypothesis is in error and should be rejected or not.

This idea rests on the [Law of Large Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers), an intuitive, well-established theorem at the foundation of statistical theory, first proven by Jacob Bernoulli. Simply stated, it claims that as the number of samples in a population increases, the mean of those samples will converge to the true proportion of a targeted variable in the long run. James Surowiecki popularized this idea as [The Wisdom of Crowds.](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/175380/the-wisdom-of-crowds-by-james-surowiecki/)

The Law of Large Numbers became the mathematical basis of Peirce’s *convergence theory of truth*: truth is what results from sufficient inquiry over time. It fit nicely with Darwin’s theory of evolution that shook the scientific world in Peirce’s time. The evolutionary process, combined with the method for detecting error, led to the idea that science would correct its errors over time, eventually converging to what was true. Paradoxically, the way to truth is through error. “While the conclusion (of reasoning) may be more or less erroneous, yet the same procedure diligently persisted in must… lead to a result indefinitely approximating to the truth.”

**The problem of ethics**

However, some thirty years later, Peirce came to realize through the ethics-polished lens of logic that his pragmatism harbored an ethical flaw. He had a chance to acknowledge this problem and present his remedy in a series of lectures at Harvard in 1903.  Peirce realized that the pragmatic maxim is what Immanuel Kant called a “hypothetical” or “pragmatic” imperative. Such imperatives are prudential. They merely tell people what they ought to do relative to their purpose: If you want to be healthy, then you must eat a balanced diet. Kant pointedly expressed the ethical problem with pragmatic imperatives: “The precepts to be followed by a physician in order to cure his patient and by a poisoner in order to bring about certain death are of equal value in so far as each does that which will perfectly accomplish his purpose.” “Whether the end is reasonable and good is not in question at all, for the question is only of what must be done in order to attain it.”

This has implications for science. By showing how theoretical hypotheses can be converted to practical ones, the pragmatic maxim reveals how science can easily be made instrumental for any purpose, good or ill. One thinks easily of a number of examples. We are occupied today by the promise and the peril of AI. Science could find a cure for the dreaded polio in the 1950s, but not long after it unleashed the dreadful power of the atomic bomb.

\_\_\_

Kant’s proof of the categorical imperative was questionable for Peirce, who believed in proof through practical experiments.

\_\_\_

So how was the ethical problem of pragmatism to be fixed?

Peirce rejected the solutions offered by the two dominant ethical theories of his time—utilitarianism and the duty-based ethics of Immanuel Kant.  Utilitarians argued that the ethical measure of any action was whether its consequences created the greatest happiness for the greatest number. That could ethically constrain any hypothetical imperative. In contrast, Kant had argued that there were certain categorical imperatives, universal duties, such as promise-keeping, truth-telling, and not using others as means to one’s own ends, that trumped hypothetical ones.

But Peirce had his doubts about these principles. He didn’t think that all ethical decisions should center around people’s happiness, which the utilitarians had not clearly defined and, in many cases, reduces to a “vulgar hedonism.” And Kant’s proof of the categorical imperative was questionable for Peirce, who believed in proof through practical experiments; it is hard to believe that a universal ethical principle could be proved for all time by “a mere formalism.” To prove that truth-telling is a universal principle, Kant argued, one only has to assume lying is universal, and show that this leads to an absurdity. Obviously, if we assume that everyone lies, then no one would believe the lies, which foils the purpose of lying. But Peirce, the mathematician, knew that indirect proofs—showing something true by proving its contradictory false—are suspect.

**How the scientific method can resolve our ethical problems**

Peirce struck out a new direction. He thought that, even though the instrumental use of science was the problem, science might still be the cure. What was lacking in scientific practice was an account of the sorts of ends that ought to be pursued and the means of doing so. What if communities adopted the scientific method, as characterized by the three features outlined earlier, as the basis for inquiries into the search for good ends and righteous means? What if inquirers into ethics (i) assumed that there was real order in the world, such that their erroneous beliefs would tend to be corrected, (ii) clarified their ethical concepts into what they mean *in practice*, and (iii) proceeded to test these concepts through trial and error? The result would be, in effect, *normative* sciences. Once the proper ends have been identified and constraints on the means for attaining them, then, given those constraints, the empirical sciences could then be properly employed to figure out the likely ways to attain those ends.

But Peirce had another insight. He argued that inquiry has norms that promote “reasonableness.” For Peirce, being reasonable is a willingness to correct one’s erroneous beliefs. In inquiry, one has to justify one’s beliefs to others. One has to be open to criticism. The views of others have to be included. Reasonable people will recognize that others in their community also pursue ends, some of which will conflict with theirs. The goal is to correct each other’s errors, avoid inconsistencies, and resolve anomalies in order to establish the better aims and the means to attain them. Collectively, these norms of reasonableness promote what [Cheryl Misak](https://iai.tv/video/pragmatism-and-the-pursuit-of-truth) calls an “[epistemological democracy](https://www.routledge.com/Truth-Politics-Morality-Pragmatism-and-Deliberation/Misak/p/book/9780415140362).”

\_\_\_

What the social experiment, like any good experiment, demonstrated over time was that these reasons against were fallacious and the predictions in error.

\_\_\_

Peirce’s sense of reasonableness is similar to T.M. Scanlon’s notion of the same name. In *[What We Owe to Each Other](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674004238)*, he makes an important distinction between rationality and reasonableness. To act rationally in the classic sense is to do what will likely attain your ends. It is rationality that can lead to ethical trouble. It is merely a world of hypothetical imperatives. Reasonableness is a different matter. People act *reasonably* when they consider how their actions will affect others and so accept the demand from others to provide reasons for their actions—all the while accepting that others might reasonably reject those reasons. This very fundamental feature of reasonableness is perhaps why Peirce argues that “the only ultimate good” is “to further the development of concrete reasonableness.”  Without it, inquiry would be unlikely, if not impossible.

Peirce left much of his work on ethics and the normative sciences unfinished, leaving lingering questions. Kant might ask: Is the call to inquiry a call to duty—a categorical imperative—or is it just another hypothetical one? If the latter, why should we inquire?

I think Peirce would say *that* question is itself a matter of inquiry. However, *whether the inquiry matters* is another matter. Given any inquiry, there are certain commitments, certain duties that fall out of the very nature of reasonableness. Inquirers exchange claims with the aim of making co-inquirers “believe what is asserted, to which end a reason for believing it must be furnished.  To assert a proposition is to make oneself responsible for it,” as Peirce writes. However, it is not so much that these commitments are proven formally, but practically in that they come to be recognized as essential for good inquiry.

But what makes an inquiry matter? This is tied to the importance of the ends at stake. When these conflict, a problem arises that demands a resolution, and inquiry is called upon for “the settlement of opinion.” The more problematic, the more intense the conflict, the more inquiry matters.

Consider a historical example of a well-known social problem. Women’s suffrage was a contentious issue in America for some 60 years. In the public discourse, reasons were given for and against. Among the latter, that women were too emotional and irrational to vote on important matters; it was also predicted that their involvement in public life would destroy families. Their place was in the home. This conflict became intense enough at the turn of the twentieth century to garner enough legislative support to attempt a social experiment known as the Nineteenth Amendment, granting women the right to vote.  What the social experiment, like any good experiment, demonstrated over time was that these reasons against were fallacious and the predictions in error.  The words of Angela Grimké, abolitionist and suffragist, were prophetic: “The very truth you are now contending for, will, in fifty years, be so completely embedded in public opinion that no one need say one word in their defense.” The lived experience of the experiment, its real effects, changed the normative structure of generations to come, so much so that it’s hard to imagine that any democratic community would revert to the disenfranchisement of women.

Rather than an abstract exercise in reasoning, normative inquiry would be [an “evolutionary” and “synthetical process,” engaged with the norms of the time](https://iai.tv/articles/russell-vs-american-philosophers-and-the-attack-on-truth-auid-3165). Just as science progresses through the correction of its errors, so would normative inquiries seek improvement through the solution of problems caused by the errors, inconsistencies and anomalies in the norms that make up our lived experience.
