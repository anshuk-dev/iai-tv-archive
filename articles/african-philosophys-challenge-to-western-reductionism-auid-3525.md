# African philosophy's challenge to Western reductionism

*In philosophies like Ubuntu, the whole is more real than its parts*

**By Elvis Imafidon** | *18th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,837 words*

![African philosophy's challenge to Western reductionism](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/african-philosophys-challenge-to-western-reductionism.webp)

---

*Western thought often explains systems—from societies to ecosystems—by breaking them down into their parts: atoms, rocks, trees, individual people. But many African philosophies like Ubuntu challenge the idea of pre-defined, isolated entities forming larger wholes. As philosopher **Elvis Imafidon** argues, relationships don’t connect pre-existing individuals, rather all things become what they are through their relations with other things. From this relational understanding of being, a dominant theme in African philosophy develops a distinctive community-centred ethics, which places equal value on both human and non-human aspects of reality.*

One question that has kept philosophy thriving for thousands of years is the existential question of the human: What makes human existence meaningful? This is in some ways different from the question of origins: [What makes human existence possible](https://iai.tv/video/the-origin-of-life)? While the latter is a shared concern across disciplines—from anthropology and biology to philosophy—the former, about the [meaningfulness of human existence](https://iai.tv/video/creating-the-meaning-of-life-babette-babich), is often primarily within the radar of philosophy and sibling disciplines such as religion.

But these questions also have shared boundaries, a common metaphysical concern. The answer we give to the question of the origin of human existence significantly impacts our understanding of the meaningfulness of human existence. Whether we understand meaning to arise entirely from within this world, or from some transcendent, other-worldly source, will be influenced by whether we believe that life originates solely from material substances or from something transcendent and spiritual. The interwovenness of these two questions will continue to unfold during our discussion in this short essay, even though our focus is on the question of the meaningfulness of human existence.

\_\_\_

The implications of existentialism for the meaningfulness of existence, which reflects much of our thinking today on the matter, are that meaningful existence is both anthropocentric (centred on the human) and subjective (defined by the self).

\_\_\_

It is easy to decipher how the experience of two world wars in the first half of the twentieth century, during which human experience must have sometimes seemed meaningless, urged philosophers to think more concretely about meaning. What may not be readily decipherable is that the [existentialist movement](https://iai.tv/articles/can-we-reinvent-ourselves-auid-1133) that arose in this period was also a radical shift from a transcendent, other-worldly understanding of the meaningfulness of human existence to a this-worldly perspective. In this shift, the idea that the essence and meaningfulness of a human’s existence transcends or precedes their this-worldly existence is rejected and replaced with the idea that the human being exists first and only in this world, and then gradually forges out and curates their own essence and meaning. This is why, [for Friedrich Nietzsche](https://iai.tv/articles/can-we-reinvent-ourselves-auid-1133), “God” or the dominant religious and moral tradition of Europe had to die first for the *Übermensch* (super-human) with the will to create their own meaning and morality to emerge. And it explains why in the existential ontology of [Martin Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/), any understanding of being and existence has to begin from and centre the human subject, *Dasein.*

Thus, at the heart of existentialism is the idea, popular today, that I am an independent entity creating my own meaning through authentic, self-guided living. My existence and meaning are not imposed on me by some transcendent forces and ought not to be dictated by others; rather, I exert my autonomy, freedom, courage, agency and rationality to lead a meaningful life. The implications of existentialism for the meaningfulness of existence, which reflects much of our thinking today on the matter, are that meaningful existence is both [anthropocentric](https://iai.tv/articles/humans-arent-special-and-why-it-matters-auid-3242) (centred on the human) and subjective (defined by [the self](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-beyond-brutality-auid-1575)). To what extent does this capture our very way of being, existing, and meaning-making? Are our individual qualities of rationality, autonomy and freedom sufficient to guarantee meaningful existence? What is the place of other human beings and of beings other than the human in the pursuit of meaning?

My suspicion that these questions are not particularly attended to within the anthropocentric and subjective perspective has led to my exploration of the relational metaphysical perspective that emerges from my heritage, the sub-Saharan African tradition of philosophy. As I argue in my new book*,* [*Doing African Philosophy*,](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/doing-african-philosophy-9781350464230/) this tradition has been preserved for generations in both textual and non-textual (oral and symbolic) resources. In this tradition, there is a sustained understanding in languages, proverbs, riddles, adages and other enigmatic sayings, symbols, and conventional and non-conventional forms of writing that reality is composed of several entities, visible and invisible, that share, partake in, and are collectively responsible for a common essence—an aura, energy or force. A meaningful life for an entity in this ecosystem of entities is essentially dependent not solely on self-contained qualities such as rationality and instinct, but on sustained relationships with other community members and on a collective effort to preserve the community in which each entity aims to thrive. Community members that are actively in relationship with one another and collaboratively creating meaning and existence include human beings, trees, animals, water bodies, and invisible beings such as ancestors and deities.

\_\_\_

The philosophy of Ubuntu translates simply as “a person is a person through other persons” or “a being is a being through other beings.”

\_\_\_

The entities in this community either possess an active form of shared life force, making it possible for them to act and be acted upon, or possess a passive form of the shared life force, such that they would require another being to act on them to emit their energy. For example, a human being (an entity with an active life force) somewhere in West Africa can boil or squeeze the plant *vernonia amygdalina*, commonly called bitter leaves (an entity with a passive life force), to extract the juice for curing an ailment in the pursuit of meaningful life and wellbeing.

Both the human and non-human entity are thus fundamental to the thriving of the community and individual members in it. It is therefore not uncommon to see indigenous caregivers and health professionals in African societies speaking with a plant, tree or body of water as they would do with a human being during the healing process. We observe further affirmation of the being and shared reality of the non-human in the treatment of kola nuts in many parts of West Africa. Ceremonies such as marriages, child naming, funerals, and the beginning of new seasons begin with the ceremonial breaking of the kola nut, accompanied by prayers and blessings. While the kola nut is held in hand to be broken, one of the key declarations is: “the person who brings kola brings life.” The kola nuts are then broken by the eldest and shared with all attending the ceremony. The declaration that the one who brings kola brings life affirms the life-giving and meaning-making potency of the kola nut in terms of its health benefits, and signifies the profound relational impact of hospitality to others in the sustenance of meaning and wellbeing. Human and non-human entities in an African community come into existence by partaking of the shared life force, and thrive and curate meaning through relationships with other beings in a reciprocation of energy.

What African philosophy can teach you about the good life
By Omedi Ochieng](https://iai.tv/articles/what-african-philosophy-can-teach-you-about-the-good-life-auid-1147)

Ubuntu (with origins in the Zulu/Xhosa language) is now a popular African concept because it captures this relational metaphysics. The [philosophy of Ubuntu](https://iai.tv/articles/african-thought-can-rescue-western-philosophy-auid-2776) translates simply as “a person is a person through other persons” or “a being is a being through other beings.” It is an ontological and existential Afro-communitarian philosophy of relationality, solidarity, co-dependency and cooperation which holds that a person becomes fully and meaningfully so through relationships. We can easily point to similar concepts in different African languages. In my language (the Esan language in the Benin Kingdom of Southern Nigeria), the word *akomen* (“we are better together” or “meaning is collectively attained”) also captures this relational metaphysics. All beings, human, the environment, ancestors, deities and so on are in an interlocked web of relationships such that a gap between the human self, the physically dead, the phenomenal world and intangible entities is neither possible nor desirable. Existing entities need one another to thrive and lead meaningful lives. Thus, the quest for solidarity and harmony is essential and non-negotiable for wellbeing. For example, ancestors are human members of an African community who have properly departed the physical realm of existence to the non-physical. They are those who have completed the physical part of their life course and have taken on higher forms of being, a more intense energy. Once a human being becomes an ancestor, they remain intrinsically interwoven with their kin. The existence of an ancestor becomes crucial for the causal explanation of events in the family from which the ancestor has physically departed, as well as in the larger community.

There are at least two important implications of a relational metaphysics for how we live meaningful lives. First, communities are not simply social or political arrangements but metaphysical realities. We are, by the very nature of reality, interwoven with other entities, human and non-human. Humans cannot exist or flourish without other humans, plants, the sun, the moon, animals, rain, and so on. Thus, to think of ourselves as flourishing due to self-contained qualities alone is to strive to work against our very ontological essence as relational entities. Human-centric thinking is indeed overrated. We cannot even think and cognise our ecosystem successfully without relying on our co-habitants. The leaves tell us when it’s autumn; the clouds tell us when it's about to rain; and global warming tells us how much we have fractured and disrupted our relationship with non-human entities.

Second, the acknowledgement of, and relationship with, invisible yet real energies and forces in the community, such as ancestors, is relevant for our individual flourishing and meaning-making. We cannot explain the present and the presence or absence of meaning in it without acknowledging the past. A human person in a sub-Saharan African (Yoruba) community cannot, for example, makes sense of their existential presence in disconnection from their parents who may have already died. Thus, while such a parent may have lived in the past, they remain real and present in the now in forms of transferred energy, character traits, behaviour and memory. This is why common names among the Yoruba people include Yetunde (“my mother has returned”) and Babatunde (“my father has returned”), connoting the idea of the transference of energy and vitality from a physically dead parent as a living ancestor to a newborn child. Even epigenetics reminds us of how our inherited genes and environment can influence our wellbeing, attitudes, and physical and mental health.

A relational metaphysics reminds us, in a world so focused on humans and their autonomy and independence, that our existence is intrinsically tied up with the existence of other human and non-human entities. It is counterproductive to my wellbeing and meaningful existence to care only for my own rights and wellbeing and not for the rights, wellbeing and survival of other entities. We must rethink our not merely social but metaphysical responsibilities to others in reshaping our understanding of the nature and limits of freedom, autonomy, individuality, and rights in ways that sustain a fine balance with duties and responsibilities to humans and non-humans now and in future generations.
