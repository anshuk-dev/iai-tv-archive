# How Mahler's symphonies reclaim Nietzsche from the far right

*A philosophy better expressed through music*

**By Leah Batstone** | *18th August 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,702 words*

![How Mahler's symphonies reclaim Nietzsche from the far right](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/How-Mahlers-symphonies-reclaimed-Nietzsche-from-the-far-right.webp)

---

*Nietzsche’s philosophy has long been associated with far-right ideologies. Yet Gustav Mahler’s musical engagement with Nietzsche offers a radically different vision. Drawing on Nietzsche’s perspectivism,* Übermensch*, and Eternal Recurrence, Mahler’s symphonies reinterpret Nietzsche’s ideas through a pluralistic and progressive lens. In this piece, musicologist and curator Leah Batstone details Mahler’s engagement with Nietzsche, arguing that music has the potential not only to mirror political and philosophical debates, but also offer original insight into the defining ideas of an epoch.*

The inherent embeddedness of politics and philosophy in music is not always readily apparent or accepted. Indeed, Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine and the consequent decision by some institutions to cut ties with artists supporting and supported by Putin, for example, has led to outraged claims that art is beyond politics and artists are apolitical. Yet music is necessary to how we view the world politically—and to understanding how others have seen it.

The writings of Friedrich Nietzsche offer an example. The philosopher’s bold ideas exercised significant influence over a variety of 20th century political thinkers—most notoriously, members of the Third Reich—and remain apropos to ongoing political discussions. In particular, the self-actualizing drive of the Will to Power and the philosopher’s critique of morality can both be handily pressed into serving certain far-right ideologies looking for justifications for oppression and the denial of facts. Over the past century, the philosopher’s poetic prose has also garnered significant attention from composers. Aside from the tumultuous personal relationship between the philosopher and Richard Wagner, composers including Johannes Brahms, Richard Strauss, Frederick Delius, Alexander Scriabin, Arnold Schoenberg, Anton Webern, and Pierre Boulez all showed an interest in Nietzsche. One composer whose [music](https://iai.tv/articles/pinker-vs-nietzsche-is-music-the-basis-of-language-auid-3247) goes further, encapsulating a particular understanding of the philosopher’s ideas, is Gustav Mahler. Contrary to tendencies to associate Nietzsche with supremacist ideologies approaching fascism, Mahler’s musical interpretations of Nietzschean ideas offer a wholly different set of values. Reflecting the reception of Nietzsche among his university peers, Mahler’s music represents Nietzsche through evocations of compassion, perspectivism, and the power of joy. Mahler’s engagement with Nietzsche helps us understand music’s ability to mirror contemporary interpretations of philosophical and political ideas, and how artworks and the histories contained within them can endure even as our political readings of philosophy change.

By Denis Noble](https://iai.tv/articles/music-is-the-key-to-understanding-life-auid-2723)**In 1896, Mahler completed his [Third Symphony](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sA7BPdZgxGk), a work overtly engaged with Nietzschean texts. The fourth movement features a vocal setting of poetry from [*Thus Spoke Zarathustra*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1998/1998-h/1998-h.htm); the composer famously described the final movement as “God! Or if you like the *Übermensch*”; and he once considered giving the whole piece the title “My Gay Science.” While Nietzsche was popular among *fin de siècle* Viennese intellectuals, Mahler’s treatment of his ideas was not merely the dilettantism of a trend-observing *artiste*. Receiving his introduction to Nietzsche from peers who would become social theorists and politicians, Mahler’s musical encounters with Nietzsche sound a thoughtfully honed reading of the philosopher that shaped Austrian politics in the early 20th century.

\_\_\_

Mahler’s most obvious deployment of Nietzsche is his setting of Zarathustra’s “Midnight Song” in the [fourth movement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dk-7vq5kagI) of the Third Symphony.

O Man! Take heed!  
 What does the deep midnight say?  
 “I was asleep, asleep –,  
 I have awoken from deep dreams: –  
 The world is deep,  
 And deeper than the day imagined.  
 Deep is its grief!  
 Joy, deeper still than heartache!  
 Grief says: Perish!  
 But all joy seeks eternity –,  
 – seeks deep, deep eternity!”[1]

The movement is slow, proceeding with caution and reflection. The harmonies of the orchestra oscillate between shadowy ambivalence and restrained resolution. The alto voice is deliberate and measured. The sung stanzas alternate with a haunting call in the oboe that the composer termed the “bird of the night.”

Despite Mahler’s large orchestral scoring, the music makes space for each of the instrument groups—winds, strings, brass, voice, even a violin solo—and coalesces into a mysterious, pensive beauty that communicates genuine concern. The poetry is itself a telling choice and suggests a depth to Mahler’s philosophical understanding; the text appears again at the end of *Zarathustra*, revealing that the simple song is a reduction of the philosophy of the Eternal Recurrence.

\_\_\_

The motto of the final movement is “Father, look upon my wounds. Let no creature be lost,” thereby drawing Nietzsche’s Superman into the role of savior.

\_\_\_

In a letter to a friend, Mahler further evoked a benevolent interpretation of the *Übermensch*, writing that the Third Symphony’s [finale](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DOJnua08rm4) was “God! Or, if you like, the *Übermensch*.” The letter clearly equates the *Übermensch* with an altruistic figure, rather than the ruthless issuer of punishment found in the Old Testament. The composer included that the motto of the final movement is “Father, look upon my wounds. Let no creature be lost,” thereby drawing Nietzsche’s Superman into the role of savior.

The music once again bolsters this reading. The finale of Mahler’s Third, like many of his concluding movements, is triumphant but also spiritual, drawing on well-established musical tropes of religiosity. Mahler’s conclusion employs four-part melodic writing that imitates choral singing found in worship and a musical structure that returns again and again to a simple melody, imitating the [chorale genre](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HPN88O-LX70) epitomized by J.S. Bach. The addition of “nature sounds” in the form of panpipes, birdsong, and horn calls also plays with Romantic associations of nature with the divine.

At one point while composing the Third, Mahler wrote that he would call the whole symphony “[My Gay Science](https://philoslugs.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/the-gay-science-friedrich-nietzsche.pdf),” a title formulated after Nietzsche. This is perhaps the most opaque of his Nietzschean references, but an important parallel between Mahler’s music and Nietzsche’s ideas is at the center of the book. Perspectivism, an appreciation for the insights gathered from multiple points of view, is among the themes of *The Gay Science*. In Aphorism 374, for example, Nietzsche writes, “The world, on the contrary, has once more become ‘infinite’ to us: in so far we cannot dismiss the possibility that it contains infinite interpretations.” Similarly, one of Mahler’s most characteristic composing techniques is the use of multiple, simultaneous voices.

\_\_\_

Unlike the sequential reading of words on a page, musical performances allows multiple voices, even (or especially) when they are purely instrumental, to speak at once, offering a sonic representation of Nietzschean perspectivism.

\_\_\_

This musical perspectivism can be found throughout Mahler’s music, including in the Third Symphony. Five of the six movements have titles with the formulation “What … Tells Me.” These revelations come from diverse sources: flowers in the meadow, animals in the forest, Man, angels, and love. What is more, the music itself layers plural voices, sequentially and simultaneously. The symphony accumulates more and more contributors, adding to the orchestra a solo voice and ultimately multiple choirs across the third, fourth, and fifth movements. Within the third movement alone, Mahler layers contrasting viewpoints: quotations of his song “The Changing of the Summer Guard,” depicting the raucous exchange of forest animals, is juxtaposed with the distant horn call of a postal carrier, a sonic signifier of humanity. The movement is characteristic of Mahlerian pluralism: the two perspectives coexist, not necessarily engaging one another but creating a mosaic of diverse, simultaneous lives. Unlike the sequential reading of words on a page, musical performances allows multiple voices, even (or especially) when they are purely instrumental, to speak at once, offering a sonic representation of Nietzschean perspectivism.

Finally, these multiple perspectives often produce an unexpected, but powerful adjacency of tragic and comic, illustrating another [Nietzschean](https://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-and-the-perils-of-denying-your-self-auid-2325) value. In contrast to the hopeless message of nihilism sometimes associated with the philosopher, laughter is integral to his work, even forming one of the basic tenets of Zarathustra’s teachings. The contrast between the bickering bird calls in the aforementioned third movement with the melancholic call of man’s postal horn, as well as the deeply reflective and cautionary tone of the fourth movement that follows encapsulates this vacillation between moods. The fifth movement extends these contrasts with a joyful chorus of singing children who recount humorous anecdotes about Heaven’s saints. The ecclesiastical sixth and final movement shows how a joy in the face of struggle can produce an existence that embraces the Eternal Return.

Mahler’s [Nietzsche](https://iai.tv/articles/the-left-and-right-both-get-nietzsche-wrong-matt-mcmanus-auid-2414)—benevolent, solicitous, and laughing before tragedy—reflects a reading of the philosopher found among the men who introduced him to the composer. Mahler’s classmates at the University of Vienna, a group informally known as the Pernerstorfer Circle, consisted of some of the period’s most influential political thinkers and actors. Among them were Victor Adler, leader of the Austrian Social Democrats; Heinrich Friedjung, a political historian and member of the German People’s Party; Engelbert Pernerstorfer, a member of the Austrian Parliament and political ally of Adler; and Heinrich Braun, a member of the Social Democratic Party of Germany married to prominent Nietzschean feminist Lily Braun (née Kretschmann). These men, too, called on Nietzschean ideas in service of a political worldview that promoted social equality for the diverse members of their community. These men, who pioneered efforts such as the eight-hour workday, the right for men to vote, and autonomy for ethnic minorities in Austria-Hungary, connected their social philosophies to the same Nietzschean texts Mahler drew upon: *The Birth of Tragedy Out of the Spirit of Music,* *The Gay Science,* and *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*.

Mahler foretold that [Nietzsche’s](https://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-putin-and-the-spirit-of-russia-auid-2248) writings would have “an epoch-making influence” on his life. But his musical realizations of Nietzschean ideas are not only found in the Third Symphony—they are present throughout his works. The composer’s choral conclusions, plural voices, and tragicomic juxtaposition are especially common in his early symphonies, composed in closest proximity to his exposure to Nietzsche, but they permeate his oeuvre. The philosopher’s influence did more than define an epoch of Mahler’s life. The distinctive reception of Nietzsche’s ideas became a subtle but integral part of a now beloved musical language. The fact of Mahler’s ongoing popularity makes grasping his relationship with Nietzsche all the more salient, both to understanding the composer and the ways music and politics are intertwined. For today’s listeners, Mahler is not only the epitome of the late Romantic symphonist who shaped the 20th century in his own right—he is a political commentator who is offering us a progressive, pluralistic way to “hear” Nietzsche.
