# Truth is dead, and that's a good thing

*The democratic dream of social media is still alive and kicking*

**By Steve Fuller** | *3rd September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,544 words*

![Truth is dead, and that's a good thing](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/steve-fully-media-power-and-the-internet.webp)

---

*For millennia, from Plato to the Politburo, media has served the powerful—all preferring readers to consume, not write themselves. But social media has flipped the script. In a world where tweets compete with treatises for authority, expertise no longer rules. Philosopher Steve Fuller argues this "post-truth" moment isn't a crisis. It's democracy finally taking itself seriously.*

We have grown accustomed to the “democratizing” character of social media, with its bewildering proliferation of alternative points of view, any of which seems capable of commanding the collective imagination—at least for a brief period. While everyone still claims to be seeking the truth, expertise matters less than ever before. Easily accessed information on user-friendly platforms potentially renders anyone an “authority” on something of current interest—at least for a brief period. Regardless of what you make of this situation, it marks a distinct moment in media history that may be a turning point. It is only in the modern era that the idea of communication as involving equal communicants has been promoted, and today’s social media exemplifies what that idea looks like on a global scale.

In[*Media and the Power of Knowledge*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/media-and-the-power-of-knowledge-9781780930923/), I argue that for most of human history, media has operated on a “broadcast” model, based on an asymmetry between the producers and consumers of information. This point is easily seen in terms of the past hundred years, during which radio and television largely functioned as oligopolies—often controlled by the state—designed to “inform, educate and entertain” the masses (to recall the BBC’s mission statement). However, the same approach has applied to all information and communication technologies, starting with writing. Thus, literacy drives are generally more enthusiastic for people to read what is written than to write for themselves.

\_\_\_

In today’s terms, Plato was a man of mass media, not social media.

\_\_\_

While Socrates famously denounced writing in the *Phaedrus* for corrupting memory and morals, he also less famously in the *Timaeus* praised writing—specifically, the codification of the Homeric epics—for its capacity to discipline the memories and morals of readers. The difference is that Plato—the one behind the Socratic mask—objected to the Sophists teaching Athenians to write down their thoughts before they spoke: It placed too much power in the hands of individuals to generate alternative futures that might upset the social order. However, he liked people’s speech being held accountable to a script that was externally authorized. In today’s terms, Plato was a man of mass media, not social media.

But even if all philosophy is footnotes to Plato, Plato doesn’t have the final word. Had he gotten his way, relatively few people nowadays would be licensed to write, even if many would be able and even required to read. In other words, literacy would display the sort of asymmetry that is familiar from the history of other communication and transport media: One is free to be conveyed by them, but not free to convey them. You’re free to ride in a car but not free to drive it, unless you’re licensed. Interestingly, the most influential journalist of the twentieth century, Walter Lippmann, believed that exactly this principle should apply to such mass circulation media as newspapers, magazines, radio, and television. And he cited Plato as his source, albeit modified for democratic times.

But Lippmann also failed to get his way. Otherwise, there would be no social media, and all mass media would look like the BBC in its heyday. There are a couple of factors that contributed to things not turning out as Lippmann (and Plato) would have wanted.

A long-term factor was that by the fourteenth century Christian Europe had begun to think of writing as the human reenactment of the word (*logos*) by which God had created the world. Moreover, it was conceived as an exercise of free will, the only divine quality that humans retained after the Fall. This perspective was amplified in the Protestant Reformation, resulting in the Bible’s publication in the languages of the common people, so they could respond to it in their native tongues. It was in service of this cause that the printing press launched the media revolution that Marshall McLuhan famously dubbed the “Gutenberg Galaxy.” Its signature ideological statement was the poet John Milton’s defense of publishing without license in his 1644 *Areopagitica*, which coupled the right to self-expression with the right to self-defense. The legacy of this inflammatory pamphlet from the English Civil War is the juxtaposition of the first two of the ten amendments to the US Constitution known as the “Bill of Rights.”

A factor contemporary to Lippmann was that Edward Bernays, his colleague in the propaganda campaign to get Americans to fight in the First World War, had started the field of “public relations” in the 1920s. Its self-appointed task was to convert passive consumption into acts of self-assertion in democracies that were getting wealthier by the day. Bernays may be most responsible for the idea that buying is an alternative form of voting, which social media nowadays blurs as “liking.” Bernays effectively believed that purchasing power made everyone their own media platform. Of course, certain individuals, groups, and corporate entities may be better placed to take advantage of the situation. But it’s all preferable to state regulation, even in Lippmann’s benign sense. Bernays’ legacy is felt not only in the modern style of advertising, which appeals to an “empowered” consumer with “choice,” but also in the use of such standard social science research methods as interviews, surveys, and focus groups for both business and political purposes, often addressing the same sorts of questions.

Taken together, these two factors have laid the foundation for today’s social media, in which the producers and consumers of information are on a virtual level playing field. It has generated our “post-truth” condition, which is all about “naming the game” that everyone else is forced to play. In terms of knowledge, it means that there is less focus on what is true or false and more on the way the difference is decided. Nowadays, learned publications, journalism, Wikipedia, ChatGPT and UGC (“user-generated content”) all jostle in a common space for the role of epistemic arbiter. Unless or until the state regulates these competing platforms for authority, people are left to their own devices, both to decide truth for themselves and to shape the field of play as much as they can.

In [*Media and the Power of Knowledge*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/media-and-the-power-of-knowledge-9781780930923/)*,* I cast this situation in terms of two parallel transitions, one introduced by Marshall McLuhan and the other by the sociologist Max Weber. McLuhan described technology as initially a “hot” medium that forces everyone to reorient their lives towards it, but it later “cools” as it becomes the default medium of conveyance. He was especially impressed by the speed with which television became “cool” in this sense. For his part, Weber held that all religions begin with prophets promising an alternative way of being in the world, which remains undelivered when the prophet dies. This vacuum in leadership is then filled by priests who standardize the prophet’s voice into content that can be delivered for mass consumption. Both transitions are reasonably seen as a process of narrowing the space for permissible thought and action. All existing conflicts over the use of the technology or the interpretation of the religion come to be resolved in a hierarchy of authority between “expert” and “lay” practitioners.

\_\_\_

The problem with most pro-democracy theorists of the modern era is that they have been great at talking the talk, but now they are being forced to walk the walk.

\_\_\_

The punchline of my book is that social media is reversing these transitions. We are moving back to hot media and prophetic religions. The easiest way to see this point is to look at the return of aphoristic expression, most notably on Twitter, a platform whose most adept users communicate in the telegraphic style of prophets. Moreover, Twitter is not an outlier. A common feature of all social media platforms is a structural antipathy to the sort of elaborate and extended verbal displays that characterize how authority is conventionally demonstrated by academics, legislators, and members of the liberal professions. Social media favors information that appears in short, staccato bursts, with much audiovisual complementarity. In this brave new world, what academics often complain about as the public’s “short attention span” is better seen as a reconfiguration of the attention economy more generally.

Does the ascendency of social media mean the end of academic authority and rule of experts? It certainly means the end of their presumptive epistemic superiority. But that’s exactly what we should expect of an educated society that takes democracy seriously. The problem with most pro-democracy theorists of the modern era is that they have been great at talking the talk, but now they are being forced to walk the walk, which is proving much harder. While the short to medium term is likely to bring considerable turbulence and uncertainty about what to believe, say, and do, my best guess is that the situation will stabilize in a manner resembling the settlement of Christendom, in which Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, and a variety of post-Christian faiths co-exist—if not in harmony, at least in mutual tolerance.
