# "Reality is a controlled hallucination" is not incoherent

*In response to Evan Thompson*

**By Jan Westerhoff** | *28th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,945 words*

!["Reality is a controlled hallucination" is not incoherent](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/article-template-copy4.webp)

---

*A growing number of neuroscientists claim that “reality is a controlled hallucination,” a projection of our brains. But [in a recent article for the IAI](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-not-a-controlled-hallucination-auid-3517), the philosopher Evan Thompson argued that this is nonsense: if brains hallucinate reality, then the brain itself must be part of the hallucination, so the theory falls apart. Now, Oxford philosopher **Jan Westerhoff**, author of* Reality: A Very Short Introduction*, replies to Thompson, arguing that his objection misses the radical core of the neuroscientists’ claim. The idea is coherent, he says, but only if we embrace its implication that our theories aren’t descriptions of the world, but rather provide models for intervening in it.*

Some contemporary neuroscientists describe the brain as a “fantastic organ,” able to create the fantasy—or hallucination—of the world around us, a world that appears to impress itself on our senses, with photons bouncing off objects and hitting our retinas, sound-waves from the radio entering our ears, and olfactory molecules from food being registered by our noses, but which really is only something the brain has constructed. This brain-based construction, the story goes, is not entirely unmoored from extra-cranial reality: there are the sensory data against which the brain’s fantasies are tested, thereby constraining the hallucination within the boundaries of the real. The most successful contemporary incarnation of this approach is [predictive processing theory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?app=desktop&v=MbjtRqtAd-o): the [“hallucinations” are the brain’s best guesses](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston) for explaining the perceptual data, which are tested and adapted as more data come in.

[Critics](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-not-a-controlled-hallucination-auid-3517) were quick to point out a curious circularity that seems to follow from this form of [neuroconstructivism](https://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real): if the world is the model produced by the brain, and the brain is part of the world, then the brain is a part of the model that generates itself. This appears to lead to an absurd situation, “like a reflection generating its object or a film producing the projector.” Less metaphorically, this picture of the relation between the brain and the world seems to undermine itself both ontologically and epistemologically, leading to problems with respect to existence and with respect to knowledge.

If the brain produces the world-model, the brain, as a cause, seems to have to be there first, before the effect, the world-model, is produced. But the brain is part of the world-model, so how can it exist before having produced itself? Moreover, we study the brain and develop a specific account of how it creates the fantasy of the world around us. But once we have accepted this, all our knowledge of the brain also turns out to be part of this fantasy, so how can we trust that our initial claims about the brain and its model-creating capacity actually constitute knowledge?

How might one respond to this criticism? An immediate possibility is to give up brain-based constructivism, arguing that because it lands us in these inextricable puzzles about reality and knowledge, we should let go of the idea that the world we live in is just a construction of our brains. Reality is not a controlled hallucination. Computational models of cognition like the ones offered by predictive processing theory are idealized models, but cannot be literally true as theories of the mind.

\_\_\_

The solution is to let go of the realist assumptions. We know the tables and chairs in-the-model, but not the tables and chairs themselves, and we also do not know the brain itself, but merely its representation.

\_\_\_

A second possibility is to hold on to neuroconstructivism, but to reject the circularity. One might argue that the brain is somehow special. Even though tables and chairs are not really out there, but are merely our brain’s best guesses of how to systematize specific sequences of electrochemical excitation that come in through our senses, the brain is not like this. It is really out there. While this certainly avoids the circularity we have just described, it is quite unclear what the basis of this special pleading is supposed to be. According to our ordinary understanding of the world, the brain is an entity just like tables and chairs, more complex, for sure, but not different in its nature.

There is, however, a third possible response. For this, it is necessary to realize that the problem we encountered is not a problem of neuroconstructivism as such, but of neuroconstructivism combined with [a form of realism](https://iai.tv/articles/introducing-the-realism-debate-auid-1763). By “realism” I mean the claim that through our senses and our cognitive capacities we have direct access to objects in the world—not just to representations or models of these objects, but to the objects themselves. Now if this is the case, our brain provides us with a model of tables and chairs and brains (so we know the table-in-the-model, the chair-in-the-model, and the brain-in-the-model), but we also have epistemic access to the brain itself, as any neurosurgeon will confirm. This then raises the question of the relation between the brain-in-the-model and the brain itself. If they are the same thing, then we end up with the problem of the brain being part of the model that generates that very model.

The solution is to [let go of the realist assumptions](https://iai.tv/video/beyond-truth-and-reality). We know the tables and chairs in-the-model, but [not the tables and chairs themselves](https://iai.tv/video/beyond-the-reality-illusion-hilary-lawson), and we also do not know the brain itself, but merely its representation. What I am recommending here is to replace the realist background assumption with a form of transcendental idealism. Transcendental idealism, which [goes back to Immanuel Kant](https://iai.tv/articles/kant-vs-hume-can-we-access-reality-auid-3125), draws a sharp distinction between the world as it appears to us (the phenomena) and the things in themselves (the noumena). The time-space continuum filled with various things—the world that surrounds us—is the realm of phenomena. Whatever lies beyond this—the realm of noumenal things-in-themselves—is epistemically inaccessible to us, just as whatever it is that stands behind the tables and chairs in-the-model that our brain creates cannot be accessed from within the neurocomputationally created model that is our world.

\_\_\_

Our process of inquiry can change our epistemic standards.

\_\_\_

Various authors have recently pointed out the affinities between the Kantian picture and the view of the world as a brain-based construction (see, for example, [here](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/nous.12552), [here](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-025-05169-2), and [here](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05522-z)). Endorsing a view along the lines of transcendental idealism solves the ontological problem, for if we can only think about and refer to phenomenal brains in phenomenal time (but not to real brains and real time, whatever that may be), the question of how the real brain can have existed before the phenomenal brain in real time turns out to be conceptually ill-formed. It is a question to be put aside, not a question to be answered.

We might worry, however, that even having addressed the ontological problem, the epistemological problem still remains. If we are only dealing with brain-representations, rather than with brains, why would we trust the entire story of how the brain brings about the model of the world? Do we not require epistemic access to the real brain for such theories to be at all convincing?

There are two points we should note here. First, our process of inquiry can change our epistemic standards. We might begin with a theory of knowledge according to which objects in the world impress themselves onto the senses, so that our task as a perceiver is to reconstruct their properties. A cube generates a two-dimensional image on our retina, which in turn generates a sequence of electric impulses in the brain, and we try to recover the original three-dimensional structure of the cube as a representation in our mind. Greater acquaintance with the mechanics of perceptual processing might then lead us to the position that the brain essentially makes predictions about the spatial world, predictions that are constrained by incoming information, but essentially the product of a “fantastic” organ. At this point medium-sized dry goods in space and time—the tables and chairs and sticks and stones that populate everyday experience—have already left the stage, having been replaced by very generally characterized perturbations of our nervous system. Instead of saying that having come this far our inquiry has short-circuited itself (“if we don’t have epistemic access to the material brains themselves, located in space and time, we have no knowledge of brains at all”), our epistemic standards have shifted. Neuroscientific knowledge has never really been about reconstructing, in our mind, the behavior of brains “out there.”

\_\_\_

Both for the transcendental idealist and for those who believe that reality is a controlled hallucination, there is something behind the veil. It is just impossible to say anything about it.

\_\_\_

This brings us to our second point: We do not need to drill down to the objects themselves to achieve knowledge. (For the Kantian, knowledge, either of the everyday or of the scientific variety, need not, and in fact cannot, require acquaintance with the noumenal things-in-themselves.) Knowledge of connections between representations might be all the scientific knowledge we could ever want, or could indeed achieve. Much of scientific inquiry, after all, is not about grasping at the things themselves, but an inquiry into how the parts of the appearances or representations are connected so we can explain and predict their behavior. Which atmospheric conditions today are reliably correlated with the weather in five days? Which substance a can produce a more durable bond between substances b and c? How does the shape of a protein relate to its properties? All of these questions can be answered at the level of representations, without the need to access objects behind the veil of the constructed neuronal model.

Both for the transcendental idealist and for those who believe that reality is a controlled hallucination, there is something behind the veil. It is just impossible to say anything about it. The neuroconstructivist cannot say, for example, that what stands behind the table-representation are whatever physical objects cause the table-representation in our brain-based model, because both the notion of a physical object and the notion of causality are located only within the domain of the neuronal model. We cannot step out of the model to survey how causality (real causality, that is, not phenomenal causality or causality-in-the-model) links up the real table with the table-in-the-model.

Some philosophers are very critical of the epistemically inaccessible noumenal world of things-in-themselves behind the veil, [describing](https://philpapers.org/rec/PUTPPV-6) it as a “one-knows-not-what which solves our problems one-knows-not-how.” It appears as a kind of predicational void, a central notion around which our world turns, while no characteristic or predicate can be made to stick to it. This might tempt some to add a further twist to the tale: what if the noumenal entities behind the phenomena are only phenomenally real? For the neuroconstructivist, this would mean that at the level of representation we conceive of the world as the brain’s controlled hallucination, as a neuronally constructed model based on the brain’s predictive abilities, constrained by incoming sensory information. The source of this information is outside of the brain, but it is not outside of the model. Its being “behind the veil” is only a postulate on our side of the veil. Even talk of the phenomenal and the noumenal, of the veil of neuronal representation and the extra-cranial input that constrains it, does not allow us to go beyond the model.

Opinions [differ](https://www.academia.edu/79100261/The_Non_Existence_Of_The_Real_World_Analysis_symposium) on whether one wants to ride this bus all the way to the final stop. Yet in any case it is clear that for the neuroconstructivist there is no worry that the reflection generates its object on the other side of the looking-glass. We only have epistemic access to our side, and on this side the brain generates the model of the world. The source of the brain-representation we operate with might be necessarily hidden from us, but it does not generate the kind of vicious loop the critics of neuroconstructivism worry about.
