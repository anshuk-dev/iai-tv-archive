# You can change what you see by changing what you think

*Studies show that our beliefs can hack our senses*

**By Neil Mehta** | *3rd November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 5 mins* | *1,005 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![You can change what you see by changing what you think](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/you-can-change-what-you-see-by-changing-what-you-think.webp)

---

*Perception, we usually assume, delivers us the world, and it’s then up to us to decide what to believe. Ideally, we simply match our beliefs to what we perceive. But philosopher **Neil Mehta** argues that it’s not as simple as this. Your thoughts and beliefs and prejudices all influence perception itself, playing a role in constructing your experience. This means, Mehta argues, that your perceptions can be more or less rational, and even more or less moral, and that we cannot simply trust all that we see, hear, touch and smell.*

Some things, like apples and fire hydrants, are red. Some things, like frogs and blueberries, are other colors. But some things, like the number 4 and the concept of justice, aren’t any color at all—they aren’t the kinds of things that can be colored.

Similarly, some things are *rational**,* like believing that 1 + 1 = 2 or drawing conclusions that are well-supported by your evidence. And some things are irrational, like believing that 1 + 1 = 3 or jumping to conclusions that are unsupported by your evidence. But some things cannot be assessed for rationality at all. The feeling of itchiness in your elbow is neither rational nor irrational—it just *is*.

What about *conscious perceptions**,* like your experience of seeing an apple, hearing a violin, or smelling vanilla—can these be rational/irrational? The natural answer is no. For surely if a mental state can be rational/irrational, then that state can, in principle, be revised in light of new information. And although you can, when you receive new information, revise your *belief* that there is a red apple in front of you, you cannot revise your *perception* of a red apple in front of you. At best, you can close your eyes.

Despite the appeal of this argument, I think that many conscious perceptions might be rational/irrational after all. Here’s why.

When we perceive things, we often apply concepts to them. When you look around your office, you might see a desk and a laptop, and you will conceptualize them *as* a desk and a laptop. You cannot help yourself: conceptualizations like this happen automatically.

Philosopher Eric Mandelbaum [has persuasively argued](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phpr.12368), on the basis of substantial scientific evidence, that conceptualization can occur *within perception itself*. The idea is that you don’t *first* consciously perceive a scene and *then* conceptualize it. Instead, your visual system packages the conceptualization of the scene *into* your conscious perception of it. At the conscious level, you receive both things, bundled together, at the same time.

[In one telling experiment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-013-0605-z) cited by Mandelbaum and conducted by Potter et al., subjects were presented with a word, like “flowers,” followed by a series of images, and they had to say whether the image matched the word. The key feature of the experiment was that each image was presented for *just 13 milliseconds*—that’s about 1/8 of the time it takes to blink. Even so, subjects performed at well above chance. But feedback processing just within the visual system takes about 50 milliseconds, and post-perceptual feedback processing takes much longer. So this is evidence that the conceptualizations in these cases were occurring perceptually (in the visual system), not in post-perceptual cognition.

Mandelbaum cites many more experiments like this. In [one](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698905005110), conducted by Kirchner and Thorpe, subjects were presented with two images of a natural scene for just 20 milliseconds per image. One image contained an animal in a landscape, while the other contained only a landscape. The subject’s task was to look at the image containing the animal. Subjects were able to do this—to perceive the animal, conceptualize it as such, and direct their gaze to it—in just 120 milliseconds (which is about one eye-blink). Again, this is just too fast for the conceptualization to be post-perceptual.

What this suggests is that some conceptualizations do not happen after conscious perception; [they are part of conscious perception themselves](https://iai.tv/articles/how-your-beliefs-change-what-you-perceive-auid-2845). *But* *conceptualizations can be rational or irrational*. If you see a red apple-shaped object, then it is rational for you to conceptualize it as an apple and irrational for you to conceptualize it as a frog. Therefore, conscious perceptions—in particular, the conceptual parts of them—[can be rational or irrational](https://iai.tv/video/in-the-name-of-reason).

Of course, this makes sense only if the conceptual elements of conscious perceptions are, in principle, rationally revisable. And this kind of rational revision might not be possible immediately. If you see a convincing wax replica of an apple, it will *look* like an apple—you will conceptualize it as such—even if you know better. Still, conceptualizations are rationally revisable *in the long run*: we can, and routinely do, [train ourselves to perceptually recognize things better](https://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real). Chess players get better at seeing pawn structures, musicians get better at hearing melodies, and sommeliers get better at smelling wines.

But why would it matter if conscious perceptions can be rational/irrational? Philosopher [Susanna Siegel has identified one reason](https://academic.oup.com/book/10286?login=false): we are often morally responsible for our own irrationality. She gives the example of a police officer who sees a tool in the hands of a Black person, conceptualizes it as a gun, and shoots them. It might seem that the officer isn’t morally responsible: it really looked to them like the other person had a gun, and they responded appropriately to that situation. However, suppose that the officer’s conscious perception, in particular their conceptualization of the tool as a gun, was irrational—the officer perceptually conceptualized things [in a way that was warped by their racial biases](https://iai.tv/video/the-perception-bias) rather than being sensitive to the actual visual cues in the scene. In that case, it *shouldn’t* have looked to the officer as if the other person had a gun, and the officer might be morally responsible for the tragic consequences that came afterwards.

Rationality is something we prize, and when we fail to achieve it, we can often learn to do better. If our conscious perceptions can be rational or irrational, then perhaps here, too, [we must learn to do better](https://iai.tv/articles/seeing-is-not-perceiving-auid-2185).
