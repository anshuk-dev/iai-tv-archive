# The real political divide is establishment vs anti-establishment

*Anti-system movements today and the threat to democracy*

**By Jonathan Hopkin** | *19th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 23 mins* | *4,679 words*

![The real political divide is establishment vs anti-establishment](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-real-political-divide-is-establishment-vs-anti-establishment.webp)

---

*Left vs right, democrats vs socialists - political economist Jonathan Hopkin makes the argument that the most significant distinction in today's political landscape is that between those who support a system, and those who identify as anti-system. Establishment vs anti-establishment. If we want to move past this division and re-embrace liberal democracy, the political system must decrease its technocratic tendencies, and bring some of the anti-system into the system itself.*

**Let’s talk about your book** [***Anti-system politics***](https://academic.oup.com/book/33608)**. First of all – why is the dichotomy system-anti-system so important in this moment? What does it mean?**

This anti-system concept wasn’t invented by me. [It dates back to Giovanni Sartori](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23248823.2017.1389122), the Italian political scientist who coined this term to describe political parties who were outside the mainstream of politics, and in particular that could not be counted upon to sustain the basic institutions of the democratic state. He was thinking in particular at the time of the fascist party or the communist party in Italy, or similar kind of groups in Germany or France, and how the mainstream pro-democracy political parties had more or less all agreed to keep those parties out of government.

And then, after the global financial crisis in the late 2000s, we had a wave of what is often called populist parties, winning a lot of votes, and sometimes picking up on this anti-establishment theme.

So I thought it was a good idea to start thinking of this in general, rather than just thinking about [parties of the far right](http://iai.tv/video/the-rise-of-the-new-right), or far left, breaking through, but how there might just be a general move to overturn basic patterns of how politics is done, in particular related to the political economy. I’m particularly interested in how all these groups, anti-system to some degree, are challenging not only the political status quo but also the economic status quo.

**You talk about establishment and pro-democracy parties – how would you define the establishment and what is its relationship to democracy?**

That’s a more difficult question than it sounds. The establishment on one level is the elites who usually have access to influencing policy and occupying key positions of power. And quite often when we talk about the establishment, it has a negative connotation. At the same time, the establishment parties being supportive of basic norms of liberal democracy, obviously that’s not such a negative thing. Very often these anti-system parties are challenging both things. So I have a slightly awkward relationship to these movements – because on one side, I can sympathise with the desire to overturn things and change the way politics is done because I think there’s a lot of things wrong with it. But at the same time, I categorically don’t like the idea of dispensing with liberal democracy all together – very often you end up throwing the baby out with the bath water. Unfortunately, it turns out that most of the political anti-system movements that have performed best electorally and got their hands on the levers of power have tended to be the kind of parties that don’t really believe in liberal democracy at all; so namely the far right.

**You cite in your book Donald Trump, and Sartori referred initially to Mussolini – it’s interesting how these figures seem to be in many ways part of the system. Does the anti-system eventually merge with the system?**

That’s a question that I didn’t address in the book – it’s been out five years and things have moved on since. In Trump’s first term, he was very much arriving, becoming president in a system where a lot of things were stacked against him: a lot of opposition from the Republican party in Congress, a lot of opposition from the governing institutions, he couldn’t pick the people he wanted for the Federal Reserve and other key institutions. So that time, he could claim to be an anti-institution leader coming in to shake things up.

I think what we see now is that [he’s actually on the path to becoming the establishment](http://iai.tv/video/the-hope-you-find-at-rock-bottom), and a very authoritarian establishment at that. There’s nothing stopping the anti-establishment from becoming the establishment, if they manage to pare down the edifice of liberal democracy and institute a one-party, one-man rule, which is what the US is looking like at the moment. It hadn’t gotten that far by 2019 when I finished the book.

I think the US is a real warning bell: don’t expect that the mass of not so politically-engaged citizens are going to man the barricades to protect liberal democracy.

\_\_\_

But at the same time, I was reasonably convinced that this problem wasn’t going to go away. I think in the first few years of this wave, we saw a lot of people in the liberal-centre assuming that people would come to their senses and realize that someone like Trump is incompetent, and then come rushing back into the arms of the establishment, who would sort things out. And even during Covid, there were many people thinking that technocratic expertise would come back into play and people would see the benefits. [That didn’t really happen](http://iai.tv/articles/republicans-assert-party-loyalty-by-ignoring-medical-advice-on-covid-19-auid-1380). If anything, Covid seems to have charged a lot of this anti-system feeling, to such an extent that epidemiology is now a partisan issue in the US and not only.

So, clearly something has changed in that I think large parts of the population are either actively hostile to the way that liberal democracy is practiced in the West at the moment, or else are not so committed to it that they’re willing to come out and defend it. And I think that’s the opening that people like Trump have identified. Obviously nowhere else is so far down the line as the US, except Hungary perhaps. In Western Europe things look better, for now. But I think the US is a real warning bell: don’t expect that the mass of not so politically-engaged citizens are going to man the barricades to protect liberal democracy. I think that the assumption that everyone wants liberal democracy has to be questioned.

**Why do you think of late people have become so disenamoured with liberal democracy after the enthusiasm of the 90s?**

I haven’t completely made up my mind on that. On one level, clearly liberal democracy is not performing particularly well, especially over the last 20 years. In my book I talk a lot about the economic consequences of the financial crash in 2008. And it’s certainly true that we haven’t had a return to the kind of growth levels we enjoyed in the 1990s and 2000s before the crisis, and absolutely not to the growth levels we enjoyed in the post-war period, up to the 70s and 80s. Obviously there’s a lot of inequality, real income is growing very slowly, welfare states have been put under a lot of strain because of population ageing and so on. And governments – I mean [look at the Starmer government in Britain](http://iai.tv/articles/pragmatic-politicians-are-doomed-to-fail-auid-3346), it’s tentatively trying to change some aspects of policy, increase investment, borrowing to invest, regulatory reforms, pushing the green transition to get house building going. But they’re pushing on a piece of string. Governments look incapable, impotent. They look like they can’t lift the dial on the problems people are facing. A lot of it is – people look at the performance of elected governments in the West and think, they’re not doing that great, why would I support them? Which is why we see a lot of turnover, parties not getting re-elected so much; if you have a spell in government you’re going to lose the next election because nothing you do is going to bring people round. So I think that is part of it is that.

Although there is a more underlying problem, which is that perhaps the fundamental social, structural foundations of liberal democracy have been eroding without us really noticing, which is obviously a much bigger type of question.

**So do you think the critique of liberal democracy mainly comes from economic hardship and practical difficulties, rather than it is an ideology?**

I think it’s primarily that but not only. I came to this through observing party and electoral politics in Western democracies – this is my main academic field. Conventional wisdom by now is that political parties are getting more and more detached from citizen concerns, fewer and fewer people are involved in forming party politics, people don’t trust politicians anymore, etc. The US is slightly different: because of the highly polarised situation in the US, people have become more partisan but *without* having a lot of faith in politicians. One of the little data points I remember putting in the book: around the early 2010s in the US, only 8% of citizens polled had any trust in congress as an institution. 8%. Crazy number. So people don’t trust politicians – they don’t expect anything from them, they feel like they’re being lied to, deceived all the time. And not entirely without reason.

I think what else is going on is that people’s expectations of politics might have changed. They see politics as a kind of supermarket: you try that brand of burgers, or prawns, or whatever you’re buying – you choose it, and if you’re satisfied, you keep buying. Obviously politics isn’t like that, but a lot of people experience it through the lens of consumption. And then when they go for a party and they don’t get what they think they were asking for, they’re disappointed. That’s because politics isn’t a market for consumer goods, it’s something else entirely.

**You claim in your book that “politics has to restart” now. What do you mean by that – what is politics then?**

I took inspiration from Sheri Berman, who wrote this great book, [*The primacy of politic*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/primacy-of-politics/DD19C88ECBF20B33D8D6DE445D198D13)*s* about 20 years ago. It’s about the interwar period and then the post-war period. She argues that in the interwar period, politics took a back seat to economics. Because of institutions like the gold standard, without getting into the international financial history of it, politics was hamstrung from being able to do anything about the problems people had, which obviously were many in the interwar period, with the Great Depression and so on. Sheri Berman argues that after the Second World War it becomes apparent to elites that there’s no way of governing a capitalist economy without bringing people into the decision-making process, incorporating trade unions, corporatist institutions, committing the government to full employment, even perhaps at the expense of some inflation, using Keynesian macro-economic demand management. All of these new policy practices brought politics back in, plugging people back into the economic policy making process. That’s what made the post-war system successful economically and politically, attaching people to democratic rule as a concept.

And we moved away from all that after the ‘70s and ‘80s. There’s a move back to thinking that politics shouldn’t mess around with the economy, that markets should be allowed to do their thing with minimal intervention from elected politicians, who could only be expected to screw things up or manipulate the economy for personal partisan gain. So we needed to create institutions like independent central banks or the European Union, another example of economic governance that cuts popular demand out of policy-making. And unsurprisingly, especially when the kind of economy these institutions have produced works very well for the top 1%, pretty well for the top 10%, and not at all for everyone else – if you then cut off any channels for public participation in economic policy-making, and tell them that we need to leave it all to experts, and these experts end up making decisions that seem to benefit mainly the financial and economic elite of society, why wouldn’t people get mad, why wouldn’t people want to vote for someone who would smash all that up?

And for all the ways Trump is a classic wealthy elite in his own way, certainly not someone whose going to overturn capitalism, he has however challenged a lot of the neoliberal institutions that people have been railing against. He’s challenged free trade. And while his ideas about what to do about free trade are crazy and make no sense at all, his identifying free trade as a problem for some Americans is not crazy at all, it’s very clear: neoliberalization went very well for some, and very badly for others, and the others are a lot of people. But in the meantime, he’s destroying democracy and that’s very hard to recover from.

There’s been an attempt to rationalize politics, to turn it into a process without any of the excitable theatrical side of politics we get to see in elections.

\_\_\_

**Have western liberal democratic governments forgotten the affective, emotional side of politics? Has that come back?**

That’s a good point. Obviously in the neoliberal politics of the pre-financial and immediate post-financial crisis era, we trust central banks to manage the economy, make decisions on everyone’s behalf, even though we don’t get to elect the people who run them. A lot of big regulatory decisions are farmed out to non-elected bodies – the idea is to bring sober rationality to policy-making. But of course, if you don’t involve people in policy-making, and it seems to not work for them, they’re going to feel excluded, they’re going to feel marginalised, they’re going to feel humiliated even.

There’s been an attempt to rationalize politics, to turn it into a process without any of the excitable theatrical side of politics we get to see in elections. We still get plenty of that of course, but it doesn’t actually make much difference to the policies that come about. [Jacob Hacker and Paul Pierson](https://politicalscience.yale.edu/publications/winner-take-all-politics-how-washington-made-rich-richer-and-turned-its-back-middle) have this great way of describing it: they talk about the politics of electoral spectacle – the politics most of us are involved in during elections and voting is mostly a performance. Then there’s the politics of what they call organised combat going on behind the scenes, which of course no one ever really hears about unless you read *The Financial times*, and even then. The lobbying and regulatory decisions and so on. So we’ve closed off channels for political participation. There’s an assumption among political policy making elites that the people can’t be trusted because they’re a little bit crazy, irrational, they don’t understand things. That’s true in many respects. But by cutting people out, you don’t eliminate that irrationality. It’s still there bubbling under the surface, and someone like Trump comes along and very artfully turns that into political capital, and not just Trump, but all across Europe, politicians on the far right have been very successful in mobilizing the sense that people have of being excluded, belittled, shut out.

**You’ve cited Trump – in your book you go through quite a bit of anti-system politics examples. What does the anti-system politics spectrum consist of?**

The right is certainly the most successful part. There are also classic movements of the radical left trying to occupy the space that mainstream parties have left vacant. In the US, there’s Bernie Sanders, or AOC, or Mamdani – that’s anti-system too, as far as I can see, because they are challenging the standard way of doing things, the elitist style of politics. In southern Europe, in the immediate aftermath of the financial crisis we had a lot of radical left movements, like the campaign against austerity measures doing very well in Greece, Spain, and Portugal, to some extent in Italy. And then we have these weird sui generis movements like the Five Star Movement in Italy, for example, something that wasn’t easily identifiable as being either on the left or the right, but it was certainly anti-system in a very visible way. In fact, that’s kind of all it was, it didn’t have much in the way of concrete proposals to improve things, but what it was about was destroying the establishment, overturning elected politicians: they all suck, let’s get rid of them. And then these fanciful ideas of internet democracy to replace it.

Then we also have nationalist movements in multinational states such as UK and Spain. They also got a wind in their sails after the crisis – the Scottish National Party became the dominant party in Scotland straight after the financial crisis, and is still in government there. The Catalan nationalist movement took a really radical turn, suddenly campaigning openly for independence, when before it had never gone that far.

All of these are part of the same picture of people saying enough – things are not working, we all feel poorer, politicians don’t listen to us, we need to change. And of course depending on what kind of options are out there, that might be the far right, it might be the far left, it might be the nationalist movements.

Of course, what I also talk about in the book is that different kinds of people go for different kinds of options. The far right is the preserve in particular of less educated people in les prosperous areas – just about everywhere, although Trump of course has gone beyond that base because of the two-party nature of American politics. But it’s the same kind of base of people in former industrial areas – and those classic petty bourgeoisie parts of the private sector.

Whereas the far left is more urban, young, highly educated, frustrated people who can’t pay the rent, in New York, London, or Paris. And if there’s a nationalist credence – that can exploit these opportunities too.

\_\_\_

The establishment has become sociologically a really small group – it’s become a very elitist politics.

\_\_\_

**Is the establishment then the more traditional upper middle-class suburbia?**

In a way the argument that I’m trying to make in the book is that the establishment has become sociologically a really small group – it’s become a very elitist politics of the charmed circle of people who are benefitting most from today’s economy, an ever-smaller group. There’s a lot of potential for anti-system sentiment from anyone outside that hallowed circle.

So people in London and other prosperous areas who are earning good money and can afford to pay the rent, or maybe own homes, they don’t want things to change all that much. But anyone outside London or similar areas, or in London who doesn’t have a property, or who can’t find a higher-paying job, is potentially being mobilised against the system.

This is the problem – this kind of capitalism funnelled gains upwards to an ever smaller group at the top, and everybody else out there is wide open to being mobilised around some radical alternative because the way things are just isn’t working for them.

**I find it interesting that you characterise the system in a political way, as liberal democracy, rather than as an economic system, like capitalism**.

In the book I kind of try to have it both ways, and I don’t know to what extent I get away with it. For me the problem is fundamentally that we’ve allowed the economy to take over democracy. The period in which democracy became consolidated, at least in the West, was a period in which democracy also – in Sheri Berman’s description – had the upper hand. Capitalism had to tread carefully. And there were opportunities for democratically elected governments to do things that weren’t always in the best interests of capital in the short-run but were in the broader interest of society. At some point, that broke down.

This post-war consensus around capitalists not maximizing all their profits all of the time whatever the costs, accepting higher rates of taxation, accepting higher levels of regulation by governments in the public interest, started breaking down especially after the fall of the Soviet Union because the spectre of communism disciplined capitalist interests in the West. There was a fear that if we went back to the days of the Great Depression there could be a communist revolution, and that had to be avoided at all costs. So they compromised.

At some point in the ‘70s that model becomes very unstable. Partly because labor begins to ask for more and more. People ask for more government spending, trade unions are less willing to accept wages – and then at some point parts of the capitalist class thinks, we’re done with this, we need to go back to a system which works for our interests and everyone else is going to have to suck it up and take whatever the market delivers for them.

I think part of the reason we went down that path, which was very obviously going to be bad for a large part of the population, was because in the short run, through a mixture of tax cuts and credit booms, enough people made gains for that to seem a viable system. But by the 2000s, it was already becoming apparent that that wasn’t going to work for enough people to sustain the legitimacy of the system. And after the financial crash in 2008 it just became ridiculously obvious to everyone.

And yet, rather than use that as an opportunity to reset the system, there’s an attempt to double down. And that is the fatal conceit of this liberal model of capitalism: the banks screwed up and created a massive mess, but the people are going to have to pay for it.

Only today I was reading in the FT about how the Bank of England is still effectively allowing banks to make enormous profits on the interest on their reserves because of the arrangements of quantitative tightening. Which has ended up meaning that the central bank is making losses that the treasury had to make up. So the treasury is sending around 11 billion pounds, which is enough to pay for the entire higher education budget for a year, to the Bank of England so that they can just make their books look like a normal bank. But they’re not a normal bank, they print the money. So it seemed like a very symbolic capitulation of the political class to the needs of the financial sector and the ideology that underpins that part of capitalism. And if you don’t read the FT, you don’t even know it’s happening. So no wonder the people still feel they’re being fleeced by the system, because partly they are.

**It’s the long, slow, painful end of empire. But it will not end. What would you recommend going forward?**

I think the tragedy is that we’re probably never going to return to the happy days of the post-war era where we had a lot of economic growth, and it was equally spread across the population. Given the climate crisis in particular, the ageing of the population. I think the challenge now is even more serious than at the time I wrote the book. In the last five years, the speed of the climate crisis has become more and more apparent. And there’s no way that boosting people’s real levels of consumption is a viable answer to any of these problems.

So we need to find a way to shift people’s expectations about what the economy can do for them. The good news is that we’re alright in the West. On average, we are rich societies, we are much richer than any societies in the past ever were. So we must find some way of integrating everybody into an acceptable standard of living and making them feel included in the way society is governed – we’re not talking about societies where there just isn’t enough to go around. There’s plenty to go around. The problem is we don’t have the institutions that allow people to reach stable agreements and have things equally distributed. The rich are too greedy and they won’t share what they have. On one level, it’s extremely complicated; on the other, it’s extremely simple. We just need to accept that a prosperous society has to integrate everyone inside it, and you have to do that not just through policy but through integrating people in policy process and making them feel like they’re being heard.

A lot of people out there feel angry even though they don’t have any material reason to feel angry. And the material problems are fixable. Capitalism is a system which doesn’t make any pretence to offer social justice. If people see unjust things happening they get mad about it. So capitalism needs to be reconciled with social justice. And it’s not impossible to do – we’ve done it in the past, some countries still manage to do it now. We need to look to those. I’m not saying everything is rosy in Sweden and Denmark, but they’re very rich, and very egalitarian – people may get angry, but they have less reason to be angry than they do if you’re living in the United States and don’t have any healthcare.

\_\_\_

We know how to fix the problems – the problem is getting people to agree to do what we need to do to fix the problems.

\_\_\_

**Do you still stand by the proposal in your book to have the government play a more activist role in politics?**

Yes, absolutely. Neoliberalism has been rolling back social protection for 50 years, 40 years. In Britain and the US we started earlier. We need to start reversing that process. And it will take time but, little by little, there’s a lot that can be done. We don’t need to accept current levels of poverty, there’s enough money to prevent people falling into poverty in Western countries, there’s just no excuse for it. It’s more of a question of political legitimacy.

One of the frustrating things of doing this job is, I started my career thinking how can we fix these problems, and after a while I realised that we know how to fix the problems – the problem is getting people to agree to do what we need to do to fix the problems. And in a way, that’s the hardest part. It’s really not rocket science. Ensuring people have healthcare: it’s not difficult, most of Western Europe has done it. It’s expensive, but it can be done. Why can’t the US agree to something like that? It’s all about the politics. People with power, lots of resources, who don’t want the system to change, are able to convince the people who would benefit from change that it’s not going to help them. So it’s just purely a political problem.

\_\_\_

You can’t try to force feeling and anger out of politics. People are angry, you have to give them a valve for that anger, and if you don’t, like a dam it will end up bursting.

\_\_\_

We know quite a bit about how to design political institutions where people have a say. And if people have a say they might be more likely to pick something that’s good for them, rather than something that’s good for some billionaire. So in some ways I feel optimistic – it’s not a quick fix, but there are solutions to all these problems. But we need to be open to the idea of reforming our politics – that people see what options there are, and hopefully they will choose the kind of options that are good for the majority of people, because in democracies the majority of people are meant to decide.

**We need a synthesis: a bit of the energy and rhetoric of the anti-system in the system?**

Exactly. It doesn’t mean destroying everything: certainly it doesn’t mean destroying democracy. It’s tragic if some people think it’s worth trading their rights for some guy who says he’s going to solve everything. It’s bad enough in principle to accept that trade – if you look at what that means in practice, Donald Trump, one of the worst human beings to ever rise to prominence in human history – it’s too horrific to contemplate. But that should get us thinking of how politics actually works. The technocratic rationalist wanted to design a system where people wouldn’t do anything crazy, and guess what ended up happening. You can’t try to force feeling and anger out of politics. People are angry, you have to give them a valve for that anger, and if you don’t, like a dam it will end up bursting.
