# The myth of dead matter leads to modern disenchantment

*Matter is not "mere matter", it is more magical than you think*

**By James Croft** | *24th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,619 words*

![The myth of dead matter leads to modern disenchantment](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-myth-of-dead-matter-leads-to-modern-disenchantment.webp)

---

*Matter, physical stuff, in contemporary intellectual circles, gets a bad rap. Matter is often described as “mere matter” or as “dead material”. However, philosopher **James Croft** argues this is deeply mistaken. Matter is not mere or dead. It is self-organizing, able to produce life, and makes up the wonders of the world around you. This new picture of matter, Croft argues, has profound consequences for our feelings of disenchantment in the modern world and for the so-called hard problem of consciousness.*

**The misenchantment of matter**

In a studio sits a wizard—or, perhaps, a hobbit. This is poet Malcolm Guite: one part Bilbo Baggins, one part Radagast the Brown. With his white hair, big beard, and sparkling eyes, he emanates warm wisdom. Wearing a red waistcoat and a jacket of brown tweed, he’d look right at home in Bag End. His voice has a pleasing rasp, giving it depth and texture. He leans forward, elbows on the table, clasps his hands as if in prayer, and begins:

*We speak in England of a “quiet revival”...I think there’s a deep yearning for mystery and enchantment. There has to be more than just…the mere concatenation of dead material rattling about in the cosmos…That doesn’t satisfy you for long.*

“The mere concatenation of dead material rattling about in the cosmos.” This is what I call the [misenchantment of matter](https://iai.tv/articles/the-universe-is-neither-clockwork-nor-alive-auid-1982): a description of matter that strips it of its activity, powers, and relations. Each word helps cast the spell:

• Matter is "mere", diminished, nothing but. Significance, gone.  
 • Matter exists in "concatenations", an often negative word, like a random series of unfortunate events. Organization, gone.  
 • Matter is "dead", lacking in vitality and energy. Activity, gone.  
 • Matter is "rattling about", not doing anything interesting or important. Agency, gone.

Even the word “cosmos,” in this context, feels disappointing, a universal junk drawer filled with scattered odds and ends.

Matter is often described this way by those who object to scientific materialism. [Jordan Peterson](https://www.jordanbpeterson.com/political-correctness/new-years-letter/) likes to call matter “[dead](https://youtu.be/DLg2Q0daphE?si=sGW3FmQD026ojxoU&t=8326).” Psychiatrist and philosopher [Iain McGilchrist likes to call it “lumpen”](https://youtu.be/hdhTpkGggK0?si=97_OCmHRM3UN5agU&t=3206)—indeed, he once provided an almost exact parallel to Guite’s description above, [speaking](https://youtu.be/Q16ARIpxlPQ?si=krhVSAGhK11dUPYu&t=2202) of “random lumps of senseless matter bumping into one another.”

This deflationary approach to matter has a long history. In 1693, theologian Richard Bentley [wrote a letter](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/THEM00257) to Isaac Newton saying “‘Tis unconceivable, that inanimate brute matter should (without a divine impression) operate upon & affect other matter without mutual contact”. The thought is instantly recognizable.

[Matter gets a bad rap](https://iai.tv/video/mind-matter-and-everything). That matters.

**A spreading hex**

Back in the studio, Guite continues: he speaks of “bleak reductive materialism” and its inability to offer “purpose, beauty and meaning.” He worries that such views “are telling you that you have no real personhood, no real soul, that you’re just the unwinding of an enzyme.”

So, the misenchantment of matter spreads: it becomes not just a description of the stuff of the cosmos, but an indictment of nonreligious life. Because materialists see matter as dead, lumpen, mere concatenations, it is implied, they cannot make sense of beauty, purpose, person, soul: “you’re just the unwinding of an enzyme.”

This maneuver, too, has deep roots. When Bentley wrote to Newton in 1693, he was not simply satisfying his curiosity: he was about to publish a series of sermons designed to provide “a confutation of atheism,” and hoped Newton would support the description of matter he was relying on as part of his argument. In the sixth of these sermons, Bentley presents an inelegant depiction of “all the Brute Inanimate Matter of the Universe,” contrasting the “Nobility and Excellency” of our immaterial souls with “the poor Atom, sluggish and unactive as it is.” Misenchantment spreads from letter to lectern.

The “deadness” of matter is not a discovery, but a strategy: for hundreds of years this flattened picture has been employed as an apologetic tool against atheist worldviews. If matter is dead, and we are alive, we cannot possibly be made only of matter—that is the claim.

But matter resists its misdescription.

**The nose knows**

Just a few moments after Guite has expressed his view that the world cannot just be material, [his interviewer hands him some tobacco](https://youtu.be/CcYToxtmFHs?si=VokvH-YuycBmdTh4&t=320).

Suddenly, matter seems much more interesting.

Guite puts on his glasses to better examine the tin. He studies the label. Brings the tin to his nose to take a deep sniff. “Oh, this is nice!”

Delighted, he removes a clump and holds it between his fingers. “This is what we call a flake tobacco,” he rhapsodizes. “You’ve gotta rub it in your fingers…so it’s more combustible.” His whole face comes alive. “That’s part of the whole tactile experience…In a world where…everyone’s doomscrolling, we’re being distracted, we’re abstracting ourselves out…But a *pipe*, you’re rubbing the tobacco, you’re packing the pipe in a certain way, you’re lighting it more than once, you’re *centered*, you’re doing something bodily.”

He mimes each movement as he describes it, cradling the pipe like it’s his precious. “But paradoxically…once you’re smoking [you’re] opening your mind up, setting it free for conversation.”

This is an extraordinary inversion. Matter is suddenly luminous, vibrant, full of meaning. The very tactility of tobacco and pipe—smelling the scent, rubbing the leaves, packing the bowl, lighting the flame, its insistent *matterliness*—is precisely what gives it value. It opens up his mind.

Guite’s nose knows what his philosophy seems to have forgotten: matter is not a “mere concatenation of dead material rattling about in the cosmos.” An atom is not “sluggish and unactive.” As fire breaks down the tobacco’s enzymes, and the resultant aerosols enter his blood, his enzymes begin to work on them, and in that winding and unwinding is the whole delight.

The misenchantment of matter is thus unmasked: it is based on misdescription. The terms used to describe matter by its detractors are either literally false or metaphorically misleading. Matter is not “concatenated” but stunningly self-organizing. Most matter has never been alive, but it is not metaphorically “dead”—it is strikingly “lively.” It does not exist in “random lumps,” but in profound relational networks exhibiting extraordinary connectivity. The misdescription is a rhetorical trick, a strawman to burden materialists with, smuggling in the desired metaphysical outcome.

Now we can expect an objection: “Wait a minute!” the misenchanters will say, “It is not *we* who believe that matter is dead—[it is the materialists](https://iai.tv/video/iain-mcgilchrist-on-why-the-mind-is-not-a-machine)! They gave us this picture of matter, and we are just making them own it!”

There’s not nothing to this response: some materialists genuinely do offer deflationary accounts of matter, and they are wrong to do so. But it is simply false that most materialists do so, let alone all. Consider, from this [discussion between Iain McGilchrist and Anil Seth](https://youtu.be/hdhTpkGggK0?si=WqBwQtDQsTJb0KOg&t=3206):

*McGilchrist: With consciousness we are talking about something altogether new, completely unpredictable from lumpen matter…*

*Seth: I think you’re being quite pejorative in your language here: you’re saying “brute emergence from lumpen matter.” It’s those two words—“brute” and “lumpen”—that are doing a lot of work.*

Seth catches the misenchantment in real time, and here it is the materialist who defends matter’s honor.

Remember Bentley’s letter to Newton? [Newton responded](https://iai.tv/articles/we-dont-understand-matter-any-better-than-mind-auid-3065), partly echoing the language of “inanimate brute matter” Bentley offered him. But he does not speak this way when left to his own devices. In his *Opticks*, Newton presents a world of “small Particles of Bodies” with “Powers, Virtues, or Forces”. Newton saw the world Guite’s pipe reveals, a world of fire and fluidity, elasticity and ebullition, viscosity and vortex.

**Breaking the spell**

“Fine,” the anti-materialist may then respond, “matter is not dead and lumpen even on a materialist view—but it still needs something extra to become mind!”

But once we begin to describe matter more accurately, is that so obvious?

In the Bentley/Newton exchange, Bentley believed matter could not affect other matter “without mutual contact”, and that the puzzle of gravity would therefore be a safehouse for the spirit. Newton was less sure: he wrote in [his response](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/THEM00258), “Gravity must be caused by an agent... whether this agent be material or immaterial is a question I have left to the consideration of my readers.” Science smiled on Newton’s restraint over Bentley’s certainty: when, more than 200 years later, Einstein redescribed gravity as the effect of curvature of space caused by matter, the material picture was not defeated, but reaffirmed. What it was once “unconceivable” for “brute matter” to do, it did. We just needed to shift our way of seeing.

Today, the holdout problem giving hope to those who wish to avoid a materialistic picture of reality is the development of mind. Instead of incredulously asking how “brute matter” can give rise to gravity, matter-skeptics now ask how it can give rise to consciousness.

We do not yet know—the “[hard problem](https://iai.tv/articles/panpsychism-does-not-solve-the-hard-problem-of-consciousness-auid-3630)” is genuinely hard, and I do not claim we’ve solved it. Instead, I seek to trouble the assumption behind the problem: that matter is “brute” at all. We’ve seen that matter is fecund, generative, self-organizing, motive. So, let’s pose the question anew:

“How can fecund, generative, self-organizing, motive material create minds?”

That, it seems to me, is not an obviously absurd question. Perhaps the fact that a pipe can “open our mind up” is not a “paradox” at all—perhaps it reveals the deep connection between the two.

Somewhere, you are reading this text. On a phone, a tablet, a computer screen. Wherever you are, you are surrounded by matter. I am calling you to a quiet revival—a materialist one. Touch the screen on which you’re reading this. Touch it now. Feel it beneath your fingers. As you go about your day, be like Malcolm Guite with his pipe: try to love matter. Feel it. Smell it. Taste it. Hear it. Then ask yourself a simple question:

Does it seem dead to you?
