# AI isn't a dual-use technology, it is inherently violent

*AI erases the line between civilian and military life*

**By Thomas Christian Bächle** | *30th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 18 mins* | *3,669 words*

![AI isn't a dual-use technology, it is inherently violent](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Ai-is-inherently-violent.webp)

*When the Pentagon branded Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei “a liar with a god complex” over fears that his company’s AI could be used for weapons and surveillance, it exposed a deeper truth: the boundary between civilian and military technology no longer exists. The same systems that power translation, logistics, and digital assistants can just as easily identify targets or manipulate populations. **Thomas Christian Bächle** and **Jascha Bareis** argue that today’s AI is not simply “dual use” — it is inherently violent in design. Adaptive, autonomous, and globally networked, these machines fuse daily life with geopolitics, making peace itself a fading abstraction.*

Drones have become an uncanny threat—not least in the wake of the cost of human life and the degrees of suffering and destruction they have inflicted in Russia’s war on Ukraine. In many European countries they have been sighted near critical infrastructure or military sites, either used for reconnaissance or sabotage, at times causing major disruptions in civilian air travel. Drones unsettle a population that is fearful and weary of the brutality of war at their doorstep. They have become a major element to what is labelled *hybrid* warfare, fought beyond the conventional ways of violence.

But this is not the whole picture. For years, drones have also been envisioned as a technology that bears the potential of bringing about major changes for the better: more efficient disaster relief, medical supply chains reaching even the remotest areas, optimized logistics or transportation. Drones also introduced a new visual – bird’s-eye – aesthetic of how to see the world.

This ambiguity is characteristic of *any* technology. It is never distinct from social processes; technology shapes and is being shaped by them—both sides are inextricably linked with each other. Drones are no different in this regard. What makes them currently stand out, however, is that they are emblematic of what elsewhere we have called [the realities of autonomous weapons](http://autonomous-weapons-book.com): they symbolize a complex mix of meaning—a diffuse idea of destructive potential combined with a presumed human-like agency and artificial intelligence (AI). These realities blend existing military technologies with visions of their envisioned future capabilities, and in so doing point to the interplay between fact and fiction, actual developments and creative imagination.

This piece argues that recent technological developments always carry within themselves latent abilities to inflict harm, violence, and aggression. Yes, technology has certainly never been neutral; and, yes, these technologies also bear the potential to serve a benevolent purpose in a society, contributing for example to medicine, research, industry, or education. But still, digital, networked, and AI-enabled technologies bear a particularly harmful and violent potential for at least three reasons.

The first concerns the universality of these technologies, that can be used for a multitude of purposes that are not all predictable. AI chatbots such as Chat GPT, for example, can be used as information retrieval tools, as assistants, or as functional substitutes for interpersonal relationships. These open functional properties of [AI applications have long found their way into war technologies](https://thebulletin.org/2023/08/war-is-messy-ai-cant-handle-it/#post-heading).

The second has to do with the high degrees of machine autonomy that enables the execution of violence to be split into automated steps of identifying, tracking, or engaging enemies that no longer require human involvement. AI opens the gates to indeterminately multi-purpose, adaptive and autonomous qualities that make it distinct from preceding technologies, even to the point of substituting human agency.

The third relates to the changing nature of violence itself: a society that relies so heavily on digital communication and infrastructures becomes increasingly vulnerable. This vulnerability ranges from physical attacks on digital networks that are essential for transportation, health services, electricity or water supply to the disruption of financial systems or exploitation of sensitive data. The vulnerability is also political and psychological through means of manipulation that can target public opinion or individuals, with ‘deep fake’ content as one of the better-known examples.

As a consequence, the distinction between military and civilian is becoming increasingly blurry, exceeding a simple continuation of the long-standing military-civilian entanglement. In general terms, weapons can be characterised by a combination of both *functionality of the underlying technological principles* and the *context of their employment* – as we show below, both are changing rapidly. This also means that many of the terms and concepts used in regulatory, ethical or political discourses are eroding. For example, as a common descriptor, a technology’s potential for violence has been labelled, also [legally](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32009R0428), as *dual use*, indicating that it can be employed for both civilian and military purposes. While this term has always been messy and imperfect, we argue that in today’s technological age of AI and connectivity, the distinction between military and civilian purposes has become utterly untenable, making this labelanalytically ineffectual. Digital technologies are bound—even designed—to defy regulatory and ethical stances. For these reasons, we criticise that established vocabulary such as "dual use" is still normatively used in regulatory, legal, ethical discourses – even though it is an empty label, referencing outdated theoretical categories of the war and peace, military and civilian or state and private binaries. Military violence is no longer the prerogative of nation states that were either at peace or at war – it has been succeeded by hybrid types of violence and complex networks of agency.

What we propose is a greater awareness of the violent dangers underlying even seemingly innocuous AI uses. At the same time, by acknowledging these current dangers and likely future realities of warfare facing international conflict, we claim that the realistic goal can only lie in a geopolitical equilibrium of attack and defense, of force and counter-force. This claim deliberately goes against the naivety that too often finds expression in the public and political arena. A public debate that narrows in on a notion of “killer robots” is misguided and ineffective, as the violent potential of military AI lies in the more subtle ways in which violence is automated and executed – it is not a future risk, but a current and consequential practice. Clinging to political rhetoric such as the “dual use” label perpetuates a neat distinction of civilian/military that represents an outdated sociotechnical reality and glosses over actual issues in the arena of political accountability for technological developments in the military.

Beyond what we suggest here, we need to accept: In the current geopolitical state of affairs there is *no* realistic path to an end goal of world peace, an effective regulatory framework, or compliance with universal ethical values.

**AI and warfare: the old concepts of war have become obsolete**

The dual use label only makes sense if we can maintain the distinction between civilian and military applications in the first place. Making this distinction was more easily possible in the age of industrial-type warfare, with nation states waging war against each other, using mechanical and often single-purpose technologies such as rifles or tanks. One of the first dual-use cases that sparked controversy was the mass production of chemicals during the First World War. The development of procedures such as the “[Haber-Bosch process](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-51664-6_2)” were the cornerstone of both modern fertilization techniques *and* industrially produced explosives or chemical weapons on the battlefield. Entering the accelerating pace of technological modernity in the 20th century, the military/civilian distinction has since become increasingly blurry, as the two ways of characterizing the “use” of weapons—the *functionality* of the underlying technological principles, and their *context* of employment—are shifting rapidly.

Regarding *functionality*, the post-mechanical era of electronic computation that began in the 1940s heralded massive developments in automation and machine autonomy, currently being further advanced with AI. This contrasts sharply with previous styles of warfare that were necessarily carried forward by human agents who used force and “single use” technologies to achieve their goals. Regarding the *contexts of employment*, the clear-cut duality of peace and war characteristic of modernity (its end roughly coinciding with the end of WWII) has largely dissolved into a type of warfare that is characterized as hybrid: applying irregular and indirect forms of conflict, such as political, economic, cyber, or communication means, as well as integrating the possibilities of AI-enabled automation. Additionally, state actors are no longer the only ones playing in this type of warfare—private (military) companies, groups of mercenaries, terrorists, and cyber criminals are also driving it forward.

As a consequence of this newly indefinite character of both function and context, the “dual use” label is losing its relevance in regulatory, legal, and ethical discourses. Simply put*:* AI-based weapons utilized in hybrid warfare are no longer captured by the theoretical categories of an obsolete age of war/peace, military/civilian, or state/private binaries.

This has significant repercussions for research and development, education, policy-making, and legal frameworks because *any* AI-enabled, digital, and networked technology now may bring about a militarized or violent use, unforeseeable before the fact. This challenge is magnified by the rapid development and dissemination of such technologies, which can no longer be controlled. In short, the *intended* uses of a technology and the ways it is *actually* used—or appropriated—can now and more than ever before be two very different things. A widely-discussed example of this are home-installed [3-D printers capable of easily producing firearms such as rifles or pistols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D-printed_firearm).

The computer and the underlying technique of computation are the essential foundation for this broader shift. Computational technology is not developed for a single purpose (i.e., civilian or military); rather, mathematical scripts are utilized to solve problems generally. As a medium, the electronic computer relies on data and algorithms as *universal* representations of the world. Electronic computation and computer interfaces such as screens also mark a major watershed moment, as they historically incorporate all preceding technologies, defying a narrow single-purpose understanding of their uses and effects. Crucially, the rise of algorithmic processing has reinforced the idea that we can solve problems and tasks with the help of programs that run automatically, without the necessity for human agency.

What today is commonly referred to as AI adds another massive boost to automation, due in part to its idealisation as machine autonomy. While algorithmic logic allowed for the processing of scripts that were still to a large degree predetermined, machine autonomy—and its machine-learning AI component—is itself able to determine patterns from fuzzy data and adapt. Autonomous systems are seen as capable of finding their own routes and means to a goal. This is exactly what makes autonomous *weapon* systems such a controversial development.

The autonomy of these systems raises a barrage of critical questions: is there still a human involved in the selection and engagement of targets? Who is ultimately responsible? At what part of the complex human/machine dynamic are decisions made? And can we track how and why they come to pass? As part of a hybrid warfare, these technologies can be applied in unforeseeable, spontaneous, and improvised ways—evidenced in Ukraine, where seemingly “old” technologies such as wooden weapons or fiberglass are used alongside state-of-the-art AI weapons.

The universal application potential of computational autonomy combined with the vagueness of hybrid warfare make a wide range of technologies inherently “dual use”: while originally envisioned for civilian application, they are easily appropriated for military (or paramilitary) purposes. While their violent use and potential is thus inherent, nothing is solved by merely labelling the technology good or evil: conformity with ethical, social, or legal norms, rules, or frameworks cannot be derived from simply analysing intended or innocent functionality. What is crucial are the broader potentials, imaginaries, and contexts of conflict, which we turn to next.

**Automated weapon systems: AI and the promise of orderly warfare**

AI and automation technologies have ultimately resulted in what is termed *autonomous weapon systems* in war settings. These developments reflect the age-old attempt to reduce the complexity of war while maximizing the idea of control in the face of its ultimate chaos. AI and datafication are the latest iteration of this long-standing normative phantasma of control. The promises they make are of a heightened, seemingly objective knowledge achieved through the maximization of data and automated decision-making at high speed. The imagined promise of AI is to gain control by counter-balancing human weaknesses and predicting the future.

Today’s imaginations of warfare technologies cannot be understood without their conceptual precursors. The 1990s paradigm of network-centric warfare reflects the nascent technologies of digital and networked communication. A few years later, the post 9/11 War on Terror was also partly based on the notion of identifying terrorists with surgical precision, even before an act of terror was committed, combined with the use of “clean” strikes from above via drones.

Today’s visions of warfare—expectedly—mirror developments in AI, automation, and the platform-based economy. Against this background, autonomous weapon systems are usually defined as “weapons that select and apply force to targets without human intervention“ ([International Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons), [US Department of Defense Direction 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). What usually gets the most attention is the “without human intervention” part, which is normally compensated for by keeping a “human in the loop” in decision-making, or emphasizing the role of "appropriate human judgement.”

The “select and apply force” part, on the other hand, is tied to a practice that has much wider implications, namely *targeting*. This automation of identifying and selecting targets—via pattern recognition in databases—combines two processes that used to be both distinct and a mainly human prerogative: [meaning-making and decision-making](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262552349/the-world-according-to-military-targeting/). The *creation* of targets is the epistemic element of automation, which precedes the automated decision for and execution of an attack. Even though the automated process implies otherwise, targeting itself is far from neutral. It is reflective of mission goals and the value-laden parameters (such as: “enemies” or “innocent civilians”?) that are necessary when defining data as meaningful or relevant.

This combination of surveillance, optimization, speed, and prediction of future scenarios may provide some sense of threat reduction and order against chaos—yet it is also the expression of capitalist thinking in the warfare domain: targeting produces a probabilistic world of risk assessment and optimized, cost-effective action. A human is reduced to a profile and labelled with a score, measured and ranked in a system they cannot control. The violation of the individual starts with this epistemic step.

Drones, as introduced above, starkly remind us of the step that follows: the one of physical destruction, the material, corporeal, and kinetic elements of war. Drone reconnaissance and warfare are synonymous with the potentiality to be killed anywhere at any time, pointing to a liquidity of geopolitical violence. The new indefiniteness of the (formally dual use) categories of civilian/military and war/peace in today’s liquid scenarios additionally leads to the categorical dissolution of combatant/non-combatant and legitimate/illegitimate target.

We should also keep in mind that this state of affairs is heightened by the reality of current conflicts, which shows the blatant de facto international disregard for legal or ethical rules and norms. This disregard has become the new normal and can currently be witnessed in Ukraine, Sudan, China, Israel and Gaza, but also within the EU and the US, among many others. The military violence witnessed today provides plenty of evidence that legal frameworks have lost their power to make much of a difference. We are left to wonder if this disregard for normative categories is in any way facilitated by the slipperiness of the technologies being used, in that the move away from traditional clearly demarcated instruments of war has brought on a kind of parallel free-for-all in terms of ethics and norms.

**Psychological warfare and the effects of imagination**

An important constitutive element of the realities of AI-enabled warfare and autonomous weapons are powerful imaginaries on what these technologies can, cannot, or should not do. Ideas such as the capacity for control unhindered by human error sit within the historical phantasma of clean, just, legal, and orderly warfare. With autonomous and AI-based weapons this regulatory phantasma is taken to an extreme: The weapon itself is imagined as “knowing” how to act ethically and in accordance with existing legal frameworks.

What makes this picture even more complex is that, as part of hybrid warfare, these imaginaries are exploited in the global terrain as a vehicle of *political communication* and deterrence, serving to hint at and express a culture of growing violence and threat in the guise of more civilian displays.

[China, for example, is known for performing swarming capabilities of drones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJxM44npcJo) to deliberately [communicate to allies and adversaries its technological capabilities](https://doi.org/10.51952/9781529237191.ch016). Mastering complex interaction and coordination between thousands of AI-enabled drones is framed as an indicator of technological advancement, power, and national clout for the purpose of deterrence. In this case, the threat of violence remains only in the realm of *possibility*: suggestive as-if scenarios point to a potential future of military escalation, but without explicating the threat. From this perspective, technology is transformed into a message, delivered via tropes of greatness, hegemony, competitiveness, or innovation.

This form of psychologically-enforced threat is nourished by new forms of media: the violent conflicts in Ukraine and Gaza, with graphic images of human suffering, have highlighted the ways in which social media publics are targeted in propaganda wars, while public authorities try to engineer opinions in a desired fashion. These new media environments also enable first-person accounts, allowing soldiers, terrorists, and civilians to live-stream their own battleground reality. Media forms themselves co-create the realities of warfare, often in a fuzzy overlap of perspectives and across different media spheres, from news websites to TikTok reels.

One of the audiences targeted are allies and adversaries in the geopolitical realm; the other is the domestic population itself, to whom governments are keen to showcase national technological achievements. Innovative force is built as an attempt to build political consensus and gain legitimacy.

Drones and swarming play a particularly powerful role in this type of psychological warfare. In military contexts swarms are [defined](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/journals/military-review/online-exclusive/2025-ole/defining-swarm/) as an “assembly that operates cooperatively to commands from a single operator in charge through a common architecture.*”* This single operator can be a soldier but, and this is what this definition conceals, commands can also be delegated to AI-based systems. Swarming capabilities are automated and do not necessitate human control and decision-making.

The reason why swarms have such a strong effect in psychological warfare is not just limited to the technological capabilities they display. Swarms are a fascinating and uncanny occurrence: we cannot explain, for instance, how gigantic fish collectives roaming the oceans work. Where and how is the formation controlled and coordinated? There is a long cultural history of the swarm trope. The biblical accounts of locust swarms symbolizes an all-encompassing threat, an army of intruders, divine punishment.

Hitchcock’s *The Birds* is an example of how the visual evocation of this ancient psychologically potent symbolism works as an iconographic threat: crows, swifts, and seagulls unite in an uncanny, incomprehensible way to hunt down humans. The potential threat of being attacked anywhere, at any time, is characteristic of this motif, symbolizing the merging of psychological and imaginary violence, spanning over time and space. Swarming phenomena found in nature—birds, insects, fish—even [serve as the blueprint for military companies.](https://www.swarm-biotactics.com/)

**Conclusion**

As we have seen, many of the categorical certitudes of the binaries military/civilian, war/peace, and state/private are dissolving with AI and the current complex realities of warfare. This categorical uncertainty crucially has implications on the legal and ethical evaluation of technologies that can hardly be labelled in an unambiguous fashion due to the violence they have the potential to spawn.

Violence is not exclusively rooted in the brutal nature of war or in the [anatomy of human destructiveness](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780805016048/theanatomyofhumandestructiveness/)—but also in the innate functioning and the affordances of technology. AI presents a specific functionality, including high-velocity processing, task automation, digital reductionism into algorithmic scripts, and scaling with easy data reproduction. This naturally nurtures certain patterns of agency, mainly by recalibrating the role of the human in the selection of targets and decision-making, as well as the trust that is projected onto the assumed accuracy and objectivity of technical systems.

As we have argued, the “dual use” categorization of technology into civilian or military uses has become implausible, conceptually limiting, and also normatively questionable. It ultimately leads to regulators missing part of the picture, as too little attention is given to the “off-label” uses of AI-enabled processing and machine autonomy. While there are massive ongoing regulatory efforts to construct a vision of “ethical” AI with concepts such as transparency, fairness, or robustness (e.g. [the EU AI act](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/en/article/20230601STO93804/eu-ai-act-first-regulation-on-artificial-intelligence)), too little attention is given to the environments that regulate and activate the *violent* *potentials*and afterlives of these technologies. Legal and ethical frameworks become analytically and normatively void when the political system that possesses and employs such powerful technologies does not respect human rights or desired values in the first place. There is no current effective global regulation on AI, in neither civil nor military domains.

The new goal in a reality that is characterized by a global landscape of rivalry, hybrid warfare, and flexible AI-enabled weapons can no longer be captured by a commonly idealised optimum called peace, but rather an equilibrium. To borrow an analogy from the field of cybersecurity: The objective there is never absolute security; rather, the constant rivalrous dynamic between attacker and defender, malicious software and counter-measures, resembles more of a constant chase. The desired state is therefore—realistically—never set on absolute security or peace, but on maintaining this equilibrium between attack and defensive measures.

In conclusion, instead of aiming for a globally enforced treaty that regulates military AI and autonomous weapons (an endeavor that has proven pointless, without any result for years), steps must be taken incrementally, acknowledging the competitive global landscape. Any attack provokes a more efficient defense; any massive destruction or human tragedy leads to more effective preemptive measures to anticipate risks. It may well be the case that only after acknowledging this grim state of affairs the path to a less value-laden and gridlocked approach will become discernible. While this may be mistaken for a defeatist position, it is the pragmatic—and currently only realistic—way forward.
