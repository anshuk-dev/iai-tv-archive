# History is never true, but it can be justified

*How historians can do without truth*

**By Jouni-Matti Kuukkanen** | *17th December 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,033 words*

![History is never true, but it can be justified](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/History-is-never-true-but-it-can-approach-reality2.webp)

---

*History is not a collection of facts. Historians writing about, say, the French Revolution inevitably make it into a story, creating meaning and projecting their prejudices. These stories, argues Jouni-Matti Kuukkanen, are neither true nor false: they are more like artworks, composed of meanings, morals and metaphors. But then why should we prefer one story over another? Must we just pick whichever seems most beautiful, or best accords with our prejudices? Kuukanen argues that we can do better: we can pick between stories in a rational way, once we accept that rationality doesn’t require truth.*

*1.* *Is history closer to literature than to science?: the “narrativist insight”*

What is most interesting in history? What kind of historical knowledge is most significant? Should we focus on so-called facts, such as that Napoleon was born in 1769 in Corsica? While individual facts and details are important, we surely care more about giving meaning to the past and making sense of it. We seek interpretations that describe patterns or trajectories in history, perhaps drawing out lessons from it. What I call the “narrativist insight” says that these synthesizing interpretations are historians’ genuine and most important knowledge contributions (see my [*Postnarrativist Philosophy of Historiography*](https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/9781349488810?gC=qo4BgQu8OL)).

Fortunately, historical writing is full of such meaning-making views. To mention just a few examples, the period from the mid-1950s to the early 1960s has often been described as “the Thaw” in the history of the Soviet Union. It signifies the era of de-Stalinization initiated by Nikita Khrushchev, which is said to have led to liberalization and the “warming” of the cultural climate. Christopher Clark suggested in his book [*The* *Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914*](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/55884/the-sleepwalkers-by-clark-christopher/9780141027821)that the great powers of Europe were “sleepwalkers, watchful but unseeing, haunted by dreams yet blind to the reality of the horror they were about to bring into the world” (p. 562). This is his explanation of the First World War. Clark’s take is very different, for example, from Fritz Fischer’s in his book *Germany’s Aims in the First World War* (1967), which understood the war as a result of Germany’s premeditated struggle for power. Historical scholarship on the Great War is particularly rich in rival interpretations. It would be easy to go on.

The narrativist insight in its original formulation – most closely associated with the writings of Hayden White and the early Frank Ankersmit – identifies *narrative* as the pivotal unit of historiography. The idea is that historical knowledge is not made of bits and pieces of historical phenomena, but is instead made of the selection and organization of them into a narrative. Furthermore, the same evidence and set of facts can produce different narratives. A singular fact that Napoleon conquered much of Europe can be incorporated into a narrative of Napoleon as a great oppressor or as a liberator, for example.

Historical knowledge is therefore divided into two levels: the lower level consists of facts, traces and details of the past, and the higher level of narratives and interpretations that integrate them. However, narrativists deny that there are any rational “translation rules” that could be used to translate the lower-level entities into meaningful narratives. In fact, the order is the other way round: the narrative gives shape to the facts. White’s view is that every historical narrative necessarily works with a certain sort of plot: romance, comedy, tragedy or satire. For example, the historian Jules Michelet constructed the French Revolution as a drama of Romantic transcendence, while Alexis de Tocqueville constructed it as an ironic tragedy.

Furthermore, the narrativists argued for a shift in how history writing should be seen. Instead of trying to fit history in the mold of science and scientific explanation, as had many analytical philosophers of history, such as Carl Gustaf Hempel, they compare it to literature. After all, historians write texts, using various kinds of literary techniques and tropes in crafting them.

The unfortunate consequence is that it becomes hard to see why we should rationally prefer one narrative over another as a source of historical knowledge. As White put it: “When it comes to apprehending the historical record, there are no grounds to be found in the historical record itself for preferring one way of construing its meaning over another” (1987: 75). In the narrativist framework, historians’ narratives and other texts can only be evaluated by way of moral and aesthetic standards.

*2.* *Beyond narrativism: how to avoid relativism*

My postnarrativism accepts the narrativist insight but goes beyond it in two key respects. First, according to postnarrativism, the main goal of professional historians is not the production of narratives about the past, as if they were simply storytellers. Instead, historians argue for a specific view or views about the past in and through their books. Therefore, one should approach books of history by asking what theses are proposed in them, and how rationally persuasive they are. Second, postnarrativism appreciates that these textually presented theses about the past are knowledge, and should be evaluated as such. It is absurd to think that just any interpretation “goes,” epistemologically, to paraphrase Paul Feyerabend’s famous slogan. A reasonable philosophy of history does not entail relativism.

Postnarrativism therefore tries to develop tools for the evaluation of historians’ texts. However, this is philosophically challenging. The philosophical message of narrativism is anti-representationalist. The archetypical representationalist idea is that our representations are like [mirrors of reality](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691178158/philosophy-and-the-mirror-of-nature?srsltid=AfmBOopwqxPogRjztMdvKgwBHWCQUpsnmKTFXrJbT5TNtjeNmPwxF_bE), as Richard Rorty reminded us. Unfortunately for the representationalist, the pivotal knowledge contributions of historians are literary products composed of meanings, morals, tropes, and are often metaphorical. These cannot possibly be conceived of as representing a reality that is given, or as having counterparts in the past independent of historians and their cultures.

For example, Christopher Clark’s book *Sleepwalkers* does not mirror the past but is a literary product with hundreds of pages of ordered sentences and types of argumentation. The great powers did not literally sleepwalk to the war. Or consider Eric Hobsbawm’s book *The Age of Extremes: The Short Twentieth Century 1914-1991*. It would be absurd to suggest that the century was in fact short or that this “short century” is prefigured in the past objectively without Hobsbawm’s contribution. Historians are not playing the matching game or holding a mirror to the past.

Nevertheless, Hobsbawm’s “short century,” the image of Khruschev’s period as “the Thaw,” seeing the great powers of Europe sleepwalking to a catastrophe, or perhaps understanding the First World War as a result of Germany’s premeditated campaign, may all be excellent characterizations of their respective topics. The question is how good they are and how to judge this.

When there are several accounts of the same topic in history, they cannot all be equally good depictions, so long as we wish to avoid relativism. My own thinking was awakened many years ago while listening to a debate about the history of Estonia. One interlocutor argued that the annexation of Estonia and its integration into the Soviet Union in the 1940s was nothing but a violent abduction, while another reasoned that it was more like a seduction in which the seduced partner was also willing. It would be difficult to understand either of these as mirror-like representations of the past. Yet, they cannot both be equally correct. There must be ways to evaluate these historical narratives without the presumption of absolute correctness.

Postnarrativism avoids succumbing to either nihilistic relativism, according to which anything goes, or to representational absolutism, according to which one and only one account is absolutely correct. It offers three kinds of tools for comparing and ranking historians’ narratives: *epistemological*, *rhetorical* and *discursive*.

The first refers to so-called epistemic values, which can be summarized as exemplification, coherence, comprehensiveness, scope and originality. These cannot be described in detail here. The thumb rule is that a historian’s account should attempt to maximize these, although it is important to realize that trade-offs between different values must be made too. The second refers to the argumentative qualities of a historian’s book. It probes how rationally persuasive a historian’s book is for the thesis or theses it argues for. Are all its components and claims well-connected? Do they support its thesis? The third dimension refers to the wider and external context of the book’s thesis. The discourse about the First World War, for example, forms a specific argumentative context. Any new view must take the existing discourse into account, and situate the historian’s proposal within it, explicating what it accepts and what it corrects in previous views. The three dimensions contribute overall to the cognitive justification of history-writing.

*3.* *Rationality without truth or representation*

Postnarrativism departs from more traditional approaches in analytical philosophy, especially epistemology. It is not only anti-representational but it also discounts the idea that historians’ texts can be true or false. What does this mean? And why is this the case? It is intuitive to understand truth as correspondence between an objective mind-independent reality and a statement, theory or even narrative. However, we have seen that historians’ theses are not copies, or re-presentations, of the past. No match can be expected between the two because structures, patterns and meaning are not given from out there, but are imposed by the historian. It is better to talk about presentations without the “re”-suffix, because historians’ theses are colligations and constructions of the historical record, providing some kind of meaningful structure on the past. We can then say that there is no truth-functional computation from the past to the historian’s text, which would pair the components or wholes of the two, item by item, as required by the correspondence theory of truth.

\_\_\_

In postnarrativism, an account can be rationally justified without being true.

\_\_\_

Yet, historians’ accounts are assessable through the three evaluative dimensions above, which means that we can regard some specific historical account as justified and, in any case, more justified than and preferable to some others. This breaks with the tradition of epistemology, which typically assumes that justification must positively correlate with truth. In postnarrativism, an account can be rationally justified without being true.

*4.* *What truth might be*

Of course, it is possible to rethink what being true or truth consists in. In my forthcoming book, *Doing, Knowing and Getting it Right: Methodologism as Pragmatism*, I propose a deflationist understanding of truth. This says that while “truth” and “true” can be used in conversation, they should not be understood as entailing any metaphysical theory of truth, such as the correspondence theory or other traditional theories of truth. I follow here a “Cambridge pragmatist,” Frank Ramsey, who memorably expressed the idea as follows: to assert that “it is true that p” is (in effect) equivalent to just asserting that “p.” To say “it is true that Caesar was murdered” means no more than that Caesar was murdered ([Ramsey 2013](https://academic.oup.com/aristoteliansupp/article/7/1/153/1779950?login=false)).

Postnarrativism entails a specific idea of what knowing in historiography is: it is argumentative practice. “Methodologism” in my forthcoming book develops this idea in a more general form. It says that knowing in general – including in the sciences – is *doing correctly*. In historiography, it is the situation in which a historian has studied all the relevant literature, consulted all the appropriate archival material, employed the best historiographical and investigative methods, and reasons and argues cogently for a specific view in her text. Furthermore, she is also able to defend her view, if and when challenged. The postnarrativist philosophy of historiography is a special case of the pragmatist-methodologist account of knowing.
