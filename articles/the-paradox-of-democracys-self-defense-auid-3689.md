# The paradox of democracy’s self-defense

*Too little too late to fight illiberal democracy*

**By Giovanni Capoccia** | *29th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,232 words*

![The paradox of democracy’s self-defense](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-paradox-of-democracys-self-defense.webp)

---

*Liberal democracies around the world are under threat. Oxford Politics Professor **Giovanni Capoccia** argues that there’s a paradox that prevents efforts to defend them. Illiberal movements are easiest to stop early, when they’re small and their true intentions are hidden. But propaganda and subtle erosion prevent democratic forces from acting quickly, as they don’t realize the threat until it’s too late. By the time the danger is undeniable, the tools to fight back become politically impossible to use. Democrats must now prepare for a challenging war of position and use every means at their disposal to fight back.*

In a way that seemed unimaginable only a generation ago, the worldwide rise of illiberal movements is now threatening liberal democracies everywhere, including those once universally considered stable and consolidated. Like the totalitarian parties of the 1930s, today’s illiberal parties exploit liberal democracy’s rights and freedoms to subvert it. Illiberal forces have gained power through elections in the United States, Turkey, India, Hungary, and Poland, among others, where they have eroded constitutional checks on executive power, weakened judicial independence, curtailed media freedom, and limited the right to public protest. In other countries, including several in Western Europe, rising illiberal oppositions seek to emulate them. What can pro-democratic forces do to counter the rise of illiberal parties and movements that threaten to turn liberal democracy into an illiberal regime?

Standard answers centered on “addressing the social grievances that fuel extremism” are no longer sufficient. To be sure, stimulating economic growth, reducing unemployment and inequality, fostering social mobility, and strengthening civic education remain important. However, policies targeting these goals can yield results only in the long term. Even if democrats had enacted them at the right time—for example, to address the social strains caused by the 2007-08 financial crisis—illiberal forces rose too rapidly over the last two decades for these policies alone to contain them. Where democrats primarily failed was in enacting measures to counter illiberal forces *in the short term*. Doing so would have bought them the time necessary to regroup and address the roots of discontent, while putting in place a systematic strategy of democratic defense.

**The paradox**

The democrats’ failure can in good part be attributed to a paradox they face. When facing a newly emerging illiberal opposition, or an executive that unexpectedly starts eroding liberal democracy, pro-democratic forces confront a *paradox* relatingto the timing of their intervention. In principle, democrats can act quickly, and when they do, they can typically rely on a wide range of countermeasures.

When a newly installed executive begins implementing reforms that curb checks and balances, democratic oppositions can react immediately—by filibustering in parliament, litigating in court, and taking to the streets to protest. When liberal-democratic parties (of either the right or the left) govern, and a new extremist opposition emerges, democratic majorities can mobilize civil society against them—for example, by organizing counterdemonstrations and boycotts. They can also draw on the tradition of “militant democracy” (a liberal democracy that actively fights extremist dissent) to target them with formal and informal restrictions.

In other words, if they act early, democrats typically have plenty of arrows in their quiver, which makes their success in countering illiberals more likely. However, their quick response is typically hindered by *uncertainty*. Often, democrats do not recognize a new illiberal challenge right away, and illiberals themselves actively obfuscate their real intentions. When, finally, their actions make the extent of the threat clear, most countermeasures are no longer viable, and success becomes elusive.

**Facing democratic erosion**

I’ll begin by considering the case of a newly elected executive that initiates a process of “democratic backsliding,” and will then consider the case of a newly emerging illiberal opposition party. In the former case, democratic opposition parties must confront a newly elected executive that, against expectations, starts curbing constitutional counterpowers and curtailing the rights of the opposition and individual citizens.

Often, the true nature of these initiatives is difficult to detect, not only for citizens and neutral observers, but also for many democratic politicians. First, the parties that enact them—ranging from the US Republicans to Likud, from Orban’s Fidesz to the Polish PiS—were not considered illiberal, but part of mainstream democratic politics, before they took power. Furthermore, the reforms that they enact are often formally *legal*. In some cases, they operate in the gray area of “extra-legality,” such as when an elected President unilaterally convenes a Constituent Assembly, as Hugo Chavez did in Venezuela and Rafael Correa did in Ecuador. However, they rarely violate the letter of the law openly. Respect for formal procedures matters, as it induces key constituencies such as moderate electors, civil servants, the military, and the police to consider the executive’s actions as legitimate.

Then there is *propaganda*. Illiberal governments vocally defend their reforms as aiming to improve the quality of democracy and to strengthen the voice of “the people” against predatory “elites.” Furthermore, the *technical complexity* of some governmental initiatives obscures their real purpose. It is difficult for the average citizen to grasp (and for the opposition to effectively communicate) the potential danger that, say, changing the rules to appoint the head of an independent agency would represent for democracy. And sometimes, reforms that aggrandize the executive’s power are bundled together with genuinely democratic ones, which increases confusion. Finally, these changes to the constitutional fabric are *incremental*. Illiberal governments do not dismantle liberal democracy abruptly. Instead, they take decisions that gradually extend their power over the state, the economy, and society. This process can take years, with each step easing the next and further entrenching the executive’s hold on power.

At the beginning of this process, even experienced democratic leaders may have doubts about whether liberal democracy is genuinely under threat. Witness the “wait-and-see” posture of the US Democratic Party leaders following Trump’s victory in 2016. Chillingly, near-identical reactions characterized German liberal and centrist circles in February 1933, following Adolf Hitler’s ascent to the *Reich*’s Chancellery. This is not to say that Trump is like Hitler. The point is rather that the election of a new autocrat to power is typically accompanied by widespread uncertainty.

Coming back to the present conjuncture, as the gradual erosion of liberal democracy proceeds, opposition groups, the media, experts, and international actors increasingly denounce illiberalism, making the stakes of the confrontation clearer. Reduced uncertainty may incentivize democratic oppositions to join forces and resist democratic erosion more effectively. Similarly, more clarity on the risk of losing liberal democracy may also induce voters—in particular centrist, less aligned sections of the electorate—to mobilize against creeping autocratization.

However, by the time the cumulation of executive-aggrandizing reforms has made clear the danger for liberal democracy, defeating the illiberals at the ballot box may prove difficult. By then, the government may have significantly restricted the margins for legal opposition by harassing independent media, intimidating opponents, judges, and lawyers, pressuring universities and civil society organizations, and curtailing spaces for protest. A broad democratic front is more likely to emerge, but the field of competition may have tilted so much in favor of the government that the opposition has few chances to regain power.

**Confronting illiberal oppositions**

Like when democratic oppositions confront executive-initiated backsliding, the same paradox applies when pro-democratic majorities confront an emerging illiberal opposition. When a new extremist party emerges, uncertainty typically surrounds its real political agenda. Since a new opposition has obviously never governed, after-the-factevidence that, if elected, it would undermine liberal democracy is not available.

And typically, prospectiveevidence of illiberals’ future intentions is not straightforward. On the one hand, the “myth of moderation,” by which any opposition will be forced to moderate once it is faced with the responsibilities of governing, persists among part of the public. On the other hand, extremist parties often endeavor to present a public face that is more moderate than their true goals. Their public discourse is typically much more “institutional” than their internal communications with cadres and militants, where, away from public scrutiny, they can reveal their true colors—as sporadically exposed by investigative journalists.

For example, in February 2024, undercover reporters revealed the ties between Germany’s *Alternative für Deutschland* and representatives of a neo-Nazi international network, in relation to a secret meeting where they discussed the mass “repatriation” of migrants and German citizens of foreign origin. Only a few months later, Italian journalists infiltrated meetings of the *Fratelli d’Italia* youth organization and documented widespread fascist, racist, and antisemitic attitudes.

As an illiberal opposition grows stronger, however, concealing its true nature becomes harder. To begin with, increased influence attracts greater public scrutiny. Furthermore, its leaders will struggle to control peripheral actors in an expanding organization; local elites are often less media-savvy than national ones and are more prone to expressing extreme views publicly. Finally, as illiberal parties grow, their international ties intensify and become more visible. These may include foreign leaders whose illiberal views are widely recognized. Western European radical right leaders hardly burnished their parties’ liberal-democratic credentials when they publicly endorsed the electoral victories of Viktor Orbán, Jarosław Kaczyński, Donald Trump, or (mostly before the invasion of Ukraine) Vladimir Putin—or when, in 2022, their parties’ MEPs voted against the European Parliament’s resolution declaring Orbán’s Hungary an “electoral autocracy.”

When an illiberal opposition is small, the lack of a sense of an urgent threat often leads mainstream parties to ignore or underestimate its potential danger. As an illiberal opposition grows, and the threat that it can pose to liberal democracy becomes more evident, pro-democratic forces have stronger incentives to act to halt its rise, and important segments of the electorate may mobilize against it. At the same time, just as when democratic oppositions delay confronting a backsliding executive, the range of politically viable countermeasures shrinks. “Militant democracy” legal restrictions on extremist dissent become harder to enforce and more prone to backlash against a large illiberal opposition (for example, disbanding an illiberal party that has 3% of the vote is relatively easier than banning one that has 25%). Gatekeeping and informal obstruction face similar problems. For example, concluding informal agreements to exclude illiberal MPs from certain offices will be more difficult the more seats their party commands. Even some forms of civil society mobilization, such as counterdemonstrations, become harder against an illiberal opposition that commands a large support in society. As the repertoire of viable countermeasures shrinks, the prospects for successfully confronting illiberals decline.

**The road ahead**

In 1940, General Douglas MacArthur famously summarized the main reason for defeat in war in two words: “Too late.” Given the poor record of democratic forces in containing the rise of illiberalism over the past two decades, it is tempting to apply the same verdict to the survival of liberal democracy today. Yet, all is not lost, and democrats can still prevail. The struggle, however, will probably be long, and liberal democrats will likely lose battles along the way.

Today, [no uncertainty exists any longer about](https://iai.tv/video/in-conversation-john-gray-and-hilary-lawson) the danger that confronts liberal democracy. Illiberal forces are supported by a global political movement that has built momentum and now enjoys overt backing from the biggest superpower on earth. Some countries that were fully democratic twenty years ago have suffered democratic erosion at the hand of illiberal executives. Others have seen illiberal movements influence the national agenda as junior partners in coalition governments or as large opposition parties on the brink of power. Even where the danger may still appear distant, democrats have learned lessons from democratic backsliding in other countries. Few doubt that where illiberal forces get to dominate the executive, they will likely try to entrench their power by eroding checks and balances and constitutional guarantees. “Stealth” takeovers modeled on Orban’s in Hungary after 2010 are now impossible, and the [democratic credentials](https://iai.tv/video/the-age-of-the-illiberal-democracy) of new anti-establishment parties face intense scrutiny.

Yet, clarity does not guarantee survival. Even though today the illiberal threat to democracy is more recognizable, it is much stronger than twenty years ago. Illiberal governments have enacted, and illiberal oppositions have proposed, policies that are favored by large numbers of electors. Liberal democratic parties must contend with the perspective that [illiberal movements are here to stay](https://iai.tv/video/the-world-after-liberalism-maurice-glassman-sohrab-ahmari).

Having largely lost the war of maneuver over the past two decades, democrats must now prepare for a challenging war of position. In the medium term, democracies will likely cycle between periods of contested illiberal government and of difficult democratic reconstruction. During the former, democratic oppositions will resist, not necessarily always with success, executive aggrandizement. They will have to use every means at their disposal, from street protest to litigation in court, from filibustering to civil resistance, to oppose autocratization. During the latter, heterogeneous coalitions of old democratic parties or new political vehicles will face the burden of governing while eliminating pockets of illiberalism from the state apparatus.

In the long run, avoiding breakdown will probably require liberal democracies to undergo [significant realignment](https://iai.tv/articles/john-gray-and-the-end-of-the-wests-utopian-dream-auid-3266). Some old parties will disappear; others will merge or rebrand; new alliances will emerge. Long-standing political allegiances will wane, and new ones will arise. Electoral competition will take place on partially new dimensions. The struggle will be long, it will entail painful compromises and difficult choices, and democrats will certainly suffer setbacks—but it is worth fighting. The stakes are nothing less than ensuring that a system of government that, imperfectly but better than any other, has protected individual autonomy and restrained power, “shall not perish from the earth.”
