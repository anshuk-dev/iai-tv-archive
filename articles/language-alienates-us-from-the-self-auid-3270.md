# Language alienates us from the self

*Derrida, Artaud, and the performance of the self*

**By Gigla Gonashvili** | *28th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,054 words*

![Language alienates us from the self](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/alienation-and-the-self.webp)

---

*The self is always alienated from itself. Even Descartes' “I think, therefore I am” suggests a form of alienation because it is unclear who or what the “I” is that is thinking. For this reason, we are always, in a sense, inauthentic and performing as a kind of character. University of Wuppertal philosopher, Gigla Gonashvili, argues that even though our thoughts are not our own, we should allow them to play spontaneously, and through writing, attempt to carve out a more authentic self by attempting to find a language that is authentically our own.*

Alienation is not merely a psychological or social phenomenon, but rather something that emerges within thinking itself, inasmuch as it expresses a truth about the thinking subject. In fact, the Cartesian “I think, therefore I am,” despite being ultimately put forward as an absolute certainty, contains within it the possibility of spontaneous alienation – precisely because it is not clear who this “I” is that thinks and, moreover, experiments with the givenness of their self. As we know, [for Descartes, the solitary meditator is no longer a body](http://iai.tv/video/the-fantasies-of-descartes-antonio-damasio) or a rational animal (as first formulated by Aristotle and passed down through the scholastic tradition), but a thinker who, through the spontaneous creation of expressions, resists the dissolution of sense or rational certainties. Curiously, the same aspiration can be found in the thought of the 20th-century French artist Antonin Artaud, who, through his efforts to express the disappearance of the thinker within the thought itself, offers us a broad spectrum of ways to think alienation.

Let’s first take a step back to appreciate the etymological and historical perspectives. The word alienation, in its root (*alius*), denotes becoming-other. It initially had a legal meaning, the transfer of property rights to another person, but by the 17th century, it came to refer to estrangement from one’s mental faculties. For example, in French, the word *aliéné* was used to designate a mentally ill patient.

\_\_\_

In fact, as Derrida shows, Cartesian methodology, at its core, acknowledges the possibility of madness, and the claim “I think, therefore I am” is made in spite of it.

\_\_\_

The term quickly acquired social connotations as well. Most famously, [Marx spoke of the four-fold alienation of workers in industrialized societies](http://iai.tv/articles/hegel-vs-marx-ideas-change-the-world-not-economics-auid-3244). And if we cast our gaze on post-war French philosophy, we’ll discover a strong focus on becoming-other, whether in cases of pathology (analyzed through Freudian psychoanalysis) or in literature. In fact, all the basic words we use to conjure up our own perspectives – proper names, genders, social roles – inscribe themselves into the symbolic order of social relations. It’s hard to speak in one’s own name, precisely because the name is given by the Other. For instance, the French expression *je m’appelle* (literally: I call myself) expresses the desire of a subject to have power over their own name. Or, as the opening line of the great American novel reads: “Call me Ishmael.”

These ordeals of a thinking subject in language are perhaps nowhere more evident than in the thought of Jacques Derrida. I have particularly in mind his writings collected in the seminal work *[Writing and Difference](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/W/bo27619783.html)* (1967). In the article “Cogito and the History of Madness,” Derrida deconstructs the Foucauldian reading of the cogito, according to which Descartes had to simply suppress and exclude madness from the realm of reason in order to establish a clear-cut principle. In fact, as Derrida shows, Cartesian methodology, at its core, acknowledges the possibility of madness, and the claim “I think, therefore I am” is made in spite of it.

No less important is the article on Artaud titled “La parole soufflée” (that is, the whispered or breathed word), where the issue of an inner voice, so often a free-floating signification, is dramatized at its most intense. Derrida shows us how the place of the subject gets taken by an Other – not just primordially, but immemorially. That is to say, there is always a before, an *ante*; before I even begin to tell my story, there is rupture, a breakdown. In this sense, the excess of emotion or affect and its disappearance – its annulment – begin to coincide. And the subject who seeks to think and express herself feels lost: because, precisely, the word “exist” either adds no signification to the interior *élan*, or exposes her to the infinite light of existence.

The story of Antonin Artaud begins in 1923, when he sends his poems to the prestigious literary journal *Nouvelle Revue Française*, only to be rejected. Yet the editor-in-chief, Jacques Rivière, takes an interest in the young artist, invites him to his office, and after this personal encounter, begins a correspondence that has been the subject of much commentary by French philosophers. In these letters, Artaud’s concern is not so much with the work of art (which he calls “detached creation”) but rather with a vivid portrayal of his mental processes and thoughts. He insists that at the very moment of creation, when he is composing a poem and believes he has seized an expression, something within him collapses. There is a strange diminution of mental tension, an emergence of bifurcations in thought that render coherence impossible.

\_\_\_

Artaud discovers that every word, every expression, is a double-edged sword: on the one hand, there is always a nascent feeling that self-creation is possible; on the other, there is an almost instantaneous alienation from it.

\_\_\_

For Artaud, these disturbances are neither incidental nor merely psychological. The “erosion” that occurs in his thinking is, as he puts it, both “essential and fugitive.” That is to say, the essential nature of these occurrences should be grasped through their fugacious fragmentation. Furthermore, he speaks of a “higher vicious will [that] attacks the soul like vitriol, attacks the mass of words and imagery” – a will which introduces separation at the very root of expression itself. Artaud discovers that every word, every expression, is a double-edged sword: on the one hand, there is always a nascent feeling that self-creation is possible; on the other, there is an almost instantaneous alienation from it. This [alienation, in turn, should be explained](http://iai.tv/articles/authenticity-alienation-and-privilege-auid-920), or rather inscribed, within individual life, and not merely dismissed or monopolized by an external sign or symptom.

It is this passage to the other side, propelled by the alienation, that becomes Artaud’s main project, and is famously expressed in his conception of cruelty. As he states in his writings on theater, cruelty is not a desire for bloodshed but precisely something lived consciously; that is, a lived experience that accepts terror and defiguration, but also grabs a chance to make sense of life. Artaud himself, as we know, endured many cruelties and defigurations of personality: the last nine or so years of his life were spent in various mental asylums (while World War II raged on the continent), during which he produced writings, drawings, and ultimately a radio performance ("To Have Done with the Judgment of God").

The first years of his internment, following his breakdown, Artaud spent in silence. It was only later, step by step, that he returned to writing and recounting his story – a story full of gaps and delirious phantasms. It has been said, most notably by Gilles Deleuze, that Artaud came to create what is called a [schizophrenic language](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2950318/). For example, in his glossolalia, one encounters words apparently devoid of any meaning and, curiously, crammed with consonants, making them almost impossible to pronounce. Thus, they resemble bodily spasms that engulf and destroy all meaning, giving rise to, [as Deleuze maintains, the notorious Body without Organs](http://iai.tv/articles/love-is-close-to-madness-sinan-richards-auid-2832).

Yet, as shown in more recent work by the French phenomenologist Jacob Rogozinski, these practices in Artaud might rather point to an attempt to rememorate oneself and, so to speak, re-compose one’s own name. For instance, there are variations around the proper name (ratara/atara) or singular, deliberately disruptive words (draorg taorg or rouarghe), which resist the dissolution of expression into general meaning and paradoxically remain unpronounceable yet unforgettable. The issue here revolves around singular existence and reformulates the Cartesian adage: it’s me who exists. Or rather: me, the thinker of this thought, is here. The determination of this existence acquires full value precisely because it must be performatively demonstrated, expressed, and proffered each time.

The life of Artaud can be seen as a continuous effort to live in alienation. In one of his last texts, dedicated to the life and paintings of van Gogh, he speaks of authentic alienation, or more precisely, of someone who would be an “*aliéné authentique*” (authentically alienated). Art, for him, is in fact an attempt to include an element that is always left out or simply botched up. Commenting on one of [van Gogh’s self-portraits](http://iai.tv/iai-academy/courses/info?course=heidegger-meets-van-gogh-art-freedom-and-technology) (the one with the felt hat, 1887), where the anguished, penetrating gaze of the artist meets our eyes, Artaud speaks of “a vacant, hollow gaze, turned inward,” captured at the moment “when the pupil is about to spill itself into the void.” This is precisely the moment of alienation, reversed and somehow brought back to life.

Today, as we witness, among other things, the emergence of so-called Large Language Models, which enter into a kind of human-like contact with us, we sense that another form of alienation may be on the rise. On the one hand, all meaning and information now seem to lie in plain sight; on the other, the source of agency, produced through the raw material provided by humans, is continuously displaced or dissimulated. [The Archimedean fixed point](https://www.jstor.org/stable/24540769), upon which Descartes sought to build his philosophy, has long since been submerged by aleatory, contingent facts, whether of a mundane or strictly scientific nature. And yet, the need to come to terms with the dissolution of certainties feels more pressing than ever – because we are less and less sure whether our own conscious thoughts mean anything at all. And if we are to be alienated, we want to know whether, at the very least, there can be something authentic to it.

The authentic alienation does, to some extent, correspond to the Cartesian thinking substance, for the reality of this subject could only be maintained after the confrontation with the figure of a malicious spirit (*genius malignus*) – that is, with the figure who might be continuously tricking the lonely thinker and divesting him of his attributes (no body, no mind). The only solution, therefore, is to return to the spontaneously emerging thoughts, however crazy they might seem. Because if we don’t allow this spontaneity, there’s a danger that soon there will be nothing to say, and no one left to say it. As Derrida himself had to state explicitly in his response to Foucault: “The disciple must break the glass, or better, the mirror, the reflection, his infinite speculation on the master. And start to speak.”

One of the ways to face this danger of wordlessness would be to try and enter into a different relationship with language and, more importantly, with the expression of one’s own thoughts. And for that, one doesn’t have to be Descartes or Artaud, doesn’t need to be a philosopher, a poet, or a madman. For example, what has today become known as journaling (which for some people is also a practice of mindfulness) can be seen as a way to resist the dissolution of word and inner meaning. Nevertheless, even while writing alone and for ourselves, there can be a tendency to use only one kind of voice, just as we might want to show only one kind of face to our fellow human beings.

Therefore, it’s better to think that the words springing spontaneously from our interiority come from different places and different times, and imply different moods and sensibilities. We all know, for example, that when there’s a song stuck in our heads, it’s hard to say when it began. Or, to give another example, the reason why we appreciate the goofy characters of modern sitcoms or old comedies (let’s say, the Marx Brothers) is that their faces and gestures reveal a cascade of small expressions that drag us into the dramatic moment and make us experience different perspectives and feelings in a short span of time. It’s in this sense that art endows us with a different kind of openness, makes us include that which would have otherwise been inescapably lost or alienated, and helps us overcome a detached description of reality, be it inner or outer.
