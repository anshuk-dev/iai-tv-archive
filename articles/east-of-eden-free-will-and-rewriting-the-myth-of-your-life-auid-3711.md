# East of Eden, free will, and rewriting the myth of your life

*How to escape a self-fulfilling prophecy*

**By Noah Lazarides** | *23rd October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,466 words*

![East of Eden, free will, and rewriting the myth of your life](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/east-of-eden-free-will-and-rewriting-the-myth-of-your-life.webp)

*In* East of Eden*, Steinbeck instructs us to exercise our free will, break out of the bonds of fate, and put an end to cycles of violence. But there is little explicit argument for how this is possible. Contributing editor **Noah Lazarides** argues that the answer is hidden in the form of the novel itself: the retelling of myth. Unearthing the metaphysical ideas that might have shaped the writing of the novel, we find that for Steinbeck the way to change the future is to rewrite the past. In a world made of ideas, we each have the creative freedom to write our own self-fulfilling prophecies.*

**1. The problem with *East of Eden***

On first look, *East of Eden* wrestles with a contradiction between its two most prominent themes: fateand freedom. The novel weaves together a multi-generational story of two families—the Trasks and the Hamiltons—living in the Salinas Valley in California where Steinbeck grew up. The Civil War and First World War act as the historical bookends of the novel, but it is hardly free from conflict. It is, after all, a retelling of Genesis. And the unfortunate Trasks are fated to repeat the myth of Cain and Abel in violent refrain.

And yet, Steinbeck goes to great lengths to insist upon his characters’ freedom. The narrative vehicle for this theme is the Hebrew word *timshel*, which is lifted from the Cain and Abel story and discussed at some length within *East of Eden*’s dialogue. Samuel Hamilton (a fictionalized version of Steinbeck’s own grandfather) and Adam Trask (father, of course, to the stand-ins for Cain and Abel, i.e., Cal and Aron Trask) settle upon an alternative translation of *timshel*. The word is usually translated as “*thou shalt* rule over [sin],” but Steinbeck makes the case for “*thou mayest*” as a better fit. On this reading of the good book, God tells Cain that it is his choice whether he does right or wrong; he *may* do either.

But how is this possible? How can Steinbeck *tell* us that Cal Trask is free to do as he may, while *showing* us that his actions are determined by myth?  To resolve this awkward interpretive challenge, I warn the reader that I am going to offer a non-standard interpretation of the novel and, indeed, of Steinbeck’s thought.

The resolution of this conflict lies in the following radical philosophical position: the world is made of stories. For Steinbeck, your personality consists in the narrative of your personal history—the self-fulfilling prophecies we tell to ourselves. Yet in that storytelling we have some creative freedom. In other words, we are each bound to live by myths that, for Steinbeck, we are nonetheless free to *rewrite*.

At first, this might sound absurd—the idealistic flight of fancy we would expect of a novelist. But Steinbeck was seriously engaged with the philosophy of his time. And his position derives from the scientifically grounded, “no-nonsense” tradition of [American pragmatism.](https://iai.tv/video/a-tale-of-truth-with-simon-blackburn)

**2. Pragmatism and the power of myth**

When I first read *East of Eden*, I was struck by just how thoroughly American it felt. Perhaps it was Steinbeck’s immersion in pragmatism, the only American-grown philosophical tradition, which lent its distinctive lilt to the book’s themes. Led by Charles Sanders Peirce and [William James](https://iai.tv/articles/russell-vs-american-philosophers-and-the-attack-on-truth-auid-3165) in the nineteenth century, the pragmatists believed that the meaning of a concept lies in its practical implications. They sought to bring philosophy’s metaphysical concepts from their lofty, detached heights and [ground them in practice](https://iai.tv/video/pragmatism-and-the-pursuit-of-truth).

If pragmatism is America’s philosophy, then John Elof Boodin at UCLA was its representative on the West Coast, where Steinbeck lived and where *East of Eden* is firmly rooted. Boodin had a significant influence on Steinbeck, who had read his papers and at least two of his books—*Cosmic Evolution* and *A Realistic Universe*—before beginning *East of Eden*. In his work, Boodin fused pragmatism and metaphysical idealism—the belief that knowable reality is made of ideas—into what he called “pragmatic realism.”

The philosopher and the fictionalist were also in correspondence. Steinbeck was Boodin’s “writer friend” and asked his permission to use his philosophy in his work. Boodin believed that the ongoing creation of the universe consists in the evolution of ideas and, indeed, Steinbeck quoted Boodin directly in *Log from the Sea of Cortez*, before musing, “somehow, the laws of thoughts must be the laws of things if we are going to attempt a science of reality.” This is the philosophical support for the irresistible power of myth in *East of Eden*—the laws of thoughts governing our lives.

Steinbeck chooses to focus on just one aspect of this grander metaphysics: on the mythic invention of personality. Steinbeck’s idealist metaphysics of the person draws on a long pragmatist tradition. According to its founder, Charles Sanders Peirce, “my language is the sum total of myself; for the man is the thought… The identity of a man consists in the consistency of what he does and thinks.” For Steinbeck, creation myths take centre stage in “what a man does and thinks.” The stories the characters tell about who they are and where they come from come to govern their future actions.

Talking of the Cain and Abel story for example, Adam Trask says, “This is our father. Some of our guilt is absorbed in our ancestry. What chance did we have? We are the children of our father… It is an excuse.” Adam is happy to have an excuse for his actions. And in accepting the story as one, he abides by it as a cause. Thus, Adam’s great flaw is his conscious adherence, and passive deference, to myth. He says, only half joking, “I mean to make a garden of my land. Remember my name is Adam.” And indeed he does make himself Adam and thereby dooms his sons to be Cain and Abel. They cannot help but abide. Cal grows up believing he is rotten, and at the rejection of a gift to his father, he kills Aron just as the myth demands (albeit indirectly). At every turn, Steinbeck affirms the fateful power of story.

**3. The possibility of freedom**

Let us return, as Steinbeck repeatedly does, to *timshel* (*“*thou mayest”). He calls it the “most important word in the world”: an eternal reminder that God made man free. At the very end of the novel, it is all Adam, while on his deathbed, has the strength to whisper to his son Cal. And with it, Steinbeck literally gets the last word over fate. But… is it enough? On the surface, Adam’s utterance is the mere assertion of a doctrine. The claim that Cal is free to direct his own life flies in the face of all that came before and, arguably, falls flat.

However, if we take a second look, the defining act of *timshel* is not its blank assertion, but its re-translation. The few spare moments of hope in the novel come when, like in the discussion of *timshel*, the characters decide to reinvent their own creation myth. Cal tells his monstrous mother, Cathy, that he “doesn’t have to be [her],” and thereby rejects the original-sin story of inherited evil. If her other son, Aron, had done the same, the parallels with Genesis would have stopped there. Instead, “he couldn’t stand to know about his mother because that’s not how he wanted the story to go—and he wouldn’t have any other story. So he tore up the world.” The final result is Aron’s death.

At first, Cal blames himself for the death of his brother, interpreting the events in line with the biblical story. But Adam, as his final act (whispering *timshel*), encourages Cal to engage in reinterpretation. With his last word, Adam tells Cal that he has always been free to do as he may, and places the blame firmly on those who resign themselves to pre-existing myths and fail to engage in reinterpretation, including Aron and himself.

To be clear, Steinbeck’s position is not that anything goes. Boodin was an idealist, but he was also a pragmatic realist. In *East of Eden*, as in our world, you cannot directly contradict history to write a more favorable version of events and thereby make it true. This is what the Ministry of Truth aspires to in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. But for Steinbeck you can no more disobey “the laws of thought” than you can the laws of nature. However, you are free to think a little differently.

Consider the fact that Steinbeck was well aware that his translation of *timshel* would not be agreed upon by Hebrew scholars, but that the ambiguity in the translation was enough for him. The ever-evolving myths that make up our reality are all we have, and they cannot be directly contradicted. But their meaning, as every novelist knows, is ambiguous and leaves room for reinterpretation.

For Steinbeck, the laws of nature are only as restrictive as the rules of language, and in much the same way. Of course, we are not free to interpret the meaning of a text however we like. It would make my job easier if we were. But if I began ignoring the established meanings of words, I would be speaking nonsense. Likewise, one who ignores the established laws of nature, or the reality of history as it is governed by the laws of thought, would find themselves in a similar state of chaos. They would, like Aron, meet an untimely end when they try to “[tear] up the world.”

**4. Steinbeck’s own reinvention**

One of the most striking features of this book is its fusion of Steinbeck’s real family history with the mythic tale of the Trasks. Samuel Hamilton was Steinbeck’s grandfather, and our narrator indulges a fantasy of what kind of man he was. He seems larger than life: an Irish immigrant; an inventor; a famous teller of tales; a blacksmith, super-humanly gifted at delivering babies; a poet and theologian. He is patient, kind, and displays saint-like loyalty to his wife, at least, as Steinbeck tells it. He insists that “all of the Hamilton stories are true.” But we know that this is the kind of ambiguous truth a myth can have, rather than one that excludes interpretation.

While writing the novel, Steinbeck reflected, “I don’t see how it can be popular because I am inventing method and form and tone and context.” I think he is quite right to say that he was inventing a new form. It is not merely [a creation myth for America](https://iai.tv/articles/how-america-hijacked-western-philosophy-auid-3585), for “his country”; it is a creation myth for himself. He refers to *East of Eden* as “the story of me.”

In other words, Steinbeck is indulging in his own reinvention. With the uniquely human power of language and storytelling, Steinbeck exercises his [freedom to create himself](https://iai.tv/articles/is-your-worldview-making-you-depressed-auid-1969). Leading by example, he reinterprets who he is and where he came from, even as he implores his characters to do the same.

**5. Metaphysical concerns**

Before I close, a spare few of you will appreciate me taking the opportunity to clear up some misconceptions about the early American pragmatists. Those of you with some familiarity with pragmatism, especially those for whom the word first brings to mind [neo-pragmatists like Richard Rorty](https://iai.tv/articles/how-america-hijacked-western-philosophy-auid-3585) or contemporary pragmatists like [Simon Blackburn](https://iai.tv/video/how-i-changed-my-mind-about-truth-simon-blackburn), might well have baulked at the idea of a pragmatist idealist. Surely, you will say, the whole point of pragmatism—the belief that the rational meaning of a concept lies in its practical implications—is to purge philosophy of moonshine metaphysical notions like “reality is made of ideas”. After all, what does such a claim mean *in practice*?

And yet, at the dawn of the 20th century, American pragmatism went hand-in-hand with idealism, at least it did in the minds of two of its most prominent figures. [Charles Sanders Peirce](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/), the polymathic originator of pragmatism and all-round genius, came to believe that his doctrine led directly to what he called *objective idealism*—that external reality, to the extent that it is cognisable, is made up of ideas or “signs”. And [Josiah Royce](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/), a Californian like Steinbeck and Boodin, followed in Peirce’s footsteps. He was the pragmatist who famously brought [Hegel’s absolute idealism](https://iai.tv/articles/idealism-is-realism-jeremy-dunham-auid-2815) to the American stage, though in his later works he converged more closely with Peirce and shifted away from Hegelianism.

If the inventor of pragmatism thought that idealism was an admissible metaphysical concept, what does it mean in practice? To put it punishingly bluntly, the practical implications of idealism are that the world is essentially *cognisable* and that we ought to act accordingly. In other words, to say that something is an idea, is to say that it can be thought, believed, doubted, expressed, conveyed, interpreted, and communicated. An idealistic reality is one that is susceptible to inquiry. It is a world that can be *known*, because it is not fundamentallydifferent from the thoughts we already think (i.e. it is also, for want of a better word, thoughts). Thus the practical implication is that inquiry is a worthwhile practice. There is a point to conducting scientific experiments, to sharing and comparing knowledge, to trying to persuade each other through rational argument, because in each case there is already a real idea which it is theoretically possible for us to converge upon—for us all to think. But in order to do so, we must correctly interpret the signs we come across in the course of our inquiry. This question of interpretation brings us back, finally, to *East of Eden*.

**6. The last word**

I have tried to persuade you that the only way we can make sense of the apparent paradox of *East of Eden*—the assertion of both freedom and fate—is to read Steinbeck’s characters as though they were living in a world made of ideas. (This is not much of a leap; given that they are literally in a fictional story). These ideas should not be misinterpreted nor contradicted but they can be *reinterpreted* and this is the freedom that his characters have. But Steinbeck seems to want to go further. He is not only talking to the characters when he instructs them to rewrite their own stories. By the third or fourth propounding of *timshel**, you cannot help but feel that he is preaching at his reader too*. He even takes his own advice and rewrites the story of his personal genesis. His claim then, when he writes *timshel* as the last word of the book, is not only that the world of *East of Eden* is a developing, unfinished story awaiting reinterpretation, but that ours is too. In this way, Steinbeck teaches that reality is not limited to one absolute idea. It is a continuously evolving metaboly of ideas, of which each of us is a contributing member, and in which each person has the creative freedom to reinterpret the past and thereby change the future.
