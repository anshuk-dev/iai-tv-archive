# Imagination is not escapism, it brings us closer to reality

*The serious business of make-believe*

**By Amy Kind** | *24th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,126 words*

![Imagination is not escapism, it brings us closer to reality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/imagination-is-not-escapism-it-brings-us-closer-to-reality.webp)

---

*Einstein called imagination "more important than knowledge," yet we increasingly treat it as a childhood pastime we outgrow. Philosopher of mind **Amy Kind** argues that it's something far more practical: the skill we draw on to make our hardest decisions, read the people around us, and work out who we want to become. Like any skill, it weakens without use — and we're using it less. Reading for pleasure has nearly halved in two decades, fewer parents play with their children each day, and we increasingly hand our creative work to machines. If we don't make time to exercise it, we'll lose the capacity to conceive of things being other than they are, and risk being trapped in the present, unable to imagine a different future, let alone build one.*

In recent years, amidst the hustle and bustle of contemporary life, people are devoting considerably less time to imaginative activities. [Significantly fewer people are reading for pleasure](https://www.cell.com/iscience/fulltext/S2589-0042(25)01549-4) today compared to 20 years ago, and in just the last decade, [significantly fewer parents are making time to play with their children](https://www.thetimes.com/life-style/parenting/article/parents-playtime-children-under-six-qztj57d9f) on a daily basis than used to be the case.

This neglect of imagination has been accelerated by the increasing reliance on generative AI tools in both personal contexts and professional contexts. In [one recent survey](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-are-americans-using-ai-evidence-from-a-nationwide-survey/), more than 50% of adults reported interacting with AI tool at least several times a week for personal purposes, often for learning, entertainment, or supporting their children’s education. In [another study](https://www-sciencedirect-com.ccl.idm.oclc.org/science/article/pii/S0166497224001317) on business uses of AI, more than half of firms surveyed reported using AI in the creation of new products and services and, more generally, in their at innovation projects. With each passing day, we seem to be increasingly more willing—and perhaps even eager—to outsource our creative and imaginative efforts to machines.

\_\_\_

Not only does imagination enable us to escape the world in which we live, but it also helps us to learn about the world in which we live.

\_\_\_

Should we worry about these trends? Given a longstanding tradition in philosophy that dismisses imagination as unimportant, or worse, views it with disdain, perhaps the neglect of imagination is nothing to be troubled about. One might even think it is something to be celebrated. This would likely be the attitude taken by someone like Blaise Pascal, who saw [imagination as essentially deceitful and deceptive](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-h/18269-h.htm)—the “mistress of error and falsity”—or by Plato, who famously wanted to ban the poets from his idealized city; poetry, in his view, appeals to our passions rather than to our reason.

But the dismissal of imagination, in my view, is a deep mistake. [Imagination lies at the very heart of who we are as humans](https://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real), and a world without imagination would not just be impoverished but dysfunctional.

**The dual nature of imagination**

Even if one distances oneself from the deep pessimism evidenced by Pascal and Plato, one might still be disinclined to see much practical significance in the neglect of imagination. After all, isn’t imagination simply a matter of whimsy? It’s only when we’re looking for fun, or when we’re trying to escape from the dreary circumstances in which we find ourselves, that we turn to imagination. When we’re engaged in serious pursuits, whether scientific discovery or some other form of knowledge creation, imagination has no role to play. Or so the thought goes.

But this thought is mistaken, and doubly so. First, it is a mistake to think that humanity could flourish absent any opportunities for escapism and fun. When philosophers like Martha Nussbaum lay out the [ten fundamental capabilities](https://journals.law.harvard.edu/hrj/wp-content/uploads/sites/83/2020/06/20HHRJ21-Nussbaum.pdf) that humans need to live healthy and fulfilling lives, capabilities that must be protected as a matter of human rights, she includes imagination on the list. To thrive, humans must be able to use their imagination freely in all different sorts of contexts, from engaging with literary and musical works to producing them oneself.

Second, and perhaps more importantly, it is a mistake to think that imagination is merely escapist in nature and has no role to play in serious enterprises. The noted speculative fiction writer Ursula K. LeGuin [perfectly captures this point in one of her many essays](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Words_are_My_Matter/CZKzDwAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=detached%20from%20reality): “Imagination, even in its wildest flights, is not detached from reality: imagination acknowledges reality, starts from it, and returns to enrich it.” Imagination, as she says, “is a way into truth.”

This connects to a central theme that runs through my own philosophical work about [the dual nature of imagination](https://academic-oup-com.ccl.idm.oclc.org/book/1614): Not only does imagination enable us to escape the world in which we live, but it also helps us to learn about the world in which we live. As I put it, imagination has both *transcendent* and *instructive* uses. We can come to understand how a single mental capacity is able to serve two such different functions in part by thinking about the role that constraints play in our imaginative explorations. In transcendent uses of imagination, we are not constrained by facts about the world as it actually is. We release the constraints of reality and are no longer tightly tethered to it (even if, in line with LeGuin, we can never be entirely untethered). In contrast, when we engage in instructive uses of imagination, we deliberately impose constraints so that our imagining will better reveal insights into how our reality is—or might be.

**Learning from imagination**

Instructive uses of imagination lie at the core of a variety of human achievements, from scientific discovery to technological invention to artistic creation. As a teen, [Albert Einstein famously imagined himself chasing a beam of light](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/on-the-heels-of-a-light-beam1/), and he then put this imagining to use in developing his theory of special relativity. The engineer and futurist Nikola Tesla [claimed](https://www.google.com/books/edition/My_Inventions/) that, before ever starting to put pen to paper, he first developed all of his inventions entirely in his imagination. Michelin chef Aitor Jeronimo Orive [reports](https://guide.michelin.com/en/article/people/imaginative-technology-letting-creative-juices-flow-in-the-kitchen) that his development of experimental cooking techniques and avant-garde cuisine has been fueled by a unique blend of technology and imagination

It is not just scientific geniuses and award-winning chefs who call upon imagination in pursuit of new knowledge. A moment’s reflection reveals numerous ways in which we put imagination to instructive use in daily life. When you get home with a new sofa that you’ve just bought, and you’re not quite sure how you’re going to get it through the door, it’s natural to call upon imagination. You mentally rotate the sofa this way and that until you figure out how you should orient it, thereby saving your back from a bunch of unnecessary physical rotations. When you are trying to decide what to wear to your job interview, you might imagine a number of different clothing combinations to help you make the best fashion choice. And when you are flummoxed to find your friend unexpectedly glaring at you, you might imaginatively put yourself in her shoes to figure out what she might be thinking.

Granted, we are not always wholly successful when making these imaginative efforts. But imagination can be useful to us even when it’s not perfect. This point comes out beautifully in an exchange between the teenage protagonist Quentin and his childhood crush, Margo, [in the novel *Paper Towns* by best-selling author John Green](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Paper_Towns/9eemddBo3D8C?hl=en&gbpv=0). Bemoaning the fact that imagination isn’t perfect, Margo complains that “Nothing ever happens like you imagine it will.” Quentin realizes that she’s right, but after thinking for a moment, he also realizes that if you don’t imagine, “nothing ever happens at all.” As he puts it: “Imagining isn’t perfect. You can’t get all the way inside someone else. … But imagining being someone else, or the world being something else, is the only way in. It is the machine that kills fascists.”

**Imaginative skill**

That’s not to say that we should rest content with unsuccessful imaginative efforts. [Imagination is a skill](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315180809-33/skill-imagination-amy-kind). Like any skill, some people are better at it than others. But also like any skill, we can improve our imagination with practice.

[How might one practice imagination?](https://academic.oup.com/bjaesthetics/article/62/1/33/6500127) In some professions—and perhaps not the ones that might first come to mind when thinking of imagination—the practice of imagination is taken very seriously. In the realm of sport, for example, elite athletes often work with sports psychologists on [mental imagery training](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413209108406438). They use imagination scripts to help them visualize the perfect golf swing or to help them internalize [the twists and turns on an unfamiliar bobsled track](https://www.nytimes.com/2014/02/23/sports/olympics/olympians-use-imagery-as-mental-training.html).

Elite athletes aside, however, most of us are unlikely to take imagination lessons the way that we might take piano lessons, and we are unlikely to work with an imagination coach the way we might work with a basketball coach. So what other means are there to practice imagination? Here we can return to LeGuin, who notes that “one of the best exercises for the imagination, maybe the very best, is hearing, reading, and telling or writing made-up stories.” The philosopher Martha Nussbaum also [extols the values of engaging with literature for the development of imagination](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674179493)—and especially for the cultivation of our capacity for empathetic imagination. In literature, we have direct encounters with the inner lives of fictional characters, and this provides us with crucial practice for our efforts to understand [the thoughts and feelings of the real-life people we encounter throughout our lives](https://iai.tv/video/strange-new-worlds).

One practices imagination when one plays games of make-believe with one’s kids, or when one plays party games with friends. Consider also the kinds of activities involved in improv games, when you have to imagine a funny use for an unusual prop or an alternate ending to a scene. Just as one works at the improvement of one’s piano skill by playing piano or the improvement of one’s basketball skill by playing basketball, one works at the improvement of one’s imagination skill by imagining.

**The deskilling of imagination**

Like any skill, imagination improves with use. But also like any skill, imagination atrophies with disuse. This brings us back to the worry with which we began. When we don’t make time for imaginative activities, and when we outsource our creative tasks to AI, we allow our imagination skill to wither away. And that is a serious cause for concern.

The last five years have seen incredible advances in artificial intelligence, and these advances have unsurprisingly led to increased use of AI systems across a vast array of domains. We are coming to rely on AI both at work and at home, for tasks ranging from workflow management and navigation, to travel or menu planning, to help with medical diagnoses. We ask AI for relationship advice or, in some cases, develop relationships directly with AI.

There are all sorts of reasons to be concerned about all of this. There are ethical considerations about our treatment of machines, and on the flipside, concerns about how we might one day be treated by a race of superintelligent AI overlords. There are concerns about environmental impact and concerns about the future of interpersonal relationships. Most relevant to our discussion here, however, are the broad set of concerns about [deskilling](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-82086-1_4). Though the turn to AI initially looks to augment our human capacities and skills, it soon becomes clear that we’re in danger of losing those capacities and skills instead.

How does this play out in the case of imagination? It might seem harmless when we ask AI to choose an interview outfit for us, or to plan out our vacation itinerary, instead of imagining the different possibilities ourselves. But now what happens when it comes to more important decisions, decisions that lie at the core of our very identities? As a general matter, [our decision-making practices are underlain by acts of imagination](https://iai.tv/articles/imagination-is-not-an-escape-from-reality-auid-3002). When it’s time to decide what college to attend, or what career to embark on, or whether to get married, or whether to have children, we imagine the different alternatives. We imaginatively put ourselves in the shoes of our future possible selves in an effort to figure out which of those selves is the one that we want to become. But once our imaginations have atrophied, once we are entirely out of practice in conducting these kinds of imaginative simulations, we lose the ability to make good decisions for ourselves.

The same dialectic plays out on a societal scale. AI can provide us with all sorts of data about possible societal outcomes, but with an atrophied imagination, we are unable to see these possibilities for ourselves, and we are unable to imaginatively compare them in an effort to make choices between them. The deskilling of imagination thus not only threatens our personal futures but our societal futures as well.

Confronted with this potentiality, it is easy to see why it is crucially important to make time for imagination. Imagination may be the source of whimsy, but it is also the source of knowledge. In short, as the philosopher Jose Medina has put it, “[Imagination is not a luxury or a privilege, but a necessity](https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_Epistemology_of_Resistance/5PNQEAAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&printsec=frontcover&bsq=imagination%20is%20not%20a%20luxury).”
