# The fatal flaw in our search for alien life

*The size of the universe is no reason for optimism*

**By Christopher Cowie** | *10th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,540 words*

![The fatal flaw in our search for alien life](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-fatal-flaw-in-our-search-for-alien-life.webp)

---

*The universe contains trillions of planets, and scientists are more optimistic than ever that we are on the verge of finding life beyond Earth. But **Christopher Cowie**, Associate Professor in Philosophy at the University of Durham, thinks this optimism rests on a deceptively simple mistake. A vast universe doesn't mean a dense one, and unless life exists close enough for our telescopes to observe it, its abundance elsewhere is simply irrelevant to us. The universe, he argues, may be teeming with life. We just may never find any of it and we have never questioned why finding it matters in the first place.*

There is growing optimism that we are on the brink of discovering evidence of life beyond Earth. This requires that life is out there to be found in the first place. And it seems, at first, eminently reasonable to think it is. The sheer size of the universe ensures it. There are just so many possible sites: planets and moons that host liquid water, and life’s basic constituent elements.

Writing in 2024, the [British astronaut Tim Peake](https://thetraveldiariespodcast.com/episodes/tim-peake-astronaut/) gave particularly clear voice to this thought. Peake, who wagered that we would find evidence of microbial life beyond Earth within just five years, said:

*I think the universe is teeming with life. If you’re in any doubt as to the vastness and the scale of the universe, just go and Google some of these James Webb Space Telescope shots that we’re getting now … I mean, hundreds of billions of stars in our own Milky Way galaxy, which is very average.*

His point seems persuasive. It is also commonplace. Because the universe is enormous, life is out there in large quantities. Hence, optimism in the search for life beyond Earth makes sense. But there is in fact a problem with this reasoning.

There are two very different things that Peake might mean when he claims that the universe is teeming with life. One is that the universe is *dense* with life; that life is, as it were, everywhere that you look. The other is simply that life is plentiful; that there is a lot of it. These sound similar, but they are very different indeed. An analogy helps to explain the difference. The population of Australia is approximately 30 million. There is a perfectly good sense in which this is an awful lot of people; imagine them all lined up, or crowded onto a beach (as Australians sometimes are). “What a lot of people,” you might say. But in terms of population density, Australia is actually one of the lower-ranking countries in the world, clocking in at only 3 people per square kilometre. Australia, we might say, is teeming with life in one sense, but not the other. Contrast this with, for example, the Netherlands. Its population is almost 20 million, but, given its small size, contains about 500 people per square kilometre. It is teeming with life in *both* senses.

Now think about the universe. When Peake says that the universe is teeming with life, which sense does he have in mind? The reason that he gives is that the universe is so vast, so enormous, that it contains many, many stars, planets and so on. That’s not a point about density. On the contrary. If something has to be vast to contain a lot of life, then, to that extent, life is *not* particularly dense within it. So Peake can only really be making a point about plenitude. This matters a great deal. If we’re going to find life, we need it to be not only plentiful, but also dense. This is because our ability to search for it is limited to relatively short distances; a couple of hundred light-years. Beyond this, we can’t detect and characterize the small, rocky planets that might harbor it. So unless life is nearby, we won’t find it. And unless it’s dense, we have no reason to expect it to be nearby. Suppose, to return to the analogy, you were dropped at a random location in the Netherlands. Because of the density of life there, you should be pretty confident that there would be someone nearby. Not so with Australia, even though life is actually *more* plentiful there.

We can put some numbers on this. Suppose we say that we could detect a [life-bearing planet](https://iai.tv/video/looking-for-life-avi-loeb) only if it existed within a radius of 100 light years. Just how plentiful must life be for it to be rational to bet that we could detect it? Assume that there’s nothing special about us; if a life-bearing planet falls within 100 light-years of Earth, that’s because there is, on average, one life-bearing planet within a 100 light-year radius of a randomly chosen point within our galaxy. Given the size of our own galaxy, we could fit in at least one million such spheres. That means roughly a million inhabited planets. And there are, at best estimate, two trillion galaxies. That means (roughly) 2x1018 inhabited planets. That’s not just teeming. It’s positively hopping. This calculation is, of course, over-simplified. But the basic point is clear enough.

Where does this leave Peake and many like him who are optimistic that the search for extraterrestrial life will succeed? The generous reading is that they have a problem. For we can grant that the size of the universe makes it likely that life is plentiful, but unless it is also *dense* we shouldn’t expect to find it. The less generous reading is that their argument rests on an equivocation. It works from a data point that entitles us to think life is plentiful, like Australia, to a conclusion that life is not only plentiful but also dense, like the Netherlands. That’s an illegitimate move.

If we are to be optimistic that we will find life beyond Earth, we need some reason to think that life is dense. The sheer size of the universe won’t help. Finding that reason, however, is very difficult. There is only one obvious way to do it. We need to develop a theory of how life actually emerges: of the [chemical and environmental conditions](https://iai.tv/articles/cracking-the-creation-of-life-nick-lane-auid-2435) from which it reliably arises. With this in place, we could identify whether those conditions are densely distributed. If they are, then optimism about finding life would be warranted. If they are not, it would not be. For now, though, we don’t have this information. We do not have a well-accepted theory of [how life emerges](https://iai.tv/video/the-agents-of-life); of which conditions reliably give rise to it. Without this, we can’t say whether those conditions are densely distributed or not.

The result is something of a puzzle. Many scientists really do seem to think that a discovery is imminent, and some do so because they are confident that it *is* in fact dense. A good example of this is the new Astronomer Royal, Michele Dougherty; the first female to hold the post in its storied 350-year history. Dougherty has played an important role in developing robotic missions to the moons of Jupiter and Saturn; often considered the best prospects for life beyond Earth in our solar system. In a recent video released by the Royal Society, Dougherty told interviewer Brian Cox that it would be “surprising” if life were not there to be found. This is a model of life’s prevalence that is much more Netherlands than Australia; of life as sufficiently widespread that it is on our very doorstep. The puzzle is *why* Dougherty and others within the community think this, absent a well-accepted theory of how life emerges in the first place.

The existence of life beyond Earth is only one part of the problem. Even if life does exist with sufficient density, we must also be able to [discover and recognize it](https://iai.tv/articles/patterns-are-alive-and-we-are-living-patterns-auid-2919). This is a daunting challenge. The distances involved are staggering; many trillions of miles to even the nearest star. At this scale, even the most sophisticated telescopes will allow us only the scarcest data. We might be fortunate enough to know that a planet has a thick atmosphere, that it orbits near its host star, and even that it possesses a large ocean, or that there are traces of oxygen, or other elements within it. These are important pieces of information. But they are of an order of magnitude too coarse for us to confidently hypothesize that life is present. To do so, we would need to be able to *rule out* that the cause of our data is merely chemical or environmental. Making this call about an alien world trillions of miles away is next to impossible.

Next-generation technologies will allow us to do better. There is much excitement in particular around NASA’s “Habitable Worlds Observatory,” a purpose-built telescope planned for launch in the 2040s. The distances are so large, however, that the odds are still very much against; and this is the case even if life is densely distributed within the universe, which—I think—we have no reason to believe it is.

Optimism about the search for life beyond Earth is, for now, unsupported. The universe may be vast enough to contain it. Whether it is dense enough for us to find it is a question we cannot yet answer. I hope I am wrong, but I do not think we will discover life beyond Earth within my lifetime.
