# Alienation fuels imagination

*Beckett, Bowie, and how to reframe reality*

**By Rodney Sharkey** | *4th July 2024* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,525 words*

![Alienation fuels imagination](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/alienation-fuels-imagination.webp)

---

*Our most-loved artists create alien spaces which help them reframe reality. This alienation resulting from the artist being silenced by existing societal norms defines their creativity and opens up new possibilities beyond the normal, as Professor Rodney Sharkey argues using the examples of David Bowie and Samuel Beckett.*

Both [Samuel Beckett](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Samuel-Beckett) and [David Bowie's](https://www.davidbowie.com/about) creative processes are defined by the effects of alienation, as both felt silenced by normative modes or representation. To express themselves fully, Beckett and Bowie created alternative or alien spaces which helped them reframe their own realities. In doing so, they were able to transgress hitherto normative social values and incite progress in the political and cultural orders of their present day lives. Furthermore, and for contemporary readers and listeners, they leave behind a similarly alien aesthetic; one through which alienated people can see each other beyond the social codes that determine the normal parameters of the current hegemony, while also opening up new alien spaces of potential communion and collective action. Consequently, their aesthetics of alienation create a state of uncertainty for the marginalised other that nonetheless can be used to open up new narratives and new ways of being, individually and collectively.

SUGGESTED VIEWING
Creativity and freedom
With Joanna Kavenna, Myriam François, Jack Symes, Jolyon Maugham](https://iai.tv/video/creativity-and-freedom)

In order to achieve his status as quite the radical modernist, Samuel Beckett had to first break from the archly conservative and pervasive habitus of Irish literary and polite society. He knew that the Celtic mysticism which characterized Irish literature was not for him. Neither was the Irish playwright a fan of the Anglo-Irish ascendancy culture into which he was born. Indeed, as a consequence of his aversion to both mythological Irish blarney and genteel Anglo-Irish society, Seán Kennedy [argues](https://www.cambridge.org/gb/universitypress/subjects/literature/english-literature-after-1945/beckett-and-ireland?format=HB&isbn=9780521111805) that we should think of the Beckett vagrant as engaging in a state of counter-cultural alienation.

\_\_\_

Throughout the Beckett universe, characters are most at home when thoroughly alienated from the normative demands of their environment

\_\_\_

By way of illustration of such counter-conduct, Kennedy offers one Beckett character with a predilection for poking cowpats and another who withdraws into his lodgings to escape social communion altogether. Likewise, the narrator of “[The End](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/308556/the-end-by-beckett-samuel/9780241338971)” is forced to flee the meddling of priests and police who attempt to exercise ostensibly benevolent community interventions that nonetheless demarcate the new Irish free state’s emerging discourses of discipline and control.

More broadly, and throughout the Beckett universe, characters are most at home when thoroughly alienated from the normative demands of their environment. In this regard, Thomas Porter has proposed that “Beckett is an “ausländer”, the German word for an alien who has disassociated from their own heritage without adapting any other.  In Kennedy and Porter’s formulations, “alien” is at once disruptive insider, marginalized outsider *and* de-territorialised “other,” a figure representing multiple subaltern identities incompatible with polite or other forms of habitual society.

\_\_\_

They draw attention to the short-comings of the contemporary cultural moment and present alternative worlds beyond the normal

\_\_\_

Unbeknownst to Beckett at the time, the biopolitics of the Irish free state would eventually be reflected in the emerging, disciplinary architectures of western neoliberalism, his police and priests replaced by the disciplining function of digital surveillance and the aspirational discourses of wellness and resilience. Ironically, the middle classes continue to demonstrate their capacity to adhere to a now failing capitalism by still internalising its ever-evolving behavioural imperatives.

Bowie, too, was quite obviously *alien*. In playing at being Ziggy Stardust in 1972, he embodied alien-ness as metaphor in order to emphasise the presence of other, less conventional identities within English psychic topographies, extending his impact quickly into international psycho-geographies too. As the essay collection [*Enchanting* *Bowie*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/enchanting-david-bowie-9781628923056/) notes, throughout his career, he used the metaphor of outer space as a notional stage on which to “embody gender, race and sexuality, opening up liminal and transgressive capacities of identification” It is in this imaginative alternative cosmos, beyond the terrestrial, that the Bowie fan encounters the alien other.

As definitions of the alien revolve around that which is out of the ordinary, and recognisably other, in a deconstructive moment, the alien is both alien to others while also experiencing alienation itself. Bowie’s otherness, his alienness, synthesises this disjunction. Socially marginal Bowie fans have traditionally seen other Bowie fans as collective inhabitants of an alternative and radically egalitarian human galaxy.

He has served,

specifically, to challenge the normative and bordered ways in which we are asked to survey and regulate ourselves. Bowie’s star[man] body crosses thresholds and boundaries: it is or has been androgynous, polymorphous, transsexual, camp and queer.

(*[Enchanting Bowie](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/enchanting-david-bowie-9781628923056/)*)

In this way, the mama papa alligator bitches that populate his moonage daydream challenge, and continue to challenge, the official spaces of Western culture where regulated identity is housed. Such a strategy echoes Beckett’s deployment of vagabond characters within unreliable narratives staged in de-territorialized theatrical spaces, such strategies illuminating the loss of epistemological certainty felt by those on the margins, unmoored and uncertain in their otherness. In other words, although earth bound, Beckett’s characters lack faith in meta-narratives of teleology and social progress, existing instead in worlds that seem devoid of temporal logic; they are stuck in a type of Beckettian galaxy that is utterly alien to them and in which they struggle, like Vladimir and Estragon, in [*Waiting for Godot*](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Waiting-for-Godot), to acclimatise to its imperatives. Like Bowie, Beckett too is productively *alien*, and engaged in producing counter cultural discourses that have not yet been seamlessly assimilated into capital production. Vladimir and Estragon, again, by way of further example, wait fruitlessly for the work and wages that might help them make sense of the world in which they are otherwise beaten and forgotten. In short, Beckett and Bowie’s creative worlds are counter-cultural in the sense that they draw attention to the short-comings of the contemporary cultural moment and present alternative worlds beyond the normal, wherever, and however, this ever-problematic classification might be employed.

Nabokov and why the moral act is the free act
By Dana Dragunoiu](https://iai.tv/articles/nabokov-and-why-the-moral-act-is-the-free-act-auid-2760)

One such counter-cultural impulse notable in both artists is an obvious tendency towards ruination. Just as Bowie imagines Ziggy, the extra-terrestrial outsider, at first fetishized and adored by his fans, before finally being cannibalized by them, Beckett imagines characters such as Hamm and Clov, locked in a symbiotic apocalyptic death embrace while moving inexorably into disability and, finally, death. Similarly, Bowie’s *embodied* presence is also riddled with physical imperfections and prone to psychic breakdowns. He and his characters have experienced the seductive attractions and the troubling consequences of addiction as they explore the relationship between alterity and mental illness. In this, Bowie’s body is similar to those of many Beckett characters whom their creator referred to as “[falling to bits](https://www.routledge.com/Samuel-Beckett/Federman-Graver/p/book/9780415852708)”.

\_\_\_

\_\_\_

In such ways, Beckett and Bowie are engaged in an aesthetics of ruination; their target the parameters of conventional ideological presuppositions regarding sociality, health and belonging within the capital mode of production. Their work, resolutely outside the false consciousness that dominates this mode, utilizes dilapidated cities, collapsing tenements and a multitude of estranged locations to mirror their shared sense of exclusion, dis-ease and consequent disaffection. As both fold architectural dereliction into a complementary disintegration of mind and body, mental breakdown is therefore produced by the vicissitudes and degradations of the socio-political environment, which wreaks havoc on both the human body and the body politic. However, of the resultant rag and bone society, which trades transformation for calamity, their work manages to communicate a filth lesson regarding destituent ruination as unwelcome conclusion. Conversely, it details how we need to curtail our contemporary unconscious wish for oblivion, and, instead, reimagine an inclusive status quo that begins with adequate housing, with refuge, with social conscience for the other. This is a powerful political paradigm for it creates, among admirers of Beckett and Bowie, the conditions for a recognition of the alien other as co-extensive with the self – a moment in which outsiders recognising their community through art. Historically, communal shifts in hegemonic cultural consciousness generate the need for transformative change within society itself, and today, arguably, greater inclusivity for the underprivileged, for people of colour and for broader gendered sexualities, requires a liberationist consciousness towards which Beckett and Bowie’s work gestures.

Tellingly, in their late work, both move *beyond* an aesthetics of ruination – beyond thoughts of finitude – to an outer space where they explore the idea of humanism giving way to the post-human. Leaving behind Beckett’s ‘little panic steps’ in a ‘ruin strewn land’ estimated to give up the ghost ‘Five Years’ hence, they relocate their creative investigations to the outer limits of the galaxy. Whereas critical thought has often sought to bring the subaltern in from the margins, in “Blackstar” and “Lazarus” and [*Worstward Ho*](https://genius.com/Samuel-beckett-worstward-ho-annotated), Beckett and Bowie push further out in a quest beyond normative and normalizing modes of representation. At a cold interstellar distance, they explore the terrestrial and extra-terrestrial spaces of the universe, here harnessing our incomplete knowledge of being to a radical poetics of being anew; a space where, finally, we may find we are free from alienation at the cost of the very idea of belonging itself.
