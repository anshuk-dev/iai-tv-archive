# It is time for the world to embrace uncertainty

*Beyond the modernist trap of risk and control*

**By Ian Scoones** | *30th October 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,829 words*

![It is time for the world to embrace uncertainty](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/we-need-to-embrace-uncertainty2-copy.webp)

---

*We like to think the future can be predicted, uncertainty eradicated, and that risk can be managed and controlled. But what if this approach to uncertainty doesn't work, never has, and never will? Ian Scoones argues our current technocratic frameworks for managing uncertainty fail to account for the complexities of our world. Instead, we should embrace uncertainty, and learn from people who live with it, to take a more flexible approach to decision-making and resilience.*

Uncertainties are everywhere. Climate change, financial volatility, new technologies, pandemics, disasters. Uncertainties frame our lives. But what are the implications of taking uncertainty seriously, embracing it rather than trying to ignore it?

We are currently stuck in a linear, mechanistic, technocratic risk-based paradigm that fails to address the enormous complexity of today’s turbulent world. This is problematic, and sometimes dangerous. We often act as if our world can be predicted and understood in its totality. But what if the world is dominated by uncertainty and complexity, not risk and stability? What if the modernist systems - of formalised planning, risk management, control systems and so on - just don’t work, and never will? As I argue in my [new book](https://www.politybooks.com/bookdetail?book_slug=navigating-uncertainty-radical-rethinking-for-a-turbulent-world--9781509560073), we need to rethink our approach.

**Distinguishing risk and uncertainty**

How then should we understand uncertainty and distinguish it from risk? Drawing on the work of [Andy Stirling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Pul8mb1ZPE), a simple framework shows how risk is where the probabilities of both outcomes and their likelihoods are known, or can be calculated and predicted, [while uncertainty is where likelihoods are unknown](http://iai.tv/articles/its-time-to-give-up-the-phrase-climate-change-auid-2903), even if we know the potential outcomes. Conditions of uncertainty – and indeed ignorance, where don’t know what we don’t know - are by far the most common situations we encounter in policy and practice, and indeed in daily life. Such knowledge conditions are not amenable to simple risk management.

\_\_\_

Whilst embracing uncertainties can open up debate, requiring deliberation amongst different knowledges and diverse publics, this may be unsettling for those in power, despite the obvious futility of control.

\_\_\_

The problem is that currently, most approaches tend to close down to risk, assuming that futures can be predicted, managed and controlled. For example, in many fields – from [economic policy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X23002449) to [pandemic response](https://issues.org/covid-19-and-the-futility-of-control-in-the-modern-world/) – we often rely on risk models. These are mathematical constructions of reality that attempt to provide a precise risk assessment, yet in practice uncertainty, even ignorance, prevail. What follows are plans, protocols, targets, metrics and, with these, liability law, insurance and so on. All seek closure around a risk-based solution. Such responses emerge through processes of justification, professionalisation and institutionalisation, [deployed by those in power wishing to exert control.](http://iai.tv/video/neoliberalism-a-soviet-nightmare)

Think of any situation where complex, variable situations are being managed, and the pressure to assume risk rather than uncertainty is strong. Whilst embracing uncertainties can open up debate, requiring deliberation amongst different knowledges and diverse publics, this may be unsettling for those in power, despite the obvious futility of control. Yet,  such risk-based framings and practices frequently push us dangerously into zones where knowledge of outcomes is assumed to be known; or at least thought to be able to be estimated, predicted or calculated.

**Opening up to uncertain knowledge: preparing for and responding to disasters**

What though would an alternative be that opens up to uncertain knowledge, and accepts the complexities of a turbulent world? This, I argue, is a major challenge across policy and practice domains the world over.

Let’s take two challenges, where perspectives on uncertainty – not risk – are essential. Many other examples could be taken, and others are explored in the book. First, how should we manage ‘critical infrastructures’ in highly volatile settings? Critical infrastructures are those that deliver important goods and services to society in the face of high levels of input variability. For example, electricity supply is vital for people to heat and light their homes, but balancing a national grid, planning where different methods of generating energy work best and at what times, and constantly changing this supply in the face of unforeseen circumstances is critical.

The key question is how do the goods and services (whether electricity or food) get delivered reliably, and who is involved?  The answer is people who are called ‘reliability professionals’ and their networks – people who can respond both to immediate, day-to-day challenges and also have understandings of and intelligence on the wider system. In the privatised electricity supply system of California, the lights are kept on through networks of highly skilled people including control room engineers, IT professionals, grid operators and many others; all linked through often informal connections. In the same way, in the dryland areas of northern Kenya, livestock keepers must keep their herds alive in highly variable environments, and again rely on networks of professionals. These may be local indigenous weather forecasters, shopkeepers offering small-scale credit, truck operators moving hay or water or local NGO workers able to adapt aid delivery to local contexts.

Such reliability professionals – in any complex infrastructure – are usually massively underappreciated, often ignored as they operate below-the-radar. Yet their role is crucial in averting disasters and ensuring reliability. Continuously every day. Rather than only relying on standard safety protocols and regulatory systems that make use of the repertoires of risk management, reliable flows of goods and services – and wider guarantees of safety – instead emerge through the practices and networks of reliability professionals. They are the unsung heroes and heroines of critical infrastructures.

A similar argument emerges in a second, related challenge: how should we prepare for and respond to disasters? Earthquakes, hurricanes, floods, droughts and so on seem to dominate the headlines, and there’s a huge industry focused on disaster risk reduction and response. The [UN Sendai framework](https://www.undrr.org/implementing-sendai-framework/what-sendai-framework) encapsulates this – but once again this is largely focused on managing risk and improving prediction through modelling the likelihood of disasters and preparing response plans, rather than the necessity of navigating uncertainties in real time.

\_\_\_

This is not an argument for letting go of expertise and technology and assuming that local people can manage on their own. But, instead of imposing solutions from outside, working with what people already do, supporting existing networks and increasing response options is a more appropriate way forward.

\_\_\_

How then do people manage disasters, such as major droughts, on the ground? Take the case of pastoralists in southern Ethiopia. Externally imposed technological and financial risk management solutions tend to dominate - early warning systems, anticipatory action projects or de-risking through livestock insurance, for example. But these are often not trusted by local people and frequently fail to integrate well with local experiences of multiple, intersecting and unfolding uncertainties.

Pastoralists rarely see disasters in the same way as external experts. These are not singular events amenable to prediction and control, but unfolding processes that are always uncertain. In contrast to the top-down risk management approaches often advocated, local responses are rooted in social relationships, forms of collective solidarity, moral economies based on social networks and so rely on vernacular forms of resilience building.  This is not an argument for letting go of expertise and technology and assuming that local people can manage on their own. But, instead of imposing solutions from outside, working with what people already do, supporting existing networks and increasing response options is a more appropriate way forward. This in turn suggests a very different approach to disaster preparedness and response, much more suited to an uncertain world.

**Radical rethinking for a turbulent world**

Whether in relation to managing reliability in critical infrastructures or disaster preparedness and response – and [many other examples](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/39938) besides - a very different approach emerges if uncertainty – where we don’t know the likelihoods of future outcomes – is taken seriously. Current approaches however are too often constrained by a commitment to a modernist vision of risk management and control. This often blocks alternative approaches that are reliant on relationships, networks and collective organisation, which are able to respond flexibly and adaptively to diverse uncertainties.

Why is it necessary to argue for a shift to a new paradigm of uncertainty? Current policy frames – whether around infrastructures, disasters, humanitarian responses and more broadly economic, environmental and development policy – are too often trapped in a risk paradigm, centred on a dominant, narrow vision of modernity constructed through a paradigm of control. And the problem is - it really doesn’t work. The challenge then is to break free of the disciplining constraints of the modernist trap of the risk paradigm: to recast and reimagine; to ‘think without a banister’ in the words of [Hannah Arendt](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/thinking-itself-is-dangerous/).

The good news is that there are lots of experiences to draw from. There are many people who have long grappled with uncertainty who we can all learn from. These are small-scale farmers, mobile livestock-keepers, delta dwellers, inshore fisher people, swidden cultivators, but also control room engineers, local government officials and front-line workers all grappling with uncertain conditions day-to-day. As with the examples above, we must learn from, listen to and engage with those who live with and indeed from uncertainty.

\_\_\_

This may lead us to embrace ideas from past perspectives that took a more open, holistic approach, as well as from other non-Western cultures that embrace uncertainty through notions of cyclicity, regeneration, impermanence, plurality and multiple paths.

\_\_\_

We must equally ask whether current policy and practice frameworks address uncertainty or whether they close down to risk? Are there traditions out there that might help in recasting our often-narrow disciplinary, sectoral perspectives?  This may lead us to embrace ideas from past perspectives that took a more open, holistic approach, as well as from other non-Western cultures that embrace uncertainty through notions of cyclicity, regeneration, impermanence, plurality and multiple paths.

All this suggests a very different politics. The politics of risk, as we have seen, are centred on calculative, technocratic control, where we assume we can predict, model and manage towards stability, optimality and equilibrium. Such politics emerge both from centrist, state-controlled systems as well as individualised, marketised, financialised neoliberal approaches. Instead, a politics of uncertainty must be centred on distributed, decentralised, collective responses, involving diverse sources of knowledge that together can contribute to new perspectives on the future, responding to intersecting uncertainties in diverse contexts through multiple pathways.

As the world grapples with unprecedented turbulence giving rise to uncertainties across policy and practice domains, it is essential that we release ourselves from the strictures of a narrow risk-based vision and seek out alternatives that embrace uncertainty. As [Rebecca Solnit](https://www.newstatesman.com/environment/2023/07/rebecca-solnit-climate-despair-hope) argues, rather than following the ‘cheerleaders of despair’, a more hopeful vision is offered:

“What motivates us to act is a sense of possibility within uncertainty – that the outcome is not yet fully determined and our actions may matter in shaping it. …. If we can recognise that we don’t know what will happen, that the future does not yet exist but is being made in the present, then we can be moved to participate in making that future….”

This is the radical rethinking linked to a new collective politics that is required to navigate uncertainty in today’s turbulent world.
