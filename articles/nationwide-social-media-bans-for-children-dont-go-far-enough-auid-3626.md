# Nationwide social media bans for children don't go far enough

*Without serious regulation, Big Tech is let off the hook*

**By Tony D. Sampson** | *20th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,892 words*

![Nationwide social media bans for children don't go far enough](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/nationwide-social-media-bans-for-children-dont-go-far-enough.webp)

---

*Bans on social media for under-16s are sweeping the world — from Australia to Spain, Malaysia and beyond — but digital theorist **Tony D. Sampson** argues they don't go far enough. The problem is that prohibition dodges the harder fight: taking on the platform business models built to capture attention through infinite scroll, variable-reward notifications and opaque algorithms. What we need is not to stop short of the platforms but to go straight at them, forcing companies to redesign addictive features and open up their algorithms through consumer law, and making critical digital literacy a core part of every child's education.*

My response to the bans may surprise some readers. I’ve authored several theory books critical of [digital culture’s emerging toxic politics, social anxieties, and declining user experiences](https://www.mast-journal.org/2020vol1no22). However, this work needs to be read as an antidote to the raw optimism that greeted the World Wide Web in the late 1990s. Back then, many commentators hailed a democratic digitopia; an online Agora, a virtual public sphere where distance and hierarchy dissolved. As Web 1.0 became 2.0, new platforms like Twitter and Facebook were celebrated as liberation technologies: e-democracy in action that stirred rebellions like the Arab Spring into action. [When Daniel Dennett championed a Cambrian explosion of universal transparency](https://www.jstor.org/stable/26046362?seq=1), few anticipated the dangerous contagions of conspiracy, misinformation, fake news, or brain-rot that would follow. Ethical debates at the time centered on computer viruses and bugs that threatened network security, while societal anxieties were largely confined to the so-called digital have-nots: those cut off from flourishing flows of information.

Significantly, what we termed [the “dark side” of digital culture](https://archive.org/details/spambookonviruse0000unse) never claimed information technologies were inherently evil. Its aim was to shed light on problematic promises of transparency and empowerment; promises increasingly displaced by a rampant marketing ideology fixated on algorithmically curated viral growth. A platform economy emerged, compulsively designed to capture, optimize, and monetize user attention. These business models changed everything. Built atop the Internet’s military infrastructure, commercial social networks grew exponentially through the 2000s, as marketing strategies rapidly evolved to realize their enormous economic potential. Today’s social media landscape would scarcely be recognizable to early optimists. [The potential of e-democracy has been supplanted by a corporate free-for-all](https://iai.tv/video/technology-and-freedom), overseen by billionaire “feudal” lords. Meanwhile, open access to information has become ensnared in a [regulatory vacuum](https://iai.tv/video/controlling-the-tech-titans), filled with scams, deception, endless scrolling, toxic content, and online harms.

[A Post-COVID intensification of screen dependency](https://www.boundary2.org/2020/04/tony-d-sampson-and-jussi-parikka-the-new-logics-of-viral-media/) brought these negative user experiences into sharp relief. A generation of young people who had spent lockdown years mediated almost entirely through screens re-entered a world of rising parental concern and a gathering mental health crisis narrative. The current political mobilization of this anxiety as justification for an era of digital prohibition is perhaps unsurprising, but it poses exclusionary dangers, above all abandoning children to a rudderless digital life. Following Australia’s precedent, the contagious logic of a convenient yet flawed fix has rapidly spread. Indeed, [a growing academic community](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/events/2026/jun/report-launch-smartphone-free-schools) is profoundly concerned that this rushed and largely evidence-free move toward prohibition may actually be harmful to both children and democracy, since depriving young people of guided resilience leaves them unequipped to navigate and change the digital world they inherit as adults.

Political context matters here. When the UK announced statutory, Ofsted-monitored smartphone bans in schools in April 2026 and wider under-16 social media prohibition in June 2026, Keir Starmer’s collapsing leadership needed to appear decisive. The two bans were presented as distinct yet function as a single political package: the need for visible, rapid action on an issue where public feeling is intense, and the optics of inaction are more desirable than the optics of complex policy solutions. Both bans are politically convenient because they do not require policymakers to confront the platform business models that generate the toxicities and harms they claim to address.

As genuinely felt as it is, parental anxiety can also become a politically convenient evidential foundation. “Protecting children” deploys a rhetoric of blame that erases nuance and deliberation. Nobody campaigns against child safety! It is one of the stickiest affective frames in political culture. Its rhetoric forecloses scrutiny of whether the policy actually achieves what it claims, [mobilizing parental fear](https://iai.tv/video/danger-safety-and-the-future) ahead of practical assessments of evidence. It functions as a conversation-stopper rather than a policy argument.

Prohibition is also symbolically convenient precisely because its logic does the work that the evidence cannot and so avoids the harder, costlier, and politically riskier confrontation with platform business models. Regulating these infrastructures of compulsive engagement requires sustained political determination, significant legal resources, and a willingness to take on some of the most powerful corporations and politicians in the world. Banning a teenager’s phone and access to YouTube requires none of these things. Together, both bans place most of the burden of regulation on parents, children and schools rather than on the architecture of the platforms themselves. The school gate, it turns out, is easier to police than Silicon Valley.

Politicians are also selectively recruiting evidence, amplifying findings that support the ban while sidelining the body of work that complicates the picture. The research evidence for the bans is, to put it plainly, fully contested, not settled. [Candice Odgers’ (2024) *Nature* critical review](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2) of Jonathan Haidt’s *The Anxious Generation* provides a clear account of where the science stands. Hundreds of researchers have searched for the kind of large causal effects that Haidt’s thesis requires, and their efforts have produced a mix of no, small, and mixed associations. Most data are correlative. When associations over time are found, they suggest not that social media use predicts or causes depression, but that young people who already have mental health problems use platforms more often or differently. An analysis across more than 70 countries found no consistent or measurable associations between well-being and the global roll-out of social media.

Young people are conspicuously absent from policy consultation processes. Addressing this oversight, [Jessica Ringrose, Edith Rodda, and Dawn Aytoun’s (2026) study](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10227549/1/Ringrose%20Rodda%20Aytoun%20-%20Smartphone%20Free%20Schools.pdf), covering 732 pupils aged 11 to 18, found that 75% of young people disagreed with blanket banning, compared with 87% of educators and 88% of parents who supported it. Under the strictest policies, pupils brought substitute devices, circumvented pouches, and moved problematic behavior outside school hours and institutional awareness. Again, prohibition drives risk underground rather than eliminating it. Harmful incidents do not disappear. They become invisible to educators.

Significantly though, in Ringrose et al.’s study, pupils did not simply reject regulation; they asked for structured, proportionate governance that reflected their lived experiences. What I find most troubling about this revelation is the negative impact of prohibition on developing young people’s sense of agency. By constructing them as passive victims of technology, the bans deny the agency and expertise children already exercise in their own digital lives. Politicians may try to make prohibition appear to be a system of care, but it produces exactly the opposite of what care requires: visibility, trust, and the supporting framework of critical, informed engagement.

**What needs to happen now**

The alternative to prohibition is not permissiveness. It is a fundamentally different model of governance that targets the platforms rather than the users, builds critical capacity rather than enforcing absence, and treats young people as agents rather than victims.

Algorithmic transparency is the most urgent requirement. The opacity of algorithmic systems is not a technical inevitability; it is a commercial design choice made in the service of engagement optimization, and it can and must be regulated. All users (not just those under 16) have a right to understand how content is curated, ranked, and served to them, and on what basis.

Broader platform design accountability must follow. The persuasive technologies embedded in social media—the infinite scrolling, variable reward notifications, and social validation loops—are not neutral features. They are compulsion architectures, deliberately engineered to capture and hold attention regardless of the cost to the user. Designing these mechanisms out of platforms, or making them subject to rigorous regulatory scrutiny, would protect all users from compulsive tendencies.

How does this happen? Two recent successful US legal actions, which approached online harm through the lens of consumer law, might provide some valuable lessons for the further development of existing UK regulatory frameworks. In New Mexico, [Meta and Google were found by a jury to have misled consumers over platform safety](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/cmte_testimony/2026/wam/1mOzhn89ToaxSTc2UML6KsV3lp6euI5_g.pdf), in breach of state consumer protection laws. A [parallel case in California](https://abcnews.com/GMA/News/jury-returns-verdict-meta-youtube-landmark-social-media/story?id=131121474) points to a broader reorientation away from content-based concerns and prohibition to the accountability of platforms for their own distinct user experience architectures.

Critical digital literacy is the third essential reform. It is not a supplementary curriculum add-on but a core civic education. This means teaching not just how to use platforms but how platforms use us. Users need to understand how algorithms are designed to capture attention, how persuasive design exploits compulsion, and what critically informed digital participation might actually look like.

In many ways, the often-maligned study of media in schools already provides a vital space for under-16s to critically engage with most of these issues. Beyond search engine optimization, media studies should introduce students to ethical user experience design issues, helping them understand not just how platforms work, but how they are often built to shape behavior. Similarly, early business education could be reframed to foreground corporate responsibility and consumer protection in the digital realm, equipping young people to recognize manipulative practices as much as entrepreneurial opportunities.

Crucially, this need not be a top-down education exercise. As [Rebecca Coleman and Jessica Ringrose argue](https://spir.aoir.org/ojs/index.php/spir/article/view/15095/12217), a more ambitious co-creation of policy guidance and critical digital literacy, one that positions young people as active agents in their own digital lives, offers a far more inclusive path. Such an approach does more than educate; it recognizes the “[diverse identities and vulnerabilities](https://spir.aoir.org/ojs/index.php/spir/article/view/15095/12217)“ of young users; it builds the trust needed to make harmful behavior visible rather than concealed. And that, surely, is what genuine care looks like.

**What the future could and should look like**

The long-term stakes of getting this wrong are not merely personally damaging; they are dangerous for democracy. What is required is a political shift: from protecting passive children from evil technology to cultivating a generation of critically literate and activist digital citizens. We need young people equipped not merely to use platforms but to struggle against them, challenge them, and ultimately reshape them. This is the democratic impetus that prohibition excludes. Future platforms worth inhabiting are those designed, [in Lauren Berlant’s terms](https://read.dukeupress.edu/books/book/1596/Cruel-Optimism), for flourishing human experience rather than for the extraction and optimization of user engagement. That vision is not a digitopia. That place never existed. It is a design and regulatory choice, currently deferred by short-term political convenience and corporate self-interest.

It is not the age of the user, but the business model, that needs to become the primary target of regulation. A generation of young people reaching adulthood having been banned from digital public life without ever being given [the tools to navigate it critically](https://iai.tv/articles/navigating-reality-in-the-misinformation-age-auid-2849) is not a generation that has been protected. It is a generation that has been left without the critical apparatus to act in an environment architecturally designed to evade criticality. This struggle for user experience will not be won by abandoning under-16s. It will be won by encouraging them to hold platforms and politicians to account.
