# 9/11 and the genealogy of conspiracy theories

*Conspiracy theories pretend to be forensic, but are actually theological*

**By Andrew McKenzie-McHarg** | *9th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,362 words*

![9/11 and the genealogy of conspiracy theories](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/9-11-final.webp)

---

*Most see 9/11 conspiracies as an aberration in American political life. But historian* ***Andrew McKenzie-McHarg****, author of* The Hidden History of Conspiracy Theory*, argues that there’s nothing surprising here. Investigating the genealogy of conspiracy theories, McKenzie-McHarg argues they separate into two basic types: credible scenarios in a criminal or journalistic investigation; or apocalyptic demonologies in secular garb, replacing Satan and his forces of evil with historical groups. When conspiracy theories fall into this second type, framing the world as a cosmic battle between good and evil, they start to become dangerous.*

As the day of September 11, 2001 drew to a close, one thing felt certain amid all the uncertainty: nothing would be the same again. However, a full quarter-century has passed since the events of that day. A distance in time of this magnitude opens up new perspectives. In particular, it becomes possible to appreciate how the terrorist attacks mark less a radical before-and-after moment and instead find their place within a long-term continuity. This is the case if one considers the numerous conspiracy theories that emerged in the months and years after the attacks. Proponents and advocates of these theories came to be known collectively as the “truthers,” though in fact they could have been more accurately labeled as “doubters”; instead of agreeing upon a truth, they bonded over a radical doubt about the official version of events. Nineteen al-Qaeda operatives hijacked passenger planes, transforming them into missiles directed at nodal points of American political and financial power? According to the truthers, that was simply what the authorities wanted you to believe.

Conspiracy theories have been with us for a very long time. Exactly how long is hard to determine—the answer to that question depends on how one defines a conspiracy theory. Yet questions of definition diminish in importance if one instead tries to craft a genealogy that looks at the various ingredients of a conspiracy theory. One such ingredient is a profound sense of the presence of evil in the world. In this regard, 9/11 conspiracy theories occupy an intermediate position in the history of this mode of interpretation. This becomes apparent if one recalls how, from the late 1960s, skepticism about the official version of how President Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas in 1963 congealed into suspicions that the CIA might have played a part in the crime. More recently, we have been witness to the QAnon movement fantasizing about a cabal that had lodged itself deep within the government and that routinely engaged in unspeakable abominations. The 9/11 conspiracy theories that railed against the ruthlessness and cynicism of the administration led by President George W. Bush sit somewhere in the middle when it comes to the depth of wickedness they posit: more than a rogue branch of an intelligence agency but less than an irredeemably depraved deep state.

Of course, it is not unreasonable to argue that, juiced up on its neoconservative ideology, the Bush administration did indeed act ruthlessly and even cynically after 9/11. It responded to the attacks by treating them as a geopolitical opportunity. Such an admission hints at the trickiness of dealing with conspiracy theories more generally; they can point to valid concerns and articulate suspicions that are not entirely misplaced. Where does one draw the line? On occasions, truthers were given to engaging in debates about this more philosophical question. Bush and others had condemned the “outrageous conspiracy theories” that the truthers peddled, which insinuated some kind of inside job behind the attacks. The truthers retorted by declaring the official version itself to be an outrageous conspiracy theory. After all, it posited a narrative that involved coordinated action between al-Qaeda agents (in other words, a conspiracy!) and that, in the minds of the truthers, [simply didn’t stack up if one looked carefully at the evidence](https://iai.tv/articles/distrust-the-ideas-you-are-most-invested-in-auid-3695).

In such moments, how one defines—and who gets to define—what a conspiracy theory is became [part of a meta-debate](https://iai.tv/video/evidence-in-the-dock) that spins off from the more general debate about who ultimately is to blame. Yet as already indicated, the definitional approach has proven itself to be time and again a dead-end. The questions generated by the search for a definition have never been satisfactorily resolved, either in the context of 9/11 or in other contexts that have compelled us to grapple with conspiracy theories. My own research background encouraged me to come at the problem from a different angle, an alternative approach which involved two shifts: stop thinking about the thing itself and instead think about the concept, and stop chasing a definition and instead think about a genealogy. It was not as if the question “what is a conspiracy theory?” had become obsolete. It was simply that a promising route to answering that question entailed the attempt to answer a preliminary question: what elements have been absorbed into our concept of conspiracy theory?

Rather than being an airy-fairy idea, a concept is tied to words. So where do we find people first fusing the words “conspiracy” and “theory” into the composite term “conspiracy theory”? My efforts at piecing together this story located two sites where the term originated and thus revealed a forked genealogy. On the one hand, detectives, lawyers, and journalists started to combine the words “conspiracy” and  “theory” in the late nineteenth century as a more-or-less neutral, technical term to denote a particular line of inquiry or argument that, for example, in the context of an assassination, distinguished itself from the position that there had only been one lone assassin. On the other hand, the term emerged once more in the distinct context of mid-twentieth-century social-science critiques directed at ideologies that dressed up apocalyptic demonologies in secular garb. In other words, older habits of invoking Satan, the Devil, Lucifer, Antichrist, or some other mythical figure spearheading the forces of evil, ceded to more “enlightened” and modern condemnations of a verifiably historical group—freemasons, Jews, communists, capitalists, and so on. These real groups stepped into the breach and took on the equivalent role of archvillain in the unfolding drama of history

In my view, we best understand conspiracy theory when we acknowledge not only how these two contexts have fed into [the concept of conspiracy theory](https://iai.tv/articles/how-do-conspiracy-theories-come-about-auid-1235) but also how this dual inheritance is reflected in the corresponding real-world phenomenon. On this basis, it becomes easier to understand why conspiracy theories come in such diverse shapes and sizes; some of them are indebted more to the tradition of forensic analysis, some of them are enthralled to visions of the apocalypse as an imminent and climactic clash of good and evil. Yet it also accounts for our conflicted attitude towards conspiracy theories; both attraction and repulsion play a part, often in equal measure. Some find conspiracy theories intriguing and entertaining, others declare them to be problematic and dangerous, while most of us struggle with some uneasy combination of both tendencies.

As I tried to transform this research into a book, a colleague suggested a title: [*The Hidden History of Conspiracy Theory*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691177069/the-hidden-history-of-conspiracy-theory?srsltid=AU7gw4XAyxXWmMmTTjeha_hikBkDW3SzL8VkgJEcGdg7LlAxo8kd7RIV). I adopted it, not least because it had something alluringly self-referential; after all, pulling the curtains back on a “hidden history” is what conspiracy theorists claim to do. However, it was only after completing the book that I discovered a collected volume published twenty years earlier with the title *The Hidden History of 9/11*. To what degree does *The Hidden History of Conspiracy Theory* and the genealogy it reconstructs shed light specifically on *The Hidden History of 9/11* and the form of contrarian scholarship this earlier volume offers?

One contributor to *The Hidden History of 9/11* was David Ray Griffin, a former professor at the Claremont School of Theology who, in his retirement, had advanced to become the unofficial “dean” of the truther movement. The chapter represented for him an opportunity to realize a wish that he had not been able to indulge in his other publications on this topic, namely to get into the weeds and marshal all the evidence that the World Trade Center towers in New York had not collapsed due to the impact of the planes and the resulting fires, but rather due to controlled demolitions. In fact, immersion in abstruse minutiae is typical of much truther literature; it often revolves around highly technical matters: the temperatures at which jet fuel combusts and structural steel melts, how—and how fast—high-rise office buildings collapse, what patterns can one expect plane wreckage to exhibit when it litters the site of impact, whether mobile phones could send and receive calls at high altitudes, as would seem to be the case if the calls from passengers on the hijacked planes were genuine, and so on.

Admittedly, the encounter with this material posed a challenge to the general characterization of conspiracy theory I had crafted: what does any of this have to do with secularized legacy of belief in Satan and the forces of evil? Of course, an inadequate but also not entirely inaccurate response is to declare an implied presence of this tradition in this discourse. A belief that Bush and his “neocon cronies” had plotted massive attacks against the American homeland and covered their tracks by making the attacks look like the work of fanatical al-Qaeda operatives far exceeded the low opinion that someone might normally harbor for political opponents in a two-party system.

This charge of diabolical duplicity was there by implication in Griffin’s suspicions and those of the truthers more generally. But I zeroed in on Griffin because if anyone was disposed to making the implicit explicit, then in this context a former theologian who had professionally grappled with the theological problem of evil seemed like a promising candidate. For this reason, *Christian Faith and the Truth behind 9/11: A Call to Reflection and Action*, which Griffin published in 2006, proves to be a highly enlightening read. In the book’s first part, Griffin offers historical, political, and technical analysis suggesting “that 9/11 was a false-flag operation.” This was all consistent with the vast majority of truther discourse. However, in the second part of the book, Griffin indicated that he was shifting gears and changing registers. What he requested of readers for this part was a “willingness to engage in a very different kind of discourse” built on “radically different concepts.” These concepts were the “divine” and the “demonic”—in other words, concepts far removed from discussions about, for example, how structural steel might or might not be expected to buckle at high temperatures. Borrowing a term from fellow theologian Jeffrey Burton Russell, Griffin advocated a “semidualistic monotheism” that accorded the demonic some degree of autonomy and agency, even if this degree was inferior to that possessed by the divine. He was adamant that this position aligned with the core message of Christian tradition because it recognized “our world” to be “the scene of a deadly battle between divine and demonic power.”

The sophisticated elaboration of this dualism is exceedingly rare within the literature produced by the truthers, but the dualism itself very much informs the way they—and, I would argue, conspiracy theorists more generally—have viewed the world. It is admittedly important to note how Griffin speaks of the “demonic”— and not of actual demons or a singular demon. This preference for the adjective corresponds with the shift from an older mythological view marked by a habit of personifying evil to a realistic view that recognized evil as a genuine force within the world without having to condense it into a supernatural figure.

Conspiracy theories seem to have become stuck at a halfway house in this shift from the mythological to the real. Yet the reactions to 9/11 also offered glimpses of the older, more purely mythological view. On the morning of the attacks, the photographer Mark Phillips rushed to the roof of his Brooklyn apartment building to capture the sight of the World Trade Center towers that were spewing smoke from their gashed facades. One image that he took not on film but with a digital camera—and that would therefore expose itself to later charges of manipulation—was picked up by the Associated Press and distributed over the wire. A day later, Phillip’s email account was flooded with requests for him to respond to the fact that this particular photo had seemingly recorded the grim visage of Satan himself, complete with horns and a pointy beard, as formed by the smoke billowing from the South Tower.

I am yet to encounter in any of the truther texts a reference to the “Satan in the Smoke” photo (as it came to be known). Presumably most of them sensed that what was at work here was a superstitious projection; to ascribe it any meaning would be to revert to an older mythological understanding of evil. Phillips’s photo can serve therefore at most as a metaphor—but a powerful one because in all the suspicions and speculation that rose like smoke from the deep skepticism with which the truthers came to regarded both the events of that day, one can indeed make out the shape of a semidualistic monotheism. In this case, the devil was not so much in the detail but rather in the implications that framed myriad, highly technical details and that, on a deeper level, explained why all these details supposedly told a very different story to the one the authorities wanted us to believe.

Where conspiracy theories are the products of open-ended and open-minded investigation, they are necessary and are indeed to be encouraged. However, signs that such an explanation is becoming enmeshed in the myth of an imminent cosmic battle between good and evil [are to be taken as red flags](https://iai.tv/video/conspiracy-and-belief). For such explanations are no longer mere explanations. Instead, they are in the process of being weaponized into forms of action that can easily fracture the comity on which a democratic society rests.
