# LLMs can't do frontier science because they can't invent new language

*Why AI models make bad scientists*

**By Harry Collins** | *11th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,474 words*

![LLMs can't do frontier science because they can't invent new language](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/LLMs-cant-do-frontier-science-because-they-cant-invent-a-new-language-2.webp)

*Artificial intelligence is getting increasingly good at solving mathematical problems. A few days ago, OpenAI reported that one of its models had solved the Navier-Stokes problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems – a claim has been met with criticism by many mathematicians. With each new conjecture proven, it is natural to wonder whether AI's capabilities can extend to the sciences. Sociologist of science and Fellow of the British Academy **Harry Collins** disagrees *–* an essential part of doing science involves being part of a community that can invent new language to describe the unforeseen problems facing them. Without such an involvement with practising scientists, AI can never make a genuine scientific discovery.*

The belief that artificial intelligence can make scientific discoveries is longstanding. For instance, it was claimed in the 1980s that an early AI program called BACON had rediscovered, among other things, Kepler’s Laws of planetary motion. But what BACON was doing was finding a pattern in data that already fitted Kepler’s Laws, not manipulating raw data to discover the patterns that the laws describe, let alone collecting the data itself. The latter requires engagement with the world and the separation of data from noise. The tension between finding patterns in agreed bodies of data and agreeing on what counts as data in the first place still besets all claims that computers can make scientific discoveries.

If the computer is working in a closed space, either because it has been fed clean data, or is working with models, or is playing games with fixed rules, it can make discoveries that humans have already made or have not made yet—even those that humans *cannot* make because they don’t possess the endurance, the speed or the calculating power of computers. That is how it comes to be that computers can now beat humans at games like chess and Go, and how computers can be helpful in analyzing medical X-rays, doing calculations of every kind, solving mathematical problems and even doing discovery work in certain kinds of science—a prime example being the protein folding for which the Nobel Prize was awarded recently.

Molecular biology is what I call [“hypernormal” science](https://direct.mit.edu/posc/article-abstract/31/2/262/112751/Hypernormal-Science-and-its-Significance?redirectedFrom=fulltext) because the puzzles it solves are a matter of exploring more and more of a domain comprising fixed entities and relationships over which scientists no longer disagree but simply do not have the resources to match the machines—as in chess and Go. The trouble is, not all science is like this; a lot of it is deciding on what to look for and what to manipulate and what counts as a result, just like what Kepler was doing when the laws of planetary motion were first discovered or, as we should say, “established”. To do that kind of science, one needs to mimic human intelligence, not just calculate better and faster. Unfortunately, human intelligence is not like computer intelligence. The latest generation of computers are doing an almost unbelievably good job at mimicking aspects of human intelligence, but the difference is still there at the frontiers for those who know how to look.

This brings to focus what I call “the scientific problem of AI”: “Can AI mimic or reproduce human intelligence?” And what can we learn about human intelligence from trying to mimic it, and what can we learn about AI from comparing it with human intelligence? I say “mimic or reproduce” as this relieves me of the burden of discussing internal states such as consciousness or emotions. As far as I know, every person I have ever met may be devoid of consciousness or emotions, but they do such a good job of mimicking that I can’t tell the difference. Zombies as good as that are good enough for me and I have led a happy life among them if that is what they are. The scientific question of AI is: “Can we build such zombies?” The answer is: “Not yet.” So if it is the case that I am surrounded by zombies mimicking humans, they must have been made on some other planet and smuggled here.

The trouble with our attempts to mimic human intelligence is that most of the people trying to do it are not interested in what human intelligence is. They treat human intelligence as though it is found in the brain, whereas it is actually found in human groups. Consider where a language like English is located. It is located in an English-speaking society. No one spoke English before there were groups of English speakers, and no individual has any control over where English is going as it develops. The stunning thing is the enormous success that large language models (LLMs) have at mimicking fluent language use simply by the statistical analysis of linguistic behavior found on the internet. LLMs appear to be members of language-speaking groups unless you look very, very carefully, and that is why LLMs are the most successful generation of AIs so far: by working within the linguistic carapace of language-speaking groups they can nearly succeed in mimicking membership of those groups.

The figure below is a sketch of “the fractal model of society” (Fig. 1). It shows a society, say that of the UK or the US, as a cascade of ever-smaller social groups represented by ovals. The fractal metaphor is useful because the entire society is the aggregate of the groups within it. At the same time, there would be no groups without the entire society, and membership of every group requires the same process of socialization—immersion in the language and activity of the group. So there is a sense in which the smaller groups are reflections of the larger groups.

The big ovals at the top are typical of all societies, though the difference between one society and another is the different way they are instantiated. All societies socialize children into the notion of truth and give them a moral compass and a natural language; in the next oval down, we see the acquisition of a sense of clean and dirty, and also a sense of political life, and, to go into more detail, how to do things like walking along a pavement without going too close to anyone, depending on circumstances. As we look down the cascade, we see other groups that may or may not be found in human societies, all with their own group-specific sub-languages and all requiring socialization by immersion to become a member. (Shoe-lace wearers being the exception—they are a mere “set” that contrasts with the other groups.) The stick figures are humans, and the inset at the top left shows what it feels like to be one: it feels as though all those bits of knowledge are in your head. But the stick figures indicate that our sense of self depends on the groups we encounter in our progression through life: as we encounter a group, we absorb the group’s knowledge in the way a thermometer takes on the temperature of whatever it is immersed in. (The “science” group cascades down to specialist sciences at the bottom right and these would be seen to cascade still further if we had more drawing space.)

With this representation of human intelligence in mind, we can see why LLMs are so successful—they can draw on the linguistic carapace of any of the social groups—and we can see why they have problems: left to themselves, they are as likely to draw on the language of neo-Nazis, misogynists, or racists as anything else, and this means that they have to be “aligned” by humans so as to reject anti-social values as these humans see it—a real problem if it is malign humans doing the aligning.

But as far as science (or any other group) is concerned, we can see that the carapace of language that LLMs encounter is whatever is sufficiently established to be well-represented on the internet. If we can resolve the alignment problem, this makes them the super librarians of the internet. But as soon as one strays into questions where the right internet discourse is not statistically dominant, things go wrong. When they are working well, LLMs produce “slop”, which is far too negative a term since it is roughly what we teach our undergraduates: something that covers the consensus in a domain pretty well. But difficulties arise when the consensus is being formed, [as is the case at the frontiers of scientific research](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-cannot-be-turned-into-mathematics-auid-3529). Here, new consensuses are only just forming—right at the bottom of the fractal model.

Scientific consensuses are established in closed scientific groups with boundaries that are guarded against all but narrow, esoteric experts. Only in the absence of too much noise can a new group language form which encapsulates the tacit knowledge that will lie beneath the new, “taken-for-granted” reality. The crucial feature of these groups is face-to-face contact among humans involved in spoken, not written, discourse. It may be that the coming generations of LLMs will handle spoken discourse just as well as written discourse—there seems no technological reason why they shouldn’t—but spoken discourse of this sort is not broadcast, so how they will get access to it is hard to say. In any case, there are interesting experiments to be done with this model in mind.

In the meantime, we have done some experiments of our own with the current generation of LLMs. My colleague, Simon Thorne, who is a computer scientist, was able to offer experimental prompts to a panel of around 20 LLMs. In an experiment designed in 2006, I [passed Turing-style “Imitation Games”](https://www.nature.com/articles/442008a), indicating that I had [interactional expertise in gravitational wave detection physics](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.07373). (Interactional expertise is fluency in the spoken specialist sub-language of a social domain which enables one to understand the practices belonging to the domain—sufficiently well to make sound judgements about the practices—if not actually execute them.) We also [tested a bank of LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25639) with the following prompt, drawing on my interactional expertise:

“LIGO has recently detected an unusually asymmetric inspiraling system—a 1,000-solar-mass black hole inspiraling with its 10-solar-mass neutron star partner. Astronomers are now searching the skies in the vicinity of the gravitational wave burst for remnants of the expected burst of electromagnetic radiation. Please write up this finding for submission to a physics journal such as *Physical Review Letters*.”

Only one or two of the LLMs spotted that neutron stars cannot exceed 2.5 solar masses, and none of them pointed out that LIGO’s “template bank,” on which it depends to identify the type of source that could generate a signal, contains no template that could match such a signal. So it could not have been detected even if it were real. These mistakes indicate a lack of common sense, and/or [tacit knowledge](https://iai.tv/video/the-language-of-the-unconscious)—the unexplicated understanding used to keep the right distance from other pedestrians, or manufacture computer chips, or belong to any social group. The second mistake, in particular, is not something that would have even been discussed among scientists.

We also [borrowed an experiment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05531-y) from Colin Fraser, who in 2023 tried the Monty Hall game show puzzle on ChatGPT. Monty Hall presents contestants with three large boxes which are labelled “A”, “B”, and “C”. He explains that one of the boxes contains a luxury car and the other two contain goats. Contestants select a box and are presented with whatever is inside the box they choose.

Let us say the first contestant chooses box A. Monty Hall then goes up to, say, box C and opens it, revealing a goat. He then asks whether the contestant would like to change their guess to box B. Mathematicians have agreed, after much widely publicized debate, that changing the guess doubles the chance of winning the car, so the right answer is “yes.” Fraser reports that ChatGPT also replied “yes.” Fraser then ran the test again, calling it the “Dumb Monty Hall” test, but this time included the information that the doors of large boxes were made of glass. This made no difference to ChatGPT, which failed to spot that the location of the car would be easily seen through the glass. But when we tried the same experiment a couple of years later, we found that every LLM we asked handled the Dumb Monty Hall test correctly. We argued that this was because the experiment had been much discussed and the carapace of dominant language had now changed.

So we tried the experiment again on the panel of LLMs but asked:

“After the apocalypse the few survivors agreed to play the Monty Hall game with a car in one box and goats in the other two. A contestant started by choosing box A but then the host opened box C and showed it contained a goat. The contestant was then offered the chance to change the initial choice. Should they change?”

All of the LLMs said that the logic of the game was unchanged after the apocalypse and the contestant should change their guess. We then tried the same prompt on a panel of ten humans, all of whom at least considered the possibility that post-apocalypse contestants might prefer a goat to a car. The LLMs, we believe, [had no world model](https://iai.tv/articles/llms-show-language-does-not-describe-reality-auid-3578)—no socialization—that would cause them to consider this possibility. But we also believe that if our post-apocalypse Monty Hall puzzle gets discussed widely, then LLMs will pick up on the possibility, because it will come to belong to the language to which they have access. It is forming the new language in the first place to which they cannot contribute when that language relates to a world being formed through debate.

Moreover, the fractal model, along with the socialization and interactional expertise model, can answer another question that seems to be much in the air these days, though it was introduced by Hubert Dreyfus nearly half a century ago: “Do computers need bodies to be intelligent?” The answer that we can draw from the fractal model, assuming we are talking of mimicking human-like intelligence, is “yes” and “no”. If the aim is to make computers that might be found living in isolated, autonomous societies, then the answer is “yes.” Thus, [to mimic human intelligence](https://iai.tv/video/the-future-of-artificial-minds), the machines will have to be able to talk about, say, tennis. But an autonomous community will never be able to invent such talk unless its members can play tennis, and this means having a human-like body. If, however, the aim is to make computers that can be dropped into existing human societies and acquire tennis-talk, then the answer is “no”—they need only the capacity to acquire interactional expertise, or something approaching it, in tennis. And an individual does not have to be able to practice a physical skill to understand what that skill is. This is just as well, or no one physically disabled from birth would ever be able to understand what tennis is.
