### **Friedrich Nietzsche**

\_\_\_

“What is tradition? A higher authority which one obeys, not because it commands what is *useful* to us, but because it *commands*..."

\_\_\_

Friedrich Nietzsche offers a provocative critique of tradition that still resonates with modern secular Christmas celebrations. In *The Gay Science*, [Nietzsche](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/audio/2012/feb/04/big-ideas-podcast-nietzsche-death-of-god) famously declared that "God is dead," yet he acknowledged that the "shadow of God" would continue to linger. Christmas, even in secular contexts, retains elements of its religious origins. For many, the holiday is celebrated as a cultural tradition rather than a religious one. And with nearly every country having some break from Japan to Sweden to celebrate the season in their own style it really can’t be directly attached to the traditional Christian/pagan holiday. Nietzsche, however, had little patience – unlike his attitude to everything else – for unexamined adherence to tradition, which he saw as a form of obedience to an abstract authority.

“What is tradition? A higher authority which one obeys, not because it commands what is *useful* to us, but because it *commands*. What distinguishes this feeling in the presence of tradition from the feeling of fear in general? It is fear in the presence of a higher intellect which here commands, of an incomprehensible, indefinite power, of something more than personal—there is superstition in this fear.” (Daybreak : Book I - Aphorism 9)

Nietzsche’s aphorism calls us to question why we continue to follow certain Christmas customs, such as gift-giving, elaborate meals, and decorating, even when they no longer hold their original spiritual meaning. Are these acts expressions of genuine joy, or are they remnants of inherited obligations that we perform out of habit? For Nietzsche, tradition can often serve as a substitute for critical thought, perpetuating practices that lack relevance or personal significance.

Yet Nietzsche [did find value in individuals who reinterpret these traditions](http://iai.tv/articles/new-gods-auid-651) in creative and meaningful ways. To celebrate Christmas authentically, according to his philosophy, would require us breaking free from blind adherence and imbuing the holiday with personal significance. Whether through acts of generosity, connection with loved ones, or even playful subversion of traditional norms, Nietzsche would encourage us to make the holiday a reflection of our own values rather than a mere echo of the past.

### **Hannah Arendt**

\_\_\_

“Tradition implies continuity, and continuity provides the link between past and future that we call time.”

\_\_\_

[Hannah Arendt’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hannah-Arendt) philosophy provides a positive perspective on Christmas and the enduring of tradition. Arendt saw tradition as a means of grounding societies in shared histories and values, offering a sense of continuity in a rapidly changing world. However, she also warned against the dangers of mindlessly adhering to traditions. A needed rallying cry in the face of a secular Christmas period.

“Tradition implies continuity, and continuity provides the link between past and future that we call time.” (Between Past and Future)

Arendt’s invites us to consider how traditions serve as a bridge between past generations and the present. The act of decorating a tree, singing carols, or preparing the holiday feast can be seen as rituals that anchor us in collective memory, fostering both a sense of belonging and identity. Her work calls us to see value in these traditions, how they can be meaningful if we actively participate in them rather than merely performing them out of habit.

However, Arendt’s critique of the unreflective participant, the slave to tradition, challenges us to question which elements of Christmas truly resonate with us now. Are we engaging in these practices because they hold personal or cultural significance, or are we perpetuating them without thought? I think many would find it difficult to say that Christmas isn’t a part of even their secular upbringing. But as Socrates would have added the [unexamined is not worth living](http://iai.tv/articles/how-and-why-to-choose-your-philosophy-of-life-auid-1308). Arendt would encourage us to reimagine and reinterpret holiday customs in ways that make them relevant to our present lives, while still honouring their roots.

Arendt’s philosophy offers a hopeful view of Christmas: a time to reflect on our connections to the past and to each other, using traditions not as rigid scripts but as opportunities to create meaning and continuity in an ever-changing world.

### **Karl Marx**

\_\_\_

“Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of a soulless condition. It is the opium of the people..."

\_\_\_

Karl Marx famously critiqued religion as the "opium of the people," offering illusory happiness in a world rife with suffering and inequity. This perspective extends to secular celebrations like Christmas, which often serve as a temporary reprieve from the challenges of life. For Marx, such moments of comfort, though necessary for survival, risk masking the systemic issues that perpetuate societal hardship.

“Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of a soulless condition. It is the opium of the people. The abolition of religion as the illusory happiness of the people is the demand for their real happiness. To call on them to give up their illusions about their condition is to call on them to give up a condition that requires illusions.” (A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right)

Applied to Christmas, Marx might view the holiday’s comforting traditions—gift-giving, feasting, and gathering with family—as distractions from the harsher realities of modern capitalism. The joy and generosity of the season, while genuine for many, can also reinforce the inequalities they seek to momentarily escape. For instance, the emphasis on consumerism during Christmas turns acts of love and goodwill into transactions, further entrenching capitalist systems. Though Marx had a softer side, often noted by his children to have been a loving father despite the poverty which his focus on philosophy left them in. Perhaps we should all look to find benefactors such as Engels to support us through trying times..

However, Marx’s critique does not entirely dismiss the value of celebration. Instead, it challenges us to question whether the comforts of Christmas are built on sustainable foundations or fleeting illusions. The holiday’s potential lies not in providing an escape from reality but in inspiring solidarity, reflection, and action toward creating a fairer society. For Marx, a truly meaningful Christmas would transcend the transactional and consumerist aspects of the holiday, focusing instead on authentic human connection and collective well-being.

By shining a light on the systemic conditions that make the holiday a needed refuge, Marx encourages us to see Christmas not as an end in itself, but as an opportunity to envision and work toward a more equitable world. This perspective deepens the meaning of the season, urging us to ask: What would a truly liberated and joyful celebration of Christmas look like?

### **Simone Weil**

\_\_\_

"We are attached to the possession of a thing because we think that if we cease to possess it, it will cease to exist…"

\_\_\_

[Simone Weil's](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/) philosophy of detachment provides a striking counterpoint to the indulgent and material aspects of Christmas celebrations. For Weil, attachment is a "manufacturer of illusions," binding us to false perceptions of reality and obscuring our understanding of what is truly real. She argues that attachment to possessions, traditions, or even people reflects an insufficiency in our sense of reality—mistaking the existence of things for their value to us.

“Attachment is no more nor less than an insufficiency in our sense of reality. We are attached to the possession of a thing because we think that if we cease to possess it, it will cease to exist… Attachment is a manufacturer of illusions, and whoever wants reality ought to be detached.” (Gravity and Grace)

At Christmas, the season's emphasis on material gifts, elaborate decorations, and indulgent celebrations could, from [Weil's](http://iai.tv/articles/why-attention-matters-for-morality-auid-2711) perspective, reinforce these illusions. By clinging to objects or rituals as central to the holiday’s meaning, we risk mistaking their symbolic value for genuine significance. In contrast, Weil advocates for total detachment as a means to perceive independent reality, free from the distortions of self-centred desires. This detachment does not mean rejecting all joy or celebration but rather seeking deeper meaning that transcends material possessions and cultural expectations.

Weil’s critique challenges the modern conception of Christmas, urging a focus not on the accumulation of things but on the spiritual truths that the season might reveal. In her view, the most profound experiences of joy and connection arise not from attachment to traditions or possessions, but from a recognition of reality unclouded by personal desires. For Weil, a Christmas grounded in detachment might involve acts of generosity, humility, and reflection—practices that honour the spirit of the season without succumbing to the illusions of attachment.

### **Bertrand Russell**

\_\_\_

“One of the symptoms of approaching nervous breakdown is the belief that one's work is terribly important..."

\_\_\_

Bertrand Russell, philosopher, logician, pacifist and mathematician in a more popular work, *The Conquest of Happiness*, critiques the modern obsession with work and productivity, identifying this mind-set as a source of unnecessary stress and unhappiness. A mind-set which is still often touted [by influencers and thinkers](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-relentless-rise-of-the-productivity-influencer/) online today. He notes that prioritising work above all else not only leads to burnout but also diminishes the ability to appreciate life’s simple pleasures. Russell famously remarked:

“One of the symptoms of approaching nervous breakdown is the belief that one's work is terribly important, and that to take a holiday would bring all kinds of disaster. If I were a medical man, I should prescribe a holiday to any patient who considered his work important.” (The Conquest of Happiness)

Russell’s advocacy for leisure as an antidote to the overvaluation of work. A direct attack perhaps on the modern celebration of Christmas. The holiday season should be viewed as a rare opportunity to step away from professional obligations, reconnect with loved ones, and indulge in rest and recreation. For Russell, this would represent a healthy and necessary break, aligning with his belief in the value of leisure for achieving happiness and balance in life. But one which should be carried with us throughout the year. Perhaps however being a lord allowed old Bertie more leisure than we common folk can currently allow for.

However, Russell’s ideas still challenge us to examine the ways in which Christmas has become another form of labour—be it the mental load of organising festivities, the financial pressures of gift-giving, or the societal expectations of hosting and entertaining. These aspects of the holiday can sometimes mirror the stress of work rather than provide true relaxation. Russell’s words give rise to caution against letting these obligations overshadow the restorative power of the season.

Then in a Russellian view, the key to reclaiming the spirit of Christmas lies in recognising that leisure is not a luxury but an essential component of a fulfilling life. By consciously choosing to detach from work, embrace rest, and find joy in small, meaningful moments, we honour the deeper purpose of the holiday: to recharge, reconnect, and rediscover the happiness that comes from being fully present in the moment.

### **Epicurus**

\_\_\_

“The wealth required by nature is limited and is easy to procure; but the wealth required by vain ideals extends to infinity.”

\_\_\_

Epicurus is often misunderstood as just a proponent of excess, actually championed a philosophy of moderation as well as pleasure. For him, true happiness lies in the enjoyment of simple, natural pleasures that foster long-term contentment. In the context of Christmas, this perspective shifts the focus from material indulgence to moments of connection, gratitude, and joy that require little more than good company and a sense of appreciation.  
 “The wealth required by nature is limited and is easy to procure; but the wealth required by vain ideals extends to infinity.” (Principal Doctrines)

Epicurus would likely support Christmas celebrations that prioritise meaningful experiences over excessive consumption. Sharing a meal with loved ones, enjoying laughter, or reflecting on the year’s blessings would align with his philosophy. However, he would caution against allowing the holiday to become a pursuit of "vain ideals"—extravagant gifts, lavish parties, or unnecessary spending—rooted in societal pressures rather than genuine enjoyment.

In Epicurean terms, the ultimate goal of Christmas should be to maximise happiness while minimising stress. Overindulgence, whether in food, drink, or financial expenditure, risks undermining the holiday’s potential to bring lasting joy. Epicurus would advise us to ask: Are our Christmas traditions bringing us true satisfaction, or are they chasing an elusive ideal of holiday perfection?
