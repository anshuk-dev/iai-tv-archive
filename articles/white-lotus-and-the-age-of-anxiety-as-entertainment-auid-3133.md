# The White Lotus and the age of anxiety as entertainment

*Bad karma, Buddhism, and nothingness*

**By Dylan Skurka** | *8th April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,481 words*

![The White Lotus and the age of anxiety as entertainment](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-white-lotus-and-the-age-of-anxiety.webp)

---

*What did we do, in some past life, to deserve the karmic force that is the TV show The White Lotus? This show goes beyond watching for pleasure, ‘hate-watching’, or watching while we scroll on our phones… this is anxiety as entertainment. Philosopher Dylan Skurka argues The White Lotus refuses to deliver the catharsis we crave. Instead, he argues, we should embrace what the Buddhists call emptiness. For ourselves, as well as for the guests at the White Lotus, it’s our attempts to escape the nothingness at the heart of everything that most often leads to our most profound suffering.*

Karma’s a b\*\*\*\*. Karma’s a saint. Karma is many things, but karma isn't the mysterious cosmic scoreboard it’s often assumed to be. The Sanskrit word simply means ‘deed’ or ‘action,’ as predictable as rain drops splashing in a puddle. What makes karma interesting is not the act itself, but the cognitive dissonance that often facilitates it — that jarring human blindness to cause and effect. Should we really be surprised when people who live in deceit, greed, and cruelty eventually find themselves swallowed by those very forces? No, but more often than not, for some reason, we are. *The White Lotus* has always been a deliciously sardonic study of this blindness, but its third season — set against the cinematic backdrop of Thailand — escalates the theme to almost surreal proportions. Here, karma isn’t supernatural justice; it’s the simple, unrelenting weight of actions meeting their consequences.

[In Buddhism, suffering is said to stem from desire](http://iai.tv/articles/reinventing-ourselves-according-to-the-buddha-auid-1108). The remedy, then, is to recognize desire, meditate on it, and let it pass. Financial constraints often take care of this for you: I may really want a Porsche 911, but I’m never going to afford one, so I’m going to have to make do with my old Toyota Corolla — the suffering of not getting what I want will eventually fade. But if extreme wealth multiplies desire, *The White Lotus* asks, doesn’t that mean extreme wealth multiplies suffering as well?

\_\_\_

In a crippled attention economy, where we anxiously swipe through content and expect instant gratification as though it were our birthright, The White Lotus turns the frustration of unfulfilled desires into an aesthetic — it dares us to sit with our own stunted patience, our addiction to more and more as fast as possible.

\_\_\_

This time around, the hotel guests we meet are Tim and Victoria Ratcliffe and their three kids: their eldest son Saxon, daughter Piper, and their youngest son Lochlan. Middle-aged Rick arrives with much younger Chelsea; and a seasoned television actress, Jaclyn Lemon, brings along her two childhood friends, Kate and Laurie, for some vacation fun. An obnoxious family, a sugar daddy with his muse, and an aging girls' trip? As stereotypical as it gets, you might think; but we should all know by now that if anything, the White Lotus is a place where stereotypes get subverted.

And what better country for this unraveling than Thailand? A land where suffering and indulgence exist side by side, each feeding off the other. Where ascetic chants meet the neon glow of grotesque sexual misadventure. Where brooding Baptists, numb on enough lorazepam to wipe out the entire Thai army, stumble through gilded hotel lobbies. Where the menacing spectre of danger and crime lingers over postcard-perfect beaches like an approaching monsoon. Here, every hotel guest is chasing something—sex, power, enlightenment—until their paths inexorably collide, and somebody pays the ultimate price, bringing to life with dark irony [what Martin Heidegger once called *running towards death.*](http://iai.tv/articles/hegel-vs-heidegger-can-we-uncover-reality-auid-3001)

Rick comes to Thailand with one burning desire: to kill the man who murdered his father. Chelsea desires Rick’s love and attention, but he’s so consumed by his obsession with revenge that he barely notices her. The Ratcliffe parents are trying to maintain the illusion of generational wealth and control, but cracks are forming: Tim discovers that a shady business deal may ruin them financially, and Victoria has to reconcile that her children are no longer compliant props in their puppet show of family success.

Laurie and Jaclyn desire to one-up each other — Laurie from a place of envy and Jaclyn from a place of self-importance — but these desires turn inward and rot, sabotaging both of them. Kate wants to hold everything together, to play the diplomat. But she can’t help gossiping about Laurie and Jaclyn behind their backs, feeding the very drama she also disdains.

Saxon wants sexual power — to conquer — but instead finds himself disarmed and dominated. Piper longs to escape her family’s suffocating grip and live in a monastery. But once there, she realizes it wasn’t the monastery she was drawn to, only the fantasy of rising above her family. And Lochlan imagines he’s shedding control and floating free, but he’s only drifting through the wake of everyone else’s chaos.

Karma — as predictable as rain drops splashing in a puddle.

It’s tempting to get frustrated with the monotonous volatility with which these characters chase their desires and the show’ssluggish, plodding pace in presenting it. Inkoo Kang of *The New Yorker,* for example, [has criticized this season of *The White Lotus*](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/03/03/the-white-lotus-tv-review-hbo) for “overstaying its welcome,” while [*The Atlantic*’s Derek Thompson has griped](http://podcasts.apple.com/mt/podcast/the-future-of-everything-with-derek-thompson/id1043699613?i=1000663392320) that it [“grinds things down to an absolute halt…totally [falling] off in terms of plot development.”](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/ezra-klein-and-derek-thompson-have-a-plan-for-the-left/id1570872415?i=1000699620497) Even for the staunchest defenders of the show, this logic can be hard to argue with: how many episodes do we need of a zonked out Tim Ratcliffe mumbling to himself on painkillers? How many times do we need to hear Rick *say* he’s going to Bangkok before he actually makes his move?

But what if that’s the point?

What if the season’s inertia isn’t a flaw, but a meditation? Not lazy storytelling, but a deliberate stalling — a refusal to deliver the catharsis we crave? In a crippled attention economy, where we anxiously swipe through content and expect instant gratification as though it were our birthright, *The White Lotus* turns the frustration of unfulfilled desires into an aesthetic — it dares us to sit with our own stunted patience, our addiction to more and more as fast as possible.

The liminal space between wanting and getting is nothingness. Nothingness is always a moment — the second after you read a rejection letter for the perfect job, the instant you lose Wi-Fi on the subway mid-scroll. Or when you’re watching *The White Lotus* and realize the plot isn’t moving as fast as you want it to.

\_\_\_

As for the rest of us? It’s who we’d be without the dopamine hit of social media and the white noise of constant entertainment. No distraction, just you.

\_\_\_

Instead of seeing them as seekers of desire, consider viewing the White Lotus guests this season as fugitives running *away* from nothingness. For the Ratliffes, it’s who they fear becoming if their wealth and social status were to disappear. For Rick, it's who he would be without his obsession with finding his father's killer. For Chelsea, it’s who she’d be without her exhausting mission to rescue Rick. And for Jaclyn, Laurie, and Kate, it’s who they’d be if their “friendship” dissolved. As for the rest of us? It’s who we’d be without the dopamine hit of social media and the white noise of constant entertainment. No distraction, just *you.*

Suffering, then, is often the choice we make to escape nothingness and coddle familiarity, no matter how much this familiarity is guaranteed to hurt us. [We construct false identities](http://iai.tv/articles/why-there-is-no-self-a-buddhist-perspective-for-the-west-auid-1044), false narratives, and relive past traumas to hold on to the false hope that there is something rather than nothing. It’s not the pain we fear, it’s the emptiness beneath it.

\_\_\_

It’s not that the journey is more valuable than the destination — it’s that the journey and the destination are the same thing.

\_\_\_

But in Buddhism, nothingness isn’t a curse — it’s a gift. A rare moment when you can resist the tedious chase of desire and unshackle yourself from past mistakes. Nothingness is the opportunity to change your karma in the truest sense of the word — and to this end, the White Lotus this season isn’t really a leisurely resort so much as a karmic pressure cooker, where each character’s evasion of nothingness reaches its natural conclusion. Some don’t make it out. Others wake up. The rest simply roll over and fall back asleep, but no one leaves untouched, and no one leaves unchanged.

Travelling to a distant country like Thailand is one path toward enlightenment, but it’s not the only way because, existentially speaking, we are always in motion. “Remember that you are just a visitor here, a traveler passing through,” goes a [famous Buddhist poem](https://www.dhammawiki.com/index.php/Dhammavadaka). “Your stay is but short and the moment of your departure unknown.”

It’s not that the journey is more valuable than the destination — it’s that the journey and the destination are the same thing. That new Porsche would be great and all, but my Toyota will get me there just fine.
