# John Gray and the end of the West's utopian dream

*"The meaning of life ... is gone before you know it."*

**By Andy Owen** | *24th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 16 mins* | *3,162 words*

![John Gray and the end of the West's utopian dream](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/John-Gray-and-the-end-of-the-Wests-utopian-dream.webp)

---

*John Gray prophetically called the end of liberalism. Criticising liberalism's universalism, egalitarianism, hyper-individualism (which became identity politics), and its belief in moral progress, Gray sees liberalism as a hangover from religion. But it is now crumbling. China, Russia, and the US are fighting to lead the new world order. In this piece, former intelligence officer **Andy Owen** argues that it is liberalism's utopian notions that ultimately led to its demise, and that this too is a hangover from religion in that it reflects a search for salvation. Instead, he argues, with Gray, that we should give up on looking for utopia, or meaning in life at all.*

It was in a warzone that I first read John Gray’s work. His words cut through the silence of the still, mid-afternoon heat of Helmand Province, Afghanistan. They helped me make sense of the political ideology that I had unknowingly become a missionary for: liberal humanism. In [*Black Mass: Apocalyptic Religion and the Death of Utopia*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Black-Mass-Apocalyptic-Religion-Utopia/dp/0141025980)*,* Gray showed how President George W Bush and Prime Minister Tony Blair framed the ‘war on terror’ as an apocalyptic struggle that would forge the new American century of liberal democracy, where personal freedom and free markets were the end goals of human progress. Gray’s latest work, [*The New Leviathans; Thoughts After Liberalism*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/New-Leviathans-Thoughts-After-Liberalism/dp/0241554950/ref=pd_lpo_d_sccl_1/258-1883501-1704324?pd_rd_w=zomRC&content-id=amzn1.sym.bb13d3fc-af40-4fff-a822-e0e4c415da96&pf_rd_p=bb13d3fc-af40-4fff-a822-e0e4c415da96&pf_rd_r=P9HJVYN3KM918JZPT002&pd_rd_wg=6yLhI&pd_rd_r=3c6ce02c-7c9a-4096-a015-2c19f4fe1fd8&pd_rd_i=0241554950&psc=1), uses the work of Thomas Hobbes (according to Gray the first and last great liberal philosopher), to conduct a pre-mortem examination of the hubristic and utopian world that ideology failed to forge and a meditation on the new world order that is being born out of its death throes. As much as Gray helped me make sense of the wars I experienced, his discussion of Hobbes can help us make sense of our troubled times. Whilst Gray doesn’t offer easy solutions to the violence of a post-liberal world, his observations on our unchanging human needs that lead us on the constant search for ideologies that give us meaning, purpose and belonging, can offer personal consolation.

\_\_\_

According to Gray, belief in progress is the Prozac of the thinking classes. After the last few decades, for many, the drugs are no longer working.

\_\_\_

A message repeated throughout Gray’s work is that scientific and technological knowledge increases over time, yet there’s no reason to think that morality or culture will also progress, nor – if it does progress for a period – that this progress is irreversible. History is not an ascending spiral of human advance from unenlightened times to a better world. It is an unending cycle in which changing knowledge interacts with unchanging human needs in oscillating periods of war and peace, during which violence is often inflicted by those thinking that they were doing the right thing for the greater good. In science progress is fact, in ethics and politics it is a superstition. According to Gray, belief in progress is the Prozac of the thinking classes. After the last few decades, for many, the drugs are no longer working.

In Gray’s[*Straw Dogs: Thoughts on Humans and Other Animals*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Straw-Dogs-Thoughts-Humans-Animals/dp/1862075964) he attacks what he believes is the illusory faith that our species is apart and above the rest of nature, uniquely privileged in the universe with the gifts of self-consciousness and reason. There is no such thing as ‘humanity’ only ‘humans, driven by conflicting needs and illusions’. Due to the plurality of these needs and illusions, it’s utopian to imagine that any one political system or social order could be universally good for all. There’s no teleological end of history. Instead, our ceaseless attempts to try to find meaning invariably drive us into the embrace of religious belief systems and their secular imitations – and, consequently, to continual conflict. Gray ironically notes that killing and dying for nonsensical ideas is how many human beings have made sense of their lives, attempting immortality through death.

For Gray, ‘liberal humanism’ is a quasi-religious faith in progress, the subjective power of reason, free markets, and the unbounded potential of technology. Screened off from conscious awareness in secular society, the religious impulse has mutated, returning as the fantasy of salvation through politics, or - now with increasing political apostasy - a cult of science and technology. During the Enlightenment the Christian doctrine of salvation was subsumed by a secular idealism that developed into modern-day liberal humanism (Gray argues that capitalism has its origins in positivism, the secular cult influenced by the late-18th-century French philosopher Henri de Saint-Simon, who believed that science would end all human ills). With the failures of Iraq and Afghanistan, the 2008 financial crisis, and the climate crisis, faith in the utopia that liberal humanism once promised has waned. It’s being replaced by beliefs that look backwards, through the distorting lens of nostalgia, to imagined better times to which we hope to return.

In *New Leviathans*, Gray returns to Hobbes, one of the founders of modern political philosophy. When the English Civil War started, Hobbes publicly supported the authority of Charles I. Fearing persecution by the parliamentarians, he fled to Paris where, in 1651, he wrote *Leviathan*. Hobbes argues that human beings are governed by involuntary passions and deliberate reason. Power is the ability to acquire what one deems good and evade what one deems bad; with good and bad equating to things we like or dislike. In our relentless quest for domination over others, we each seek to further our own ability to have the power to do what we like and avoid doing what we don’t like. This is a recipe for endless conflict, driven by competition, distrust and the attainment of glory or status. In this state, it can be rational to kill others before they kill you. Hobbes calls this forever war our “state of nature”, the ubiquitous and inescapable basic state of humankind, in which life is “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short”.

Gray puts forward four ideas that define liberalism. Firstly, it is individualist in that it asserts the moral primacy of the individual against the claims of any social collective. Secondly, it is egalitarian inasmuch as it confers on all men the same moral status. Thirdly, it is universalist in affirming the moral unity of the human species and according a secondary importance to specific historical associations and cultural forms. Lastly, it is meliorist in its belief in the improvability of social institutions and political arrangements. Gray believes that [Hobbes’ political theory features all of these.](http://iai.tv/articles/hobbes-vs-rousseau-are-we-inherently-evil-or-good-auid-1221)

For Hobbes, society is made up of individuals, who can assert their claim to self-preservation against any demand by the state; if a ruler fails to protect them, they can be disobeyed or overthrown. Hobbes believed that human beings are equal to being exposed to death at each other’s hands. Human nature is universal in its needs.  Divergent cultural identities are superficial. Lastly, with the application of reason, government could be improved and human beings could overcome conflict.

\_\_\_

There is no final exit from the state of nature.

\_\_\_

According to Gray these are half-truths. Individuals may be the basis of society, but self-preservation is only one of their needs: bare life is not enough. We need meaning, belonging and purpose. Human beings regularly give up peace and security in order to defend a form of life they believe to be superior to another. The most basic human goods may be universal, but they are often sacrificed in order to fight for values specific to particular ways of living. Society and government can always be improved, but what is gained can be lost. There is no final exit from the state of nature. Gray believes that Hobbes, known for his pessimistic views on human nature, was ultimately an optimist, whose belief in the meliorist potential of reason mirrors over-optimistic modern liberals.

Hobbes’ political theory is achieved through a Leviathan: [a state with unfettered power](http://iai.tv/articles/what-hobbes-really-thought-about-war-auid-2098) necessary to protect people from one another and external enemies. This is an artificial animal created to escape the state of nature. Hobbes didn’t anticipate that, through attempts to remodel humanity, totalitarian regimes would create artificial states of nature. For Hobbes, state powers were unchecked, but their goals were limited. Beyond securing its subjects against one another and external enemies, it had no remit. In uncertain times, Gray’s New Leviathans aim to secure meaning in life for their subjects. As in the twentieth-century totalitarian Nazi and Soviet regimes, they have become “engineers of souls”.

Gray examines the New Leviathans constructed in Russia and in China. Gray sees Russia as a quasi-theocracy, but also a full-blown kleptocracy, with the potential to morph into “a steampunk Byzantium with nukes”. He rubbishes the idea held by some in the European and American right that Russia is ethically superior to the West as “a decadent fever-dream” — highlighting that family breakdown, drug addiction, suicide and anomie run at higher levels than in most Western countries. This is only likely to increase with the hundreds of thousands of soldiers returning from the “meat grinder” of the Ukrainian frontline. He warns those hoping for Putin’s downfall to be careful what they wish for. As he has written elsewhere, “For all its cultural achievements, this vast country has never enjoyed an extended period of freedom — only interludes of chaos in immemorial despotism.” Post-Putin scenarios include a version of the brutal Russian Civil War and a version of the ethnic mayhem that followed the break-up of Yugoslavia, intensified by fights over the unevenly distributed resources. Yevgeny Prizoghin’s Wagner Group may no longer be a threat to Putin, but other oligarchs and intelligence operators are growing their private armies for the coming struggle.

China is described as a high-tech Panopticon, influenced as much by the interwar German Jurist Carl Schmitt as Mao and Confucius. Xi’s attempts to unify the Chinese people in a single national culture echoes Schmitt, whilst his system of government by surveillance seems inspired by the Panopticon, the ideal prison designed by philosopher Jeremy Bentham to keep its inmates under constant observation. Schmitt argued that law was made by the decision of the sovereign, whilst politics was a struggle between enemies. He tasked the state with the protection of a unified people. This is laid down on top of a long history of legalism, which was synthesised by Han Fei, a 3rd-century philosopher of the “Legalist” (Fa-jia) school. Fei’s Fa includes explicit, publicly promulgated standards of conduct — encompassing laws, standards for job performance, criteria for military and bureaucratic promotion, and regulation of the general population. Xi’s social credit system is a modern merging of the Panoptican and Fa concepts of merit.

Gray questions the narrative around the inevitable rise of China, asking whether China’s one nation approach to governance will survive the ageing Xi’s succession and its economic and demographic challenges. Much will depend on its technology race with the US. The breakup of the country into warring states, or a more traditional empire of multiple fiefdoms with their own cultures and allegiances, are possible futures.

Gray then turns to Western liberalism, which Gray believes was a creation of monotheism, with liberal freedoms part of a civilization that monotheism engendered. Yet twenty-first-century liberals reject this civilization whilst continuing to claim the universalism of its values — in particular, those of individualization and freedom.

This is being taken to what could be seen as its logical endpoint, in what Gray calls hyper-liberalism, where each human being is sovereign in deciding who or what they want to be. Another view is that it is a project of forging new collectives and the prelude to a state of chronic conflict amongst the identities they embody. Gray points out that we can never be wholly self-defined. If our identity is to be more than private fantasy, we must induce others to accept it. For Gray, hyper-liberalism fulfils several roles — it operates as a rationale or distraction for a failing variety of capitalism and a vehicle through which surplus elites struggle to secure a position of power in society.

Gray criticises hyper-liberalism’s placing of identity at the centre of politics as this results in conflicts of economic interest being disregarded. Identity politics consigns to obloquy and oblivion those who suffer most from current economic systems. It also provincialises the universal evil of racism, projecting a particularly American history onto all. Writing before the current round of conflict in the Middle East, Gray cites the massacres of Muslims in the Balkan Wars, the Rwandan genocide, and the Chinese attempts to obliterate Tibetans and Uighurs as examples of racist atrocities that cannot be understood through the lens of American racism. Gray doesn’t deny the existence of racism, stating that racial oppression of black people in America is “stark, extreme and enduring”. Instead, he levels the same criticism against the hyper-liberals as he has against the neocons behind Iraq and Afghanistan. They believe they have found the one right universal way of living for all, and attempt to force their template onto profoundly different cultures, with incompatible social, political and economic structures developed over millennia.

Gray sees a West where any curb on human will is now condemned as a mode of repression. By a droll necessity, this freedom demands that every aspect of life be monitored and controlled. The logic of limitless freedom is ultimately despotic. Gray sees this new despotism controlling language and even the private realm of the mind, which comes under scrutiny for hidden biases and errors. Hobbes believed that humans needed limitation as much as they needed freedom, but his limitations were on actual violence rather than on the psychological violence we are told we now need to be protected from.

These criticisms aren’t those used by sections of the right who cry “woke” at anything they do not like or understand; they are challenges from a sincere actor with a long history of considering these issues. In 1989, Gray highlighted the dangers of where the American experiment in rights-based liberalism was heading, anticipating the 2022 ruling on abortion. “In the US … every moral and political dispute is cast in the legalistic idiom of the rights discourse. Accordingly, the courts, and for that matter the entire procedure of judicial review, have become theatres of doctrinal conflict.” Writing before but adding to the explanation of the second coming of Trump, Gray again highlights the challenges with the uniquely American branch of liberal legalism that aims to replace politics by the adjudication of rights, making law a branch of politics. Conflicts of rights reflect divergent understandings of the human good, which cannot be resolved by legal arbitration. In the US, different understandings of human good are getting further apart. Trying to arbitrate the law is becoming increasingly politicised.

New Leviathans are fostering uncertainty rather than creating security. By deploying food and energy supplies as weapons of war, Russia has projected famine and poverty across the globe. A Chinese invasion of Taiwan could impact the global control of microprocessors, tipping the scales in the race to Artificial Superintelligence in China’s favour. Trump’s “America First” foreign policy is accelerating and exacerbating all of the uncertainty Gray highlights. Gray lays down the challenge of the emerging age: navigating a peaceful co-existence for the competitive New Leviathans. Building on the work of Isaiah Berlin, Gray proposes aspiring to an approach of *modus vivendi*. This recognises that there is a plurality of human values that determine many ways of living, and these values — and those that hold them — will inevitably clash. It is the search for a way of living together despite this, humbly embracing the multiple forms of human life as a good thing in itself. While grand projects of global unification are not realistic options, neither is isolationism. In recognising that peace can be achieved in many kinds of regime, Hobbes was a truer liberal than those who came after him. The belief that a single form of rule is best for everyone is itself a form of tyranny.

Gray also offers a personal realism to accompany the political realism. He tells us that the real Leviathan is the human animal. In *Straw Dogs*, Gray likened our fate to that of the straw dogs of ancient Chinese rituals. Used as offerings to the gods, these dogs were treated with the utmost reverence, until the ritual was over, when they were tossed aside. We need to recognise that our lot is no different from the other animals, straw or flesh, even if we are alone amongst living creatures in knowing that our lives are bound by death. This awareness impels us to seek immortality in ideas. In so doing, we exercise what Hobbes described as the “privilege of absurdity”.

Gray advocates a more contemplative life, lived moment to moment, that appreciates the immediate joys of existence in the skin we are in. The last line of *Straw Dogs* asks: ‘Can we not think of the aim of life as being simply to see?’ In [*Feline Philosophy: Cats and the Meaning of Life*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Feline-Philosophy-Cats-Meaning-Life/dp/0241351146), Gray ponders the essential nature of cats, noting they live for the sensation of life, not for something they might achieve or not. They have no concept of striving to become the perfect specimen of their type. He offers ten hints on how to live well, including, ‘Do not look for meaning in your suffering’; ‘Never try to persuade human beings to be reasonable’; and ‘Beware of anyone who offers to make you happy.’ He concedes that, for many, such a life is overwhelming. Gray cites Renaissance philosopher Michel de Montaigne, who in grief, acknowledged he needed a distraction (and who also famously asked: “When I am playing with my cat, how do I know she is not playing with me?”). His last hint states: ‘If you cannot learn to live a little more like a cat, return without regret to the human world of diversion.’ But it is a diversion in the consolations of the ‘mystics, poets and pleasure-lovers’ rather than utopian thinkers who, as I saw in Afghanistan and we see again in Gray’s New Leviathans, break lives in the name of unattainable goals.

Gray claims, “If we go on, it is because we cannot do otherwise. It is life that pulls on, against the tide, life that steers us into the storm.” Acceptance of human limits isn’t a defeat, but a source of wonder and enrichment. Gray concludes that: ‘The meaning of life is a touch, a scent, which comes by chance and is gone before you know it.’ It is the pleasure of a purposeless life in a meaningless universe. The contemplation Gray advocates isn’t a turning away from the world, but one that allows us to turn back to it and embrace its folly. Grace and humility at both the personal and political levels, is accepting our flaws and limitations, and humbly working with them rather than retreating to fantasies or destroying lives in Quixotic attempts to change the unchangeable.
