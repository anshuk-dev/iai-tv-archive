# The self cannot exist in isolation

*Punishment as ontological violence*

**By Federica Coppola** | *23rd September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,111 words*

![The self cannot exist in isolation](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-self-cannot-exist-in-isolation.webp)

*Incarceration is usually framed as the justified removal of freedom of movement. **Federica Coppola**, Associate Professor of Criminal Law at IE University Law School and author of The Real Pain of Punishment, argues this is a misidentification. Imprisonment is a form of manufactured exile, punishing not just the body's freedom but the self's very capacity to exist in relation to others. Against the Kantian notion that personhood is a self-contained bundle of autonomy, Coppola argues the deepest harm of imprisonment isn't the loss of liberty itself but the loss of belonging.*

We tend to think of imprisonment primarily as the deprivation of liberty. That is, after all, what a prison sentence most obviously does: it takes away a person’s freedom to move, to choose where to live, whom to see, how to spend their time, and what to do with their own body. Because prison sentences are measured in months and years, it seems intuitive to assume that the longer a person is deprived of liberty, the greater the punishment. The loss of freedom is therefore understood as the defining pain of incarceration, and the duration of that loss as the measure of its severity—or so the theory goes.

Yet, when speaking with people who have experienced imprisonment firsthand, I have often been struck by how rarely they identify the loss of freedom alone as the most painful aspect of incarceration. What emerges far more often is the profound distress of feeling persistently invisible. This invisibility arises not only from physical confinement in places removed from the public eye. It is also produced through structural dynamics of misrecognition, powerlessness, and distrust that often shape life in prison. Under these conditions, personal histories, past traumas, and emotional needs recede behind the imperatives of risk management and control, while people’s voices may go unheard, their accounts of suffering disbelieved, and their needs left unmet.

For many people, especially those who have spent long periods in prison, invisibility is intertwined with an enduring sense of isolation. Isolation here not only includes physical separation from society, but also the sense of abandonment, neglect, loneliness, helplessness, and hopelessness that comes with it. Separation from loved ones is central to many accounts. Yet its pain lies not only in being apart. It also lies in being unable to participate in and contribute to their lives—in knowing that children, partners, parents, or friends cannot rely on you when they need support. Imprisonment therefore deprives people not only of contact, but also of the ability to inhabit meaningful social roles: to care for others, support them, and matter in their lives.

Over time, these experiences can reshape how people understand themselves and their place in society. Being persistently unseen and unheard, and unable to sustain meaningful relationships, can erode a person’s identity, foster self-stigma, and diminish their sense of worth. Their individuality, growth, and capacity for change may go unrecognized, as may the intellectual, social, and other contributions they could still make to society.

What if, then, one of the most damaging aspects of imprisonment—one that may help explain the psychological and physical harms so widely documented among people in prison—is not simply the loss of liberty, but the progressive erosion of a person’s sense of belonging? Can the suffering caused by that erosion still be considered a legitimate part of punishment, or reconciled with basic standards of dignity and humanity? Put differently, the state may claim the authority to deprive people of their liberty—but can it legitimately deprive them of the conditions necessary to satisfy their need to belong?

Belonging is a fundamental human need, rooted in the inherently social nature of human beings. It reflects our innate and universal desire to connect with others, to receive care and support, and to feel accepted, respected, and valued as individuals. Belonging matters not only because it supports other basic human needs—including survival, safety, and health—but also because it plays a central role in shaping our identity. Who we are and who we become are profoundly influenced by the relationships we form and the social contexts in which we live. We construct our sense of self through others: through the ways in which we are seen and recognized, through our ability to feel part of a community, and through the experience of having our humanity acknowledged. Belonging, in this sense, is not simply about feeling connected to others; it is central to how we understand ourselves, our place in the world, and our own value within it.

The need for belonging is rooted in our biology. Human beings are naturally predisposed to seek connection with others. Meaningful social contact is fundamental to our survival and well-being, much like food and water. When this need is frustrated, the consequences can be profound and long-lasting. Threats to belonging—including through forms of social exclusion such as isolation, rejection, and ostracism—can give rise to a distinctive form of distress known as *social pain*: the emotional suffering caused by the actual or threatened loss of meaningful social connections or of one’s social value. This pain may be experienced as loneliness, abandonment, invisibility, or a diminished sense of self-worth. When exclusion and disconnection become prolonged or chronic, their effects can extend far beyond immediate emotional distress, contributing to mental health difficulties, harmful patterns of behavior, and adverse physical health outcomes. In more severe cases, they may also contribute to self-harm and suicidal thoughts.

\_\_\_

If belonging is as fundamental to human well-being as basic physical needs such as food and water, then a punishment that systematically frustrates it raises difficult questions.

\_\_\_

People in prison do not possess a different set of human needs from the rest of us. Their identities, sense of worth, and capacity to flourish are sustained by recognition and connection in the same ways as ours. Yet the frustration of the fundamental need to belong runs through multiple exclusionary dimensions that commonly characterize prison life. It is evident in the constricted carceral spaces, in the relentless experience of time, in asymmetrical power relations, and in relationships with both institutions and others that are often marked by distrust and perceived unfairness. Importantly, these dynamics are not confined to extreme conditions such as solitary confinement or to instances of intentional abuse. Rather, they are embedded in structural features that commonly shape the everyday experience of imprisonment. Some of its deepest harms may therefore escape scrutiny precisely because they are produced by the ordinary operation of the institution rather than deliberately inflicted.

Although distinct, each dimension points to a common underlying process: the incarcerated person comes to be seen as a “different other,” their complex social identity increasingly reduced to a single defining label—a “criminal,” a “murderer,” a “convict,” an “inmate.” Seen primarily through this lens, the person behind the label disappears, making it easier to regard them as less deserving of care, recognition, and respect, and to neglect or minimize their emotional and social needs. As a consequence, this dehumanization makes it easier to overlook the fact that they are social beings like everyone else, with the same fundamental need for connection, recognition, and belonging; to discount their capacity for change and their human potential because of past wrongdoing; and even to regard forms of suffering that frustrate those needs as a legitimate or inevitable part of punishment. As a scholar and dear friend who is currently incarcerated once wrote to me: “Criminals are animals, and animals don’t feel.”

These dimensions of punishment remain largely invisible within the legal standards and practices through which punishment is defined, imposed, and assessed. One reason for this gap is that legal frameworks often abstract punishment from the structural realities of imprisonment and from the different ways in which incarceration is experienced by those subjected to it. As a result, suffering arising from the social and relational conditions of confinement can remain beyond the law’s field of vision. This is a serious omission. If belonging is as fundamental to human well-being as basic physical needs such as food and water, then a punishment that systematically frustrates it raises difficult questions under justice systems’ commitment to humane treatment, itself grounded in universal respect for human dignity.

Belonging is integral to dignity. It affirms our equal membership in the human community, sustains our capacity to flourish, and allows us to experience our presence and voice as meaningful. Our identity, autonomy, and sense of worth develop through relationships and social recognition. By implication, prison conditions that systematically undermine identity, relationships, agency, and recognition may therefore become inhuman or degrading—not only because they cause distress, but because they fail to treat incarcerated people as equal and continuing members of that community.

Making belonging a benchmark for penal humanity would require the law to attend to the *quality of punishment* as well as its quantity, and to assess it from within the experience of the person being punished. In sentencing, this would mean looking beyond duration to consider how deeply a sentence is likely to affect a person’s relationships, identity, mental health, and prospects for reintegration. In assessing prison conditions, it would mean looking beyond intentional cruelty and egregious abuse to confront suffering that can arise from institutions operating exactly as designed—through isolation, disempowerment, and control. The law’s understanding of carceral harm would expand to include forms of suffering that are structural, diffuse, and less visible, but whose cumulative effects may profoundly damage a person’s integrity over time.

Bringing these harms into view would shift the boundaries of carceral legitimacy. Depriving someone of liberty cannot, by itself, justify forms of social exclusion that systematically frustrate a fundamental human need. Under a belonging-based standard of penal humanity, abandonment, humiliation, and misrecognition would no longer be treated as merely incidental consequences of incarceration; they would become relevant to the legal assessment of prison conditions. Many conditions currently regarded as “humane” might then cease to meet that standard.

\_\_\_

It is to ask whether—and under what conditions—incarceration can remain legitimate once its systematic threat to belonging is recognized as a genuine form of penal harm..

\_\_\_

A belonging-based approach would also change whose knowledge counts in determining the meaning and severity of punishment. It would bring the voices of system-impacted people more fully into debates about justice reform, treating them not merely as objects of legal regulation but as knowledge producers with particular authority to interpret their own experiences. Their insights could reshape how the law understands punishment and the harms it creates, while informing the interpretation of standards of humanity, dignity, and hope. This, in turn, could enable legal protections to respond more effectively to the vulnerabilities that incarceration itself produces.

My understanding of this principle has been shaped by the privilege of working and writing with five scholars who have experienced imprisonment, four of whom are currently serving very long sentences. None argues that the harm caused by crime should go unanswered, or that temporary separation from society is never necessary to protect others. Their reflections point instead to a different starting point: a person cannot be reduced to the worst thing they have done, and a just response should not hold them forever at that moment. Rather, it must enable people to confront the harm they have caused and participate in its repair, while also attending to the life histories, relationships, identities, and social conditions that shape harmful behavior. In this way, accountability becomes a process of recognition, reparation, and change—one that preserves the possibility of transformation without depending on the infliction of further suffering.

The practical implication is not to sacrifice public safety, but to pursue it through more constructive and less punitive responses to harm. Research on belonging suggests that lasting change is more likely to emerge from positive relationships, healthy environments, respect, recognition, and opportunities to exercise agency than from isolation, degradation, or control. Such an approach would address unresolved trauma and the conditions underlying harmful behavior while building on people’s strengths and capacities. It would connect accountability with healing and prevention, enabling the past to be confronted without allowing it to foreclose the future.

The challenge for a “humane” penal system, then, is not simply to make prisons less harsh. It is to ask whether—and under what conditions—incarceration can remain legitimate once its systematic threat to belonging is recognized as a genuine form of penal harm. This shifts the inquiry from how much liberty the state may take away to what forms of suffering it may permissibly impose on a person’s identity, relationships, and place within society. It also requires us to ask whether our responses to crime address the conditions that give rise to harm and create meaningful possibilities for change, or merely reproduce isolation, powerlessness, and pain. These are not collateral concerns. They go to the heart of whether our systems of punishment can meaningfully claim to deliver justice.
