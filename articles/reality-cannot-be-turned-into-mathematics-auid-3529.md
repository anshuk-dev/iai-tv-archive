# Reality cannot be turned into mathematics

*The world will never fit inside an equation*

**By Jobst Landgrebe** | *24th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,265 words*

![Reality cannot be turned into mathematics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/reality-cannot-be-turned-into-mathematics.webp)

---

*For centuries, scientists and philosophers—from Leibniz to today’s AI visionaries—have dreamed of mathematizing reality, fully describing the world in equations that a machine can compute. But this is a dangerous illusion, argue mathematician and biochemist **Jobst Landgrebe** and philosopher **Barry Smith**, whose work on scientific modelling has been utilized by institutions like the US military and the National Institutes of Health, making him one of the world’s most highly cited philosophers. Natural systems like the climate and the human brain, they contend, can never be fully captured by models, because they are fundamentally irregular and unpredictable. True progress depends on flexible heuristics, which allow us to intervene in reality without needing an exhaustive description of it.*

Already in the seventeenth century Leibniz advanced the idea of a reasoning machine that could manipulate symbolic representations of all human thoughts—thereby essentially mechanizing thought itself. Scholars facing a disagreement could simply say “let us calculate” and arrive at the truth.

Today we live in a time of renewed belief in our ability to model the world mathematically, using physics and its manifold applications, supported by fantastically powerful reasoning machines called [Large Language Models](https://iai.tv/articles/all-knowing-machines-are-a-fantasy-auid-2334).

For example, we are told that we can have reliable predictions of the Earth’s globally aggregated weather (the climate) for the next 100 years. Or that we will soon be able “to develop genetic engineering technologies and techniques to be able to write circuitry for cells and predictably program biology in the same way in which we write software and program computers” ([US Executive Order 14081](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-14081-advancing-biotechnology-and-biomanufacturing-innovation-for) of September 12, 2022).

\_\_\_

We will always be in a position where we can use mathematics to create predictive models of only small portions of reality.

\_\_\_

We are also told that we will soon be able to emulate the human mind and human intelligence inside a machine. Indeed, there are some who hold that [such emulations will perform so well](https://iai.tv/video/the-dawn-of-machine-consciousness-with-joscha-bach) that [the machine will become conscious](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2403.17101) and surpass our intelligence. Others believe that we can optimize the human mind via genetic manipulation or [brain implants](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-02368-8).

Unfortunately, none of this is feasible. Moreover, the belief in these fantasies is *harmful*, since, by failing, they can lead not only to enormous malinvestments of scarce resources but also to harmful technology and harmful experiments.

**1. How we came to believe we can model everything**

It was not only Leibniz but also Kepler, Galileo, Newton, and Descartes who, in the course of the seventeenth century, invented mathematical physics. Not only did their ideas, especially the differential calculus, massively improve our ability to model the world, but they also led to a revolution in the design of technology to change the environments in which we live. Leibniz, for example, devised a new kind of mechanical operating system for the Falun copper mine in Sweden, which massively increased the mine’s output. Since then, physics and its applications in engineering have brought incredible progress and fundamentally changed the human world. On the other hand, these successes have also given rise to a widespread conviction that the trajectory of improvement of the world through science is limitless, and that we will be able to model, and over time predict, and technically manipulate, everything, from molecules to planets.

**2. Why we can only model fractions of reality**

Unfortunately, however, we will always be in a position where we can use mathematics to create predictive models of only small portions of reality. This is because such models, of any system, rely on *regularities* of the system’s behavior—and most systems do not behave in a regular fashion.

Now there is a lot to unpack here. To begin with, *a system* is a totality of dynamically interrelated physical elements participating in a process. Humans delimit systems by selecting, from the massive gallimaufry of objects in reality, *system elements*, which are typically at a certain level of granularity. A living cell, for example, is a system of *microphysical particles* (proteins, lipids, ions, together with water and nucleic acid molecules). The Solar System is delimited as the totality of planets and smaller system bodies joined together via gravitational attraction.

\_\_\_

Most natural systems are non-ergodic, which is a technical way of saying that the processes which occur in such systems are overwhelmingly irregular.

\_\_\_

*What is a regularity?* The term denotes the phenomenon that, in a system, a pattern in the system behavior is repeated over time. For example, the Sun rises every morning because the Earth moves around it in an almost perfect, regular fashion.

Unfortunately, most natural systems are highly irregular, and this is true both for inanimate systems (consisting of dead matter) and for animate systems (living beings, from bacteria to sperm whales). To be sure, all animate systems manifest certain very clear regularities. In humans these are, for example, heartbeat, breathing, and the sleep-wake cycle. And there are similar regularities in inanimate nature, for example: temperature swings as surfaces become hotter during the day and radiate heat at night; tides, driven by lunar gravity. These regularities are captured by physics, chemistry, biology, and their subdisciplines. But they make up only a tiny fraction of the behaviors of these systems.

The reason for this is that *most natural systems are* *non-ergodic*, which is a technical way of saying that the processes which occur in such systems are overwhelmingly irregular. “Ergodicity” is a term from thermodynamics, the branch of physics which studies the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy, and the laws governing the conservation of energy from one form to another. An *ergodic system* is defined by the fact that the system elements move in the space of the system in a random fashion in such a way that each point of the system is visited with equal probability by all elements over time.

In a very simple (theoretical) example: an ergodic system with two elements, each of which can occupy one of nine points in space, would be such that each of the two elements would visit each of the nine points over time with equal likelihood. In a system like this, we can describe the behavior of the entire system by determining the trajectory of a certain sample of its elements. For another example, if you take a block of metal from a fridge and put it outside the fridge into a warmer environment and then wait until its temperature is in equilibrium with the temperature of its environment, the temperature will be constant throughout the block. The block will in this situation be ergodic. Or take as another example the pouring of milk into tea. Once you have stirred the mixture, its color will become homogeneous as the milk particles are uniformly distributed. With regard to the mix of water and milk in your cup of tea the system is ergodic. However, it is not ergodic along other dimensions; for example, with regard to its temperature during the period when the tea is still in the process of cooling down to room temperature. (We note that “equilibrium” and “entropy” are sister terms with “ergodicity” in the science of thermodynamics.)

We can now see that, when we want to talk about the ergodicity of a natural system, we must specify the relevant dimensions of the system behavior we want to talk about. Ergodic systems are *measure-preserving dynamical systems*. This means that as the system evolves over time, the probability of finding it in a given region of the coordinate system we use to mathematically describe it (its so-called “state space”) remains constant. An example is the orbital behavior of the planets in the Solar System, which illustrates also the way in which, after a certain time (in this case in ca. 5 billion years), ergodic system behavior can come to an end.

\_\_\_

For basic properties of the human mind, such as consciousness or emotionality, we have absolutely no idea how the nervous system is generating these phenomena. And we will never find out.

\_\_\_

Most natural systems are not ergodic because they exhibit *drivenness*. This means that there is energy that is changing its form as it flows through them. For example, when water pours into a basin from an outlet, mechanical energy is transformed into heat (you won’t notice it by feeling the water in the basin with your hand, but it happens). Such processes are fundamentally irregular and non-ergodic. There is no pattern of regularity in their state space. Therefore, for non-ergodic systems, we cannot model using mathematics the aspects of the system for which the non-ergodicity is relevant. Here again there is a lot to unpack.

**3. Why we cannot model the most interesting aspects of non-ergodic systems**

The behavior of artificial systems is simple when compared to that of natural systems such as the weather. Consider, for example, the *internal combustion engine*. Here we have good models of how much fuel they need to burn (oxidize) per unit of time in order to yield a certain amount of kinetic energy. This holds of the rotations of the engine in your car and also of the propulsion of a jet turbine. We can then insert these engines into all kinds of bigger machines, such as cars or missiles, and calculate very precisely how they will behave, since the transformation of chemical into kinetic energy is ergodic at the overall (macro)-level. At the same time, we cannot model which molecule of fuel moves exactly where in the combustion chamber and gets oxidized in order to yield kinetic energy via expansion. The energy transformation at this level is non-ergodic. But so long as the overall function of the system is regular and ergodic it is suitable to serve the purposes of engineering.

For natural systems, the situation is much more complicated. Let’s start with a predominantly inanimate system, the weather. Multiple forces are here acting together, all of them driven by the sun’s energy. The latter brings about pressure differences involving air, water, and ground temperature in interaction with the detailed geographical properties of the area over a given period of time. This system is highly driven. It is dynamic, multi-causal, and deeply non-ergodic. Therefore, its regularities are nearly everywhere superficial and short-lived. They are often also highly regional, since the geographic properties typically differ from one place to the next, especially those which are inhabited by humans.

Our weather models exploit these regularities, but their prediction range is short because the salient regularities do not hold over time. Due to the nature of the weather systems, we can obtain only partial mathematical models of the weather. And this will always be the case, so that our inability to predict the weather over longer periods of time is here to stay.

The same is true for animate systems, including all living beings. We can model certain aspects of living beings—that is the reason we have pesticides and herbicides, and also medicine. But [there are many aspects we cannot model](https://iai.tv/articles/organisms-are-musicians-not-machines-auid-3436). These are the *dominant properties* of animate systems, the characteristics that differentiate them from other systems, and they are always non-ergodic and deeply irregular. In honeybees, for example, the irregularity of their detailed behavior is so dominant that it is not possible to reproduce such behavior in a way that will allow a unified mathematical model that can be shared across laboratories. This is so even when we use genetically [*identical bees*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.15280373), and even if the bees are all harnessed in the same way and thereby also massively restricted in their behavioral options.

For basic properties of the human mind, such as consciousness or emotionality, we have absolutely no idea how the nervous system is generating these phenomena. And [we will never find out, because these systems are too deeply complex and thereby also deeply non-ergodic](https://iai.tv/articles/biology-not-physics-holds-the-key-to-reality-auid-3163). And thus also not—not ever—capable of being emulated inside a machine.

**4. Consequences**

We can now see that the widespread assumption that we will over time be able to model and manipulate everything is bound to fail. [We will not have reliable climate models](https://iai.tv/articles/we-still-dont-understand-climate-change-auid-2843). We cannot arbitrarily and predictably “program biology in the same way in which we write software and program computers” (US Executive Order 14081). We will not enhance human cognition via genetic engineering. [We do not even know how body height is genetically encoded](https://iai.tv/articles/your-genes-do-not-define-you-auid-2969), but we do know that a [huge proportion of the genome](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5536862/) is needed to explain even simple traits. Neither will machines read thoughts or AI algorithms become conscious or [intelligent](https://www.routledge.com/Why-Machines-Will-Never-Rule-the-World-Artificial-Intelligence-without-Fear/Landgrebe-Smith/p/book/9781032941400) in the way humans are. Indeed, the limitations on so-called Large Language Models, limitations which the AI community is just now beginning to confront, will be seen to be insurmountable. LLMs will work in a reliable fashion, but only so long as they are dealing with ergodic patterns. Where such patterns are absent they will continue to fail.

If, on the other hand, we concentrate our scientific and technological efforts on regular aspects of non-ergodic natural systems, these still offer material for endless mathematical models and for fruitful technical exploitation. Furthermore, non-ergodic processes can to some extent also be elucidated heuristically (for example, deceleration with rubber tires is not modeled exactly, but we can slow down our cars really well). Good scientists have always known that they have to concentrate on these patterns, and they have given us not only great insights but also the many wonders of the technosphere in which we live today, with great medicine, transportation, and telecommunication, not to mention exhilaratingly addictive video game technology.
