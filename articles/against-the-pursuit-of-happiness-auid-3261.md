# Against the pursuit of happiness

*Why you should not try to be happy*

**By Lorraine Besser** | *17th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,544 words*

![Against the pursuit of happiness](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/against-the-pursuit-of-happpiness.webp)

---

*From the US founding fathers to utilitarianism, hedonism, and today's self-help, philosophers and thinkers have strived to articulate why we should place happiness and well-being above other things and how we can achieve them. [Professor Lorraine Besser](https://lorrainebesser.com/), on the contrary, argues that we should stop seeking happiness. Whether we are happy or not is largely out of our control. Instead, we should try to achieve different interesting and valuable experiences that we can control, and just enjoy happiness when it comes.*

Happiness. The very word strikes a chord. We wish we could have it. We think about what’s holding us back from it and the “if-only” tracks start to fly: if only I had more money, a different job, fewer responsibilities, a house with more room or a bigger yard, the perfect partner or the perfect children. [We read all the news articles about happiness](http://iai.tv/video/happiness). We try to make more friends. We try to meditate. We try to be optimistic. We think about moving to Sweden.

\_\_\_

Giving up the pursuit of happiness feels like a cop-out, as if we are wasting the gift of life. But what if I were to tell you that giving up on happiness is the first step to a better life?

\_\_\_

Whether we go for everyday changes or big ones, more often than not, we find ourselves in the same place. [That glorious state of happiness we are trying so hard to get is still out of reach](http://iai.tv/articles/pursuing-happiness-is-a-mistake-auid-1835). Should we try harder, and double down on our efforts to be happy? It is hard to imagine that we should not. To give up on a life of happiness, to stop striving to get to a better place, to accept the level of happiness we’re currently at, all seem poor choices. Giving up the pursuit of happiness feels like a cop-out, as if we are wasting the gift of life. But what if I were to tell you that giving up on happiness is the first step to a better life?

It's true. The best thing we can do for ourselves is to stop pursuing happiness. [The time and effort we spend pursuing happiness](http://iai.tv/video/life-s-secret) takes us farther away from that which we’re really after: a good life, full of valuable experiences.

Lots of things happen when we try to be happy. But none of them delivers what we are after: a happier life. Why not?

From a psychological point of view, the answer is that happiness just isn’t the kind of thing that we can harness, that we have agency over. Happiness is a mental state distinguished by feelings of pleasure. By nature, [happiness is an elusive goal](http://iai.tv/video/surplus-happiness), both hard to pursue and hard to sustain.

It’s hard to pursue for a few reasons. First, [unlike many of our goals, there is no recipe for becoming happier](http://iai.tv/video/desire-dreams-and-happiness). If we want to become more physically fit, there’s a reliable recipe that will get us there. Eat healthy foods, do some cardio, lift some weights. Repeat. Repeat. Repeat. It is not an easy recipe, but it is a reliable one. Make it through the steps, and you will enhance your physical fitness.

Where’s the recipe for becoming happier? Clearly, there is no one-size-fits-all recipe for happiness as there is with physical fitness. Happiness is a subjective state, that arises through the experience of pleasure. Human minds share much in common, which means that there are some shared sources of pleasure, but they are also very individualized. Going to a music festival, dancing amongst thousands of strangers, makes many people happy. But just as many people are liable to feel otherwise, aghast at the very thought of being so surrounded. Absent anything close to a universal formula, it is up to each person to create their own recipe for becoming happier.

The first step in trying to become happier is figuring out what makes you happy. As easy a task as this sounds, it is not. We’ve all heard that the grass is always greener on the other side. This isn’t just a metaphorical proverb; it’s a well-established psychological fact called [affective forecasting](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Wilson%20&%20Gilbert%20%28Advances%29.pdf)—our poor ability to predict how future events will affect our emotions. In general, people are simply bad at predicting how something will make them feel.

We think getting that promotion will make us happier, but it does not. We think that losing a job is the end of the world, destining us to a life of unhappiness, but it does not.  [Difficulties in affective forecasting make it highly unlikely that any one of us can build a recipe for becoming happier](http://iai.tv/articles/the-present-is-all-there-is-to-happiness-auid-2207https:/iai.tv/articles/the-present-is-all-there-is-to-happiness-auid-2207). The best we can do is build a recipe to get what we *think* will make us happier. But, really, what is the point of following a recipe that cannot get us to the place we want?

It just gets worse from here. Say you beat the odds and manage to find and do something that does make you happier. You make a new friend, you change jobs, you build your dream house. Whatever it is, the boost these changes deliver will—and must—be short-lived. This is because feelings of pleasure are physiologically based and designed for a certain purpose: to reward you for doing the things that are good for you. That’s why sex feels good; why exercising fires the endorphins; why eating also feels good while hunger feels bad. Pleasure originates within us as part of a reward system. And because it's a reward system designed to keep us doing the things that are good for us, pleasure must be short-lived. It must go away in order to motivate us to do these things again.

Even though our experiences of pleasure go well past that which was evolutionarily intended, they are always short-lived. The boosts they deliver cannot stick around for long. Be it in minutes or months, our minds will adapt to them. States of pleasure, and increased feelings of happiness, are states of arousal. What goes up must come down.  That’s one reason why [many psychologists believe that each of us has a happiness “set point”](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1999-02842-016) that we return to, time and time again. The good news is that we can’t stay sad for very long; the bad news is that, realistically, we can’t really become happier.

At this point I know what at least some of you are thinking: Can’t we just rig the system? At the very least, *I* can rig the system. [*I* can find the loopholes and *I* should try](http://iai.tv/articles/lifes-absurdity-is-a-cause-for-happiness-auid-2885); becoming happier is worth the effort. Maybe you are the exception. But here’s the road that line of thinking takes most of us down.

First, the reason we start thinking like this—that there *must* be a way to become happier—stems from the belief that *more is better.* More happiness is better than what I have now. But start thinking too much like this, and more will always be better; what you have will never be good enough. This is especially true in the context of feeling good. Something feels good, and then we adapt to it. So return to go for something even better, and better, and better than that. Just like someone with an addiction to drugs, we get stuck in a pursuit that will never/can never deliver.

\_\_\_

Do we really think that a life of sustained happiness is the best that life has to offer? Most of us know, deep down, that it isn’t.

\_\_\_

Second, the more we try to rig the system, the more we embrace “happiness” as an end goal and value it as such. But if happiness is always the end goal, the best thing that we can get, then everything else in our lives becomes valuable only insofar as it makes us happy. That new friend? Only valuable when they lift you up. That achievement you’ve fought so hard for? Only valuable if it makes you happier. Exercising? Only valuable if it gets the endorphins pumping. Yet what is clear is that [many of the activities or people we engage with are worthwhile even if they do not immediately produce happiness](http://iai.tv/articles/relationships-are-more-important-than-pleasure-auid-3177).

Weird things like this may crop up when we actively try to pursue happiness, while lasting, sustainable happiness remains out of reach. But do not despair. You can make your life better. You can make your life better by pursuing the things that will make it better, and that *can* be pursued.

Happiness is great, don’t get me wrong. That we can’t pursue it means only that we shouldn’t keep trying to pursue it. Instead, we should embrace it when it comes and relish the good feelings before they fade away. But when they do fade away, we should let them. It’s okay not to be happy all the time. It’s better, even. Do we really think that a life of sustained happiness is the best that life has to offer? Most of us know, deep down, that it isn’t. Even if it feels strange to admit, a life of sustained happiness really would not be that great. It would be boring. We’d become boring. We’d shut down our minds. We’d shrink, and our lives would shrink.

While it feels great, being happy is [just one way in which our lives go well](https://iai.tv/articles/optimism-and-the-good-life-auid-1135). The quality of our lives is the quality of our experiences. Happiness is experientially valuable—it’s good for us to experience—but so too are other states of mind. When we allow happiness to play the limited role within our lives that it is suited to playing, we open ourselves up to experience more from life.

One of the most valuable, yet often overlooked, ways in which our lives go better is when they are full of interesting experiences. Interesting experiences don’t always make us happier, but they still make our lives better. Happiness is not the only experiential value.

Interesting experiences are those that stimulate your mind to engage. They make you think, they make you feel, and they enhance your life. The best way to wrap your head around what interesting experiences are, and why they are valuable, is to think of your own experiences. Think of the last book that sucked you in and left you thinking about it well-after you put it down. Think of that person with whom you could talk for hours about nothing at all, conversations you walk away from feeling like something has shifted within you. Think of your first trip to another country, or even to a new state or town. Think about [how adventures make you feel](http://iai.tv/video/how-to-use-adventure-for-wellbeing); compare them to how spa days make you feel.

\_\_\_

The best, most reliable, thing we can do to have an interesting experience is to find something new, to go somewhere new, or to meet someone new. Novelty forces our minds to engage.

\_\_\_

Now think of how your life would be without any of these experiences—and without others that engage your mind, stimulate a free-fall of thoughts and emotions, generate an effortless, robust state of cognitive engagement. It would be worse, wouldn’t it? Of course. We need more out of life than happiness. And we can have it.

Experiences of interestingness are subjective. They depend completely on what stimulates your mind, and so on what’s going on in your mind. That they are mind-dependent makes them much more within our control than feelings of happiness, which arise within the mind but are dependent upon and controlled by our basic physiology. For one thing, we don’t adapt to interesting experiences. If we’re having one, we’ll have it as long as we keep engaging. At some point, our mind gets worn out and needs to rest, but, in general, the shelf-life of interesting experiences goes far beyond that of happiness.

Even better, there are recipes that reliably deliver experiences of interestingness! The key is to go about it indirectly. The worst thing we can do is make step-by-step plans to have interesting experiences. While over time we may get a sense of the kinds of things we find interesting, it’s difficult to predict exactly what those will be. (Anyone ever picked up a book you thought would be interesting, just to put it down after a chapter? This happens all the time.) Moreover, some of the most interesting experiences are the ones you don’t expect, and that you haven’t planned for. (Anyone ever done something they didn’t want to do only to find it fascinating? Or planned out a trip so much that the sites ended up being disappointing?)

\_\_\_

Don’t waste your time trying to be happy, for doing so is not likely to make your life better. Go for interestingness instead.

\_\_\_

The best, most reliable, thing we can do to have an interesting experience is to find something new, to go somewhere new, or to meet someone new. Novelty forces our minds to engage; cognitive engagement is our natural response to novelty. When faced with something new, our minds strive to make sense of it[; novelty stimulates cognitive engagement](http://iai.tv/video/the-crisis-of-the-new). If we allow our minds to engage, we’ll be more apt to have an interesting experience.

But interesting experiences don’t have to be novel. Novelty is simply one reliable source of interestingness insofar that it forces the mind to engage. We don’t always have to force the mind to engage, though. We can develop a mindset that invites—even, creates—interesting experiences.

Bringing an open mind to your experiences is sometimes all you need to have an interesting one. Drop the expectations, lighten up on the planning, go in with an open mind, and see what happens. Try to notice things, maybe the things you’d otherwise look over. Try not to evaluate or judge what you see or who you are engaging with—doing so will shut your mind down and stop an interesting experience in its tracks. Try instead to give yourself over to whatever thoughts or emotions crop up. See what happens.

Being curious will always help stimulate interestingness. If you’re doing your best to be open, yet can’t get your mind going, start asking yourself “why?” Turn yourself over to wonder. But don’t forget to be open along the way. Do not reach for the phone and start googling. Like judgments, concrete answers stop interesting experiences in their tracks.

Developing a mindset marked by openness and curiosity will help you have more interesting experiences. So too, will noticing things. Sources of novelty abound, even within familiar environments. If you look, you can find something new. And if you bring an open and curious mind towards the things you notice, you are more likely to have an interesting experience. You will have *created* an interesting experience.

Over time, it won’t even feel like you are trying. You will naturally find yourself noticing new things, questioning what’s going on around you, letting your mind run. Everything can be interesting if you approach it with an interesting mindset.

Happiness isn’t the only way to experience value in life; having interesting experiences, for instance, adds value to our lives. It’s a different kind of value. Interesting experiences aren’t always pleasant, but they still make our lives better. That they’re different from happiness makes them so much easier to have. Don’t waste your time trying to be happy, for doing so is not likely to make your life better. Go for interestingness instead. It might take a mental shift, but you can do it. You can add value to your life by making it more interesting.
