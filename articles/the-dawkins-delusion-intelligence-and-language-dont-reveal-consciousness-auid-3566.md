# The Dawkins delusion: Intelligence and language don't reveal consciousness

*The elephant in the room*

**By Ken Mogi** | *6th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,356 words*

![The Dawkins delusion: Intelligence and language don't reveal consciousness](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-dawkins-delusion3.webp)

---

*Richard Dawkins thinks Anthropic’s Claude AI might be conscious. The man who denied the existence of God so fiercely is arguably siding with those who think we are now creating a digital one. However, University of Tokyo scientist and Sony senior researcher, **Ken Mogi**, here argues that Dawkins' Claude – or Claudia – experiment only reveals the lack of progress made on consciousness studies in the last few decades and argues the real magic in AI is found in how it deals with language.*

The assertion by Richard Dawkins, recently published in *UnHerd*, that large language models such as Claude are conscious, is quite an interesting take, but understandably caused a storm on the internet. To be precise, Dawkins opined that [Claude's behavior cast some serious doubts on the evolutionary significance of consciousness](https://unherd.com/2026/05/is-ai-the-next-phase-of-evolution/). If Claude was unconscious, and yet exhibited the remarkable linguistic abilities that deeply impressed the highly intelligent Dawkins, then what was the functional significance of consciousness? Although technically the question of consciousness in AI was left open at the end of the article, the overall tone was clearly in favor of AI consciousness.

Many questioned the renowned evolutionary biologist's (from the critics’ points of view) naive and gullible conviction that Claude (which Dawkins named Claudia) possessed consciousness. Dawkins is actually not the first to make such a suggestion. Many people, including a Google engineer who went public in a high-profile case [asserting that an AI developed by the tech giant was evidently conscious](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2022/06/11/google-ai-lamda-blake-lemoine/), made similar claims. The fact that the Dawkins article made such a giant ripple is testimony to the weight any opinion expressed by the author of *The Selfish Gene*, *The Blind* *Watchmaker*, and *The God Delusion* has in the eyes of the public. At the same time, the controversy is something potentially more general and profound, touching on the very foundations of consciousness and intelligence, both natural and artificial.

The question of whether AI systems are conscious or not has grave implications and repercussions for humanity in the years to come, [discussed intensely in the burgeoning field of machine consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/a-landscape-of-consciousness). The modern renaissance in consciousness studies has a history spanning three decades, starting from events including the publication of papers by Francis Crick and Christof Koch in the 1990s. With the focus shifting recently towards the nature of consciousness, if any, in artificial intelligence, the scientific study of consciousness is gaining renewed legitimacy and urgency, as the question of machine consciousness needs to be given tangible answers within the window of a few years, if you go along with the timeline presented, for example, in [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), a high impact document forecasting the rapid development of machine intelligence. Dawkins’ assertion fits right into the optics of the rise of artificial machines, and naturally draws the attention of the general public.

Even if you disagree with the conclusion of Dawkins, it must be acknowledged that the AI consciousness assertion is based on a nuanced and detailed assessment of the capabilities of LLMs today. Things are not really fair for AI. As Dawkins suggested, it is not great to move the goalposts when it comes to assessing AI. The AI effect, the tendency of people to regard anything accomplished by AI as outside the range of genuine human intelligence, might also apply to consciousness. The AI effect in machine consciousness would imply that we humans would typically move the goalposts when it comes to assessing consciousness in a machine, reflecting our bias and self-defense rather than honest scientific appraisal.

The test of intelligence [proposed by Alan Turing in 1950](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2251299), referred to by Dawkins in his argument, has been more or less passed by artificial intelligence, although controversies as to specific implications do remain. According to the Turing test, therefore, we need to admit that artificial intelligence now has thinking abilities equivalent to humans. Indeed, artificial intelligence systems should surpass human intelligence very soon, if they have not already done so, in the controversial and yet seemingly inevitable trajectory toward technological singularity. The Dawkins assessment is based on a sense of wonder at Claude's subtle and sensitive understanding upon reading a novel that he is writing. There is certainly a personal touch in what Dawkins writes about his Claudia. However, dismissing his argument as another case of AI psychosis would not do justice to the subtlety and depth of what is at stake in this thesis, coming from one of the greatest scientists alive today.

Perhaps we should focus on the nature of language rather than consciousness per se. It is truly remarkable that AI systems such as Claude are exhibiting and developing abilities to grasp natural language at a level unthinkable even a few years ago. The [scaling hypothesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361), put forward, for example, by Dario Amodei, co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, which develops Claude, and colleagues, states that there would be a predictable and qualitative improvement of the capabilities of AI as more computational resources are put into the system. As the compute increases, involving more parameters, the qualities of the exchanges with large language models have certainly improved, sometimes beyond wildest expectations.

The enigma is perhaps in natural language itself. Bolstered by technological advancements such as the transformer architecture and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), the astonishing success of LLMs as the compute scales is ultimately due to the very nature of language. For reasons still not fully understood, intelligence based on natural language improves dramatically as compute scales. This is something we humans have experienced in history repeatedly, both in the context of individual linguistic developments from infancy to adulthood and in cases of luminaries such as William Shakespeare and Ludwig Wittgenstein. It is quite astonishing that AI systems, supported by the simple mechanism of next token prediction, can exhibit cognitive abilities ranging from essay writing to world models. Indeed, the power of AI is mainly the power of natural language, at least for now, before the age of physical AI kicks in, presumably soon.

For now, the rub is that we cannot really say that the power of AI is the power of artificial consciousness. To be honest, we cannot really say that we understand consciousness any better than three decades ago, when the modern revival of the scientific study of consciousness was initiated. There is a strange gap between the rapid progress of artificial intelligence and the stagnant state of consciousness studies. Machine consciousness, where research into machine intelligence and consciousness studies meet, is precisely the space where the cognitive strain shows. Richard Dawkins was spot on when he pointed out that if, despite its linguistic abilities, Claude is unconscious, there is something very strange at work.

Indeed, there is a widening perceptual gap between artificial intelligence and artificial consciousness – which is a vital one when we consider the ethical implications of AI seriously. It has been a service to the community that Dawkins has faced the elephant in the room, as a good evolutionary biologist. Now that the elephant is out, we ignore it at our own risk.

Anil Seth's argument, that [consciousness arises from properties unique to biological systems such as the human brain](https://www.noemamag.com/the-mythology-of-conscious-ai/), is welcomed by people who are skeptical about machine consciousness. Although Seth certainly has a point in [basing consciousness on the robust function and evolution of life](https://iai.tv/articles/anil-seth-the-hallucination-of-consciousness-auid-2525), the biological naturalist argument would not be of much help, as it stands now, in answering first principles questions about machine consciousness. It would be interesting to consider the [biological foundations of natural language and consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-in-the-machine), especially because it would provide a much-needed model of embodied cognition, which would ultimately link artificial and natural intelligence.

Richard Dawkins has a track record of pinpointing important lines of investigation, such as the selfish genes, extended phenotypes, and memes. With AI consciousness, perhaps Dawkins has done it again. As conscious Claudia tricking Dawkins becomes a meme on the web, perhaps it is time to consider the elephant in the room of machine consciousness and take it seriously, [in the biological context led by Dawkins](https://iai.tv/video/the-case-for-conscious-ai), because it is, after all, an extended phenotype, created by us humans on earth.
