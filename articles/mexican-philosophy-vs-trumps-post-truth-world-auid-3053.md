# Mexican philosophy vs Trump's post-truth world

*Perspectival philosophy in the new era of Trump*

**By Carlos Alberto Sanchez** | *21st January 2025* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,187 words*

*"In a post-Trump world, the fight for truth is also a fight against universality." Professor of Philosophy, Carlos Alberto Sanchez argues Mexican philosophy challenges Western universalism, offering a perspectival approach to truth rooted in lived experience, an antidote to the divisive narratives of our times.*

*I was privileged to speak with Professor Carlos Alberto Sanchez. His latest work, [Blooming in the Ruins: How Mexican Philosophy Can Guide Us Towards a Good Life](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/blooming-in-the-ruins-9780197691007?cc=us&lang=en&), offers a deeply provocative exploration of Mexico's rich and often overlooked philosophical tradition. Professor Sanchez, it’s a pleasure to have you with us.*

**To start, can you share a bit about the background of this book? What inspired you to write it, and what should people know before diving into its pages?**

Well, before diving into the specifics, it’s important to understand the significance of the book itself. Mexican philosophy hasn’t been a subject widely discussed or studied, particularly in English. Until recently, say, around 2012 to 2016, there wasn’t much promotion or translation of Mexican philosophical texts. This book reflects a larger movement to bring Mexican philosophy into broader conversations.

**What exactly is Mexican philosophy? How would you define it for someone new to the concept?**

Mexican philosophy is, at its core, an attempt by Mexican philosophers to have a conversation with philosophy, especially European philosophy, but in doing so, they recognize their difference. They’re bringing something to the table that hasn’t been said before.

At its heart, Mexican philosophy is circumstantial. It’s rooted in the lived history and experiences of the Mexican people, unlike the universal ambitions of Western philosophy. This doesn’t mean it’s exclusively for Mexicans. Rather, it’s a grounded philosophy that speaks from a specific context but resonates universally because of its authenticity and depth.

**How does Mexican philosophy approach truth differently from the Western tradition?**

Western philosophy often sees truth as universal, abstract, and detached. Think of philosophers like Plato or Kant striving for timeless, objective truths. But Mexican philosophy takes a perspectival approach, it sees truth as grounded in lived experience, shaped by history, culture, and circumstances.

Mexican philosophers argue that universal truths often serve to erase or dominate other perspectives, particularly those of colonized or marginalized people. Instead, they propose that truth is always perspectival, always tied to who you are and where you’re speaking from. This is a liberating idea because it allows for multiple truths to coexist, reflecting the richness of human experience.

**Why do Mexican philosophers reject universal truths, and how does this impact their view of what it means to be human?**

One of those things that Mexican philosophers react to is this idea that the West has a monopoly on what counts as a human being, for example, right? What counts as human dignity. And this is a universal idea. So when it gets to people in Mexico that are trying to think about their own history, right? Their own colonized history, they want to reject those universal ideas because they're part of the colonizing project.

They want to talk about what it means to be human from their own experience and not accept these universal concepts right off the bat.

**Why do you think this perspectival approach is particularly important today, in the context of global divisions and misinformation?**

In a world shaped by Trump-era politics, [fake news, and polarized narratives](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2016/nov/10/facebook-fake-news-election-conspiracy-theories), a perspectival approach to truth is crucial. Universalist claims, those that pretend to speak for everyone, are often used to silence dissenting voices or impose a single narrative. Mexican philosophy challenges this by showing that every truth is tied to a perspective, and no single perspective can claim total authority.

This doesn’t mean there’s no truth, it means we have to acknowledge the context and experiences behind any claim. It’s a way of resisting divisive, top-down narratives and instead fostering dialogue and understanding.

**One concept you discuss in the book is *nepantla*, the state of being in-between. Could you elaborate on this idea and its significance?**

Absolutely. [*Nepantla*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepantla) refers to the experience of being between worlds, between cultures, histories, or identities. For Mexicans, this often means being caught between their indigenous heritage and European colonial influences. They can’t fully claim either identity, so they inhabit this in-between space.

This resonates far beyond Mexico. Many people of colour in the West live in a state of *nepantla*. Take my own experience: as a Mexican American in California, I’m not seen as white, but if I go to Mexico, I’m not seen as fully Mexican either. I’m constantly navigating this middle ground, as are many others who live between cultures, traditions, or ideologies.

**You argue that truth itself can be understood as *nepantla*. Could you explain this connection?**

Yes, that’s a key point. If we accept that truth is perspectival, then it’s also in a state of *nepantla*. Truth exists in the in-between space where different perspectives meet and negotiate. It’s not something fixed or static; it’s dynamic, always shifting depending on who’s speaking and what their circumstances are.

This understanding of truth allows for more inclusive and pluralistic conversations. It encourages us to listen to marginalized voices and consider perspectives we might otherwise dismiss. In a way, *nepantla* makes truth more human, it acknowledges the complexity and messiness of real life.

**How does this perspectival approach connect to the broader ethical lessons in your book?**

If we see truth as perspectival and grounded in experience, it becomes easier to navigate uncertainty and ambiguity in life. Mexican philosophy teaches us to embrace this uncertainty instead of trying to impose rigid frameworks or universal answers.

For example, [Leopoldo Zea](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/philosophy-mexico/#LeopZea) critiques what he calls the “imperial passion” of Western thought, the drive to categorize, systematize, and dominate. Instead, he advocates for a philosophy that speaks from lived experience and resists dehumanizing frameworks. This has profound ethical implications: it encourages us to respect diversity, value personal stories, and challenge oppressive systems.

Mexican philosophy resists this by emphasizing lived experience over imposed frameworks. It encourages us to speak our truths into the world in ways that reflect who we are, not what others expect us to be.

**Your work suggests that Mexican philosophy isn’t just for Mexicans. What lessons do you think it offers a global audience?**

Mexican philosophy teaches us to embrace life’s contradictions and uncertainties. It challenges the notion of a single, universal truth and instead celebrates the richness of multiple perspectives. This is a lesson everyone can apply, whether you’re navigating cultural differences, political divides, or personal challenges.

The key takeaway is this: truth is never universal. It’s always grounded in who we are, where we come from, and how we see the world. By acknowledging this, we can build a more compassionate, inclusive, and authentic way of living.
