# The hidden prejudice tearing society apart

*Beliefism and how to reduce it*

**By Paul Dolan** | *30th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,476 words*

![The hidden prejudice tearing society apart](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-hidden-prejudice-tearing-society-apart.webp)

---

*We tend to think of prejudice as just being about sexism, racism, and classism. But could this be too narrow a view? LSE professor Paul Dolan argues many of us practice a subtler kind of discrimination—beliefism—where we judge people's entire character on the basis of a single opinion. In an age of culture wars and social media echo chambers, beliefism fuels division and moral certainty. But if we learn to recognize it, Dolan argues, we can begin to build tolerance for those we most disagree with.*

“Some of my best friends are black.” This was a phrase used by white people until a decade or so ago against accusations of being racist. It had the opposite effect. But perhaps we shouldn’t be so quick to dismiss it. I think it’s fair to say, all else equal, that a white person with black friends would be less racist than a white person with no black friends. Having black friends is a behavior that signals relatively more tolerance than having no black friends at all. As the Japanese proverb says: “When the character of a person is not clear, look at their friends.”

If you’re not convinced, consider the beliefs of someone’s friends. Would you conclude that someone who only had friends with the same set of beliefs was less tolerant than someone who had friends with different beliefs? If you surround yourself only with people who share similar views to you, then you are, by definition, discriminating against those who disagree—this is [*beliefism*](https://iai.tv/video/beliefism-paul-dolan). This can have adverse spillover effects. An employer may pass a perfectly good candidate over for a job, for example, because they find out that they hold different views about an issue that the employer cares about (abortion, say) but which is unrelated to the employee’s prospective productivity at work.

\_\_\_

There is no moral value to the claim that some of my best friends are racist.

\_\_\_

Beliefism spillovers have been shown to impact various aspects of economic behavior. For example, in Ghana, around the time of the 2008 elections, taxi riders were asked to hail a cab and relay an opening script in their ethnic mother tongue to begin the negotiation. The rider and driver learn one another’s ethnicity through language and accent and infer partisanship based on the typical nesting of ethnic groups in parties, just as they would in everyday life. [The results showed](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/does-electoral-competition-exacerbate-interethnic-or-interpartisan-economic-discrimination-evidence-from-a-field-experiment-in-market-price-bargaining/D0E5FEE73DC561F56730000B23E65F00) that the cabbies were inclined to accept lower fares from passengers who shared their political affiliation while demanding higher prices from those who supported opposing parties.

Logically, there is no difference between racism, sexism, classism, beliefism or any other “ism” in this regard. Clearly, we can quite legitimately choose to be intolerant of people who are themselves intolerant. And we are certainly under no obligation to be friends with someone who is racist—or beliefist. There is no moral value to the claim that some of my best friends are racist. But when we use someone’s (different but not abhorrent) beliefs on one issue to completely dismiss them, and all their other beliefs, we are guilty of using a tiny fraction of information about that person to judge their whole character.

Beliefism is predicated on our propensity to take sides, and to split ideas and other people into “right” and “wrong,” “good” and “bad.” We pick a side and stick with it. Quite quickly, we can become entrenched in the views of our in-group and become unwilling to accept anything the out-group might have to say. We like people more when they resemble us, including how they view the world: “birds of a feather flock together.” Groups of like-minded people can be very effective, and sometimes dissenting voices can get in the way of effective decision-making.

But there are downsides too. Perhaps the most noteworthy negative consequence of group cognition is *groupthink.* This is where a group of individuals reaches and reinforces a consensus without a proper evaluation of the alternatives. At its core, groupthink stems from a collective desire to maintain harmony and avoid conflict. If someone does disagree, it can be very hard for them to speak out. Members of the group may isolate themselves from outside opinions, believing that their decisions are superior or that outsiders wouldn't understand.

The world would be a better place if there was less beliefism, less groupthink and more tolerance of different perspectives and the people that hold them. There would be less conflict, better hiring decisions, and more economic and social progress. Well-functioning societies not only tolerate a wide range of perspectives; they celebrate them. We currently face [radical uncertainty](https://iai.tv/video/danger-safety-and-the-future), for example around Artificial Intelligence (AI). Can anyone confidently claim to know what’s going to happen next with generative AI? It makes no sense to ignore anyone’s views about it. Decision-making in households, corporations, and institutions could all be enhanced by a greater willingness to listen to different perspectives and people.

\_\_\_

We need to overcome the human tendency to favour those who agree with us and the psychological need for certainty and consistency.

\_\_\_

So, reducing beliefism is not going to be easy. We need to overcome the human tendency to favour those who agree with us and the psychological need for certainty and consistency, which can make it hard for us to update our beliefs, including our views of other people and groups, even when faced with compelling evidence to do so. We are drawn toward forming and being in groups where we can all nod along in agreement with one another rather than shake our heads in disagreement. There’s a whole tide of cognitive, [emotional](https://iai.tv/articles/the-divide-between-belief-and-emotion-is-an-illusion-auid-3396), and social beliefism to swim against if we are to become more tolerant.

So, the only effective way to reduce beliefism and its harmful spillovers is to make it easier for us to be less beliefist without having to think too hard about it. We might think that we like a challenge, but we really don’t: the path of least resistance is most often the preferred route. Willpower is weak but, thankfully, the power of design is strong. It is possible to make a habit of being less beliefist—to be tolerant by design. [In my book](https://pauldolan.co.uk/books-1), I gather up the ways of designing environments to reduce beliefism under a new checklist: EMBRACE.

**E**nvironment   We need to emphasize situational factors

**M**istakes        We need be allowed to make mistakes, and to learn from them

**B**onding         We need to remember that we are similar in so many ways

**R**eason          We need better evidence and more coherent narratives

**A**ffectWe need to improve our emotional reactions to perspectives and people

**C**ollection       We need a diversity of people and perspectives in decision-making

**E**xposure        We need to spend more time with people who disagree with us

I don’t have the space to go into every element, but let me say a few words about mistakes, because this should resonate with readers of the IAI, whose mission is “to explore the cracks in our thinking, to change how we think and how we change the world.”  This can only happen if we create a space to [make mistakes and to learn from them.](https://iai.tv/video/bart-streumer-in-depth-interview-error-theory-metaethics) Recent evidence supporting the beneficial effects of embracing mistakes can be found in various studies across organizational behavior, leadership, and innovation management fields.

There are various ways for organizations to embed error management into their workplace culture. Leaders can openly share their mistakes, and the lessons learned, setting a tone that mistakes are an acceptable part of growth. Structured debriefs after projects or significant decisions can help teams focus on what went well and badly while avoiding assigning blame.

Error management can be integrated into training and development, and support can be provided for employees who are navigating the aftermath of a mistake with mentors and peer support groups.

In viewing mistakes as lessons learned, we view the people who make mistakes differently. We move from taking one mistake as a sign that they are the kind of person who *always* makes mistakes, and toward seeing them as someone who *sometimes* gets things wrong. This means that we will listen to their views. We might be even more inclined to listen to them if they admit their mistakes and seek to put them right. We all make mistakes, so it’s not really the messing up that matters, but rather the cleaning up.

Beyond mistakes, we should all reflect on the practical steps that we can take to incorporate the EMBRACE framework more deeply into our everyday lives and institutional practices. It is for all of us—private citizens and parents, practitioners and policymakers, employees, and employers—to actively design our interactions to be less beliefist. My call to arms is to move forward with the intention to not only tolerate difference but to truly appreciate that different perspectives enrich our lives and societies.
