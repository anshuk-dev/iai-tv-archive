# Homer's Greeks had no inner life

*Julian Jaynes and why consciousness may be younger than the pyramids*

**By Brian J. McVeigh** | *11th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *2,995 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Homer's Greeks had no inner life](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/homers-greeks-had-no-inner-life-3.webp)

---

*In Homer's* Iliad, *we rarely encounter the original Greek word for 'I' or much that refers to internal thought or decision-making. In this challenging article, cultural theorist **Brian J. McVeigh** argues Princeton psychologist Julian Jaynes got it right when he claimed the Homeric Greeks had no internal life or self-awareness. On the 50th anniversary of Jayne's classic book,* The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind*, which inspired the HBO series* Westworld*, McVeigh argues that the ancient Greeks took the voices of their gods as their impetus for action and that they indeed had no inner life.*

Imagine a theoretical framework that solves a long list of mysteries: the origin of religion and its concomitant features—supernatural visitations, ancestor worship, monumental mortuary architecture, divination, oracles, spirit possession, and the vestiges of archaic spirituality still visible in churches, temples, and mosques. This approach also explains otherwise inexplicable phenomena, such as hallucinations, hypnosis, glossolalia, and children’s imaginary companions. Moreover, this theoretical approach has clinical value: its argument about the originally nonpathological, adaptive nature of hallucinations helped establish an intellectual climate conducive to the Hearing Voices Network, which regards voice-hearing as a meaningful experience. Moreover, this framework comes with hypotheses waiting to be tested.

Such a framework can be found in [*The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind*](https://ia601802.us.archive.org/18/items/The_Origin_Of_Consciousness_In_The_Breakdown_Of_The_Bicameral_Mind_Julian_Jaynes_1976.pdf/The%20Origin%20of%20Consciousness%20in%20the%20Breakdown%20of%20the%20Bicameral%20Mind_%20Julian%20Jaynes_%201976.pdf) (1976) by Julian Jaynes (1920–1997). He theorized that until about three thousand years ago (with some geographic variation) humans possessed a “bicameral” (“two-chambered”) mentality in which the right hemisphere communicated with the left hemisphere using command hallucinations. These were interpreted as the voices of gods, ancestors, or godlike rulers. But population expansion and social complexity put pressure on this dual psychological system of supernatural superior and mortal subordinate. During the late second millennium BCE, this two-tier neurocultural arrangement collapsed. In response, languages—by developing metaphoric “mind words”—upgraded psychological processes. Jaynes identified the new mentality as “consciousness” (or in a wordier rendering, subjective introspectable self-awareness). Jaynesian consciousness is neither an innate, biologically fixed trait, nor something identified with sensation, thinking, or being awake. It is a [learned cultural innovation](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-beyond-the-brain).

\_\_\_

In a radical claim that confounds many, Jaynes postulated that conscious mentality is a recent cultural achievement that must be acquired anew by each generation through language.

\_\_\_

In the modern world, residues of a preconscious, bicameral mentality greatly influence us. Sacred architecture, less intense but still relevant religious beliefs, rites of passage wrapped in hallowed ceremonies, and the watered-down divine right of kings that echo through our governmental institutions—these are all so ubiquitous that their spiritual origins have faded into the background. The challenge, then, becomes defamiliarizing ourselves with what we thought we understood by re-examining what is hidden in plain sight using a Jaynesian interpretation. I introduced this essay with some provocative claims. Let’s unpack them.

**Challenging establishment psychology**

Jaynes was a comparative psychologist who, like other researchers in his discipline, initially assumed that [“consciousness” was a feature of all organisms](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-is-biologys-biggest-blunder) and was a result of evolution. But his search for the earliest references to something akin to conscious mentality in the historical record led to a surprising discovery: texts of ancient languages that we have from before 1,000 BCE lack psychological terminology. Jaynes was also struck by the overwhelming ubiquity of descriptions of divine revelations. He did not deny the principles of biological evolution. However, he eventually realized the limitations of evolutionary psychology; by itself, this otherwise powerful paradigm cannot explain the origins of consciousness. Jaynes concluded that though consciousness is grounded in our neuroanatomy, it is [fundamentally an ideational phenomenon](https://iai.tv/video/consciousness-has-evolutionary-roots-with-eva-jablonka) that is transmitted through socialization, not genes.

Jaynes’s recognition of the role of historical-cultural factors was, from the point of view of mainstream psychology, highly unconventional. By utilizing evidence from the not-too-distant past to understand human mentality, he combined two fields that seem especially unrelated: recent history and neuroscience. Jaynes challenged cherished methodological assumptions, transcended traditional disciplinary borders, and defied disciplinary categorization. This makes him difficult to pin down as a thinker.

How has Jaynes been received, criticized, and (mis)understood over the years? The first type of reaction can be called “serious–scientific.” A number of assessments of Jaynes have been fair, balanced, and informative. Presumably, whether such reviewers of Jaynes buy his arguments or not, it is clear that evenhanded appraisals begin with reading what Jaynes actually wrote, and most importantly, understanding what he intended. Next are “popular–scientific” accounts. A common problem among popular but scientific readings of Jaynes is a stunning failure to come to terms with how Jaynes defined consciousness. As in any discussion, if the premise of one’s argument is not appreciated, one has already lost the battle of persuading others of one’s hypothesis. For many (mis)interpreters of Jaynes, the word “consciousness” acts as a blank canvas upon which they draw and impose their own definitions of what they assume Jaynes meant by this term. The consequence is a distortion of his arguments. Finally, “New Age–utopian” interpretations attribute to consciousness a magical meaning, as if it were a mysterious power, a cosmic force unfolding through time, helping individuals in their quest for personal transformation. The utopianism signals how certain commentators (sometimes inspired by an overly simplistic account of left–right brain interrelations) associate Jaynes with pseudo-scientific social Darwinism (i.e., society is progressing toward some grand telos). Such a maneuver indicates the faddish fetishization of consciousness rather than an objective appraisal of what Jaynes actually hypothesized.

**Defining conscious mentality**

Viewed from the perspective of “consciousness,” psychology faces a crisis, as seen in how many researchers sow confusion by their muddled use of this word. It is often unclear if they mean perceptual reactivity (registering something through the senses); cognition (reasoning, judging); wakefulness (not dulled by sleep, faintness, or stupor); a mental state contrasted with being in a coma; or an all-purpose sense describing any kind of mentation. For many, consciousness is a topic of fascination. The word consciousness imparts a mystique that makes otherwise careful researchers jump to unwarranted conclusions. Given its promiscuous mention in academic and nonacademic writings, consciousness has become the Holy Grail of psychology and neuroscience. It is often written about undefined and imbued with a vagueness that should embarrass well-credentialed investigators. Those with a mystical bent appear in pursuit of this sacred relic of psychology. Even physicists are in on this great quest in what looks like “quantum mysticism.” Because psychologists, neuroscientists, philosophers of mind, popular science journalists, and AI enthusiasts begin their inquiries with [different understandings of consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/the-structure-of-consciousness), they end up communicating at cross-purposes. The result is an “anything goes” free-for-all. This is not conducive to advancing science.

How did Jaynes himself understand consciousness? First of all, he did not mean any of the aforementioned definitions; he intended something specific, and he did not mean something inherited genetically and baked into neuroanatomy. Rather, in a radical claim that confounds many, he postulated that conscious mentality is a recent cultural achievement that must be acquired anew by each generation through language. It is a consequence of historical changes, not an evolutionary outcome. Jaynesian consciousness is linguistically constructed with a metaphorical mental vocabulary that convinces us that a “space” exists within our person in which an imaginary “I” moves about and “sees” imagistic sceneries. It is an introcosm of the external world. This type of cognition miniaturizes and interiorizes the real world (hence “conscious interiority,” another term for Jaynesian consciousness). Since the narratizing self of Jaynesian consciousness replaced the decision-making prerogative of deities and ancestors, this form of specialized, souped-up cognition is intimately bound up with volition, and, like mathematics, it allows us to shortcut behaviors and arrive at decisions more effectively.

Some rush to link Jaynesian psychology to “animal consciousness,” which, for Jaynes, did not exist. However, being a comparative psychologist, he certainly believed animals possess complex minds (I can remember him detailing for me the intricacies of a squirrel’s brain). And Jaynes would not accept “[AI consciousness](https://iai.tv/video/ai-consciousness-cannot-exist-markus-gabriel).” Such speculation is a nonstarter, since he emphasized that conscious mentality is a historical-cultural adaptation that is reified through linguistic practices informed by embodied experiences.

Jaynes enumerated eight features of conscious mentality. I have expanded his list. These “features/functions of conscious interiority” (FOCI) describe psychocultural techniques of adaptive reframing and metaframing that help us navigate the world better: (1) mental space (a place for manipulating mental images visible only to an individual’s mind’s eye); (2) introception (employing quasi-perceptual mental images to simulate the physical world, distinct from interoception); (3) “I” (the active, agentive, observing aspect of selfhood); (4) “me” (the passive, receptive, observed aspect of selfhood; the interplay of “I” and “me” increases role/perspective taking); (5) self-narratization (imaginary personal timeline that intensifies retrospection, prospection, and planning capabilities); (6) excerption (editing mental contents for higher-order conceptualization); (7) consilience (fitting conceptions together so as to amplify abstraction); (8) concentration (introceived analog of perceptual attention that peripheralizes unrelated mental material in order to enhance focus); (9) suppression (introceived analog of physical avoidance that deletes distracting and distressing thoughts); (10) self-authorization (attributing one’s decisions and volitional behavior to one’s own person); (11) self-autonomy (bolstering self-direction and self-efficacy); (12) self-individuation (highlighting personal characteristics in opposition to others); (13) self-reflexivity (the indescribable sense of one’s own selfness that facilitates insight, self-objectivity, and self-corrective abilities).

**Jaynes’s four hypotheses**

It is easy to forget that the Newtonian, Darwinian, and Einsteinian intellectual edifices were built over many years, painstakingly solidified brick by brick through batteries of well-designed experiments and careful analyses. As in other [grand theorizings](https://iai.tv/articles/there-will-never-be-a-theory-of-everything-auid-3090), the Jaynesian paradigm requires hypothesis-testing and experimentation to be confirmed. Big theories require big data. In order to inject some manageability into a complex, multi-, and interdisciplinary endeavor, we need to break the project down into more wieldy postulations. This is what Jaynes proposed with his four hypotheses. He intended these to be just the beginning, roadmaps that guide research, generating subhypotheses and then sub-subhypotheses. These can be confirmed in a cautious, controlled, and conservative manner.

\_\_\_

If judged by Occam’s Razor, no theory to date rivals the explanatory sharpness of Jaynes’s hypotheses, which explain so much by means of so few principles.

\_\_\_

The first hypothesis is that before people were socialized to use Jaynesian consciousness, a preconscious, bicameral mentality governed their behavior. This mentality generated audiovisual directives that were attributed to supernatural beings. Usually, attempts to explain Jaynesian psychology begin with “consciousness.” However, given how this word’s polysemantic usage hinders people from understanding what Jaynes meant by the term, it may be more fruitful to highlight his understanding of “hallucinations”; at least people know what the term means. Moreover, it seems easier to at least entertain the contention that the innumerable accounts of godly visitations characterizing the ancient world were in fact audiovisual hallucinations. The second hypothesis concerns the timing of Jaynesian consciousness. Though the transition occurred at different periods in various parts of the world, in most places, subjective introspectable self-awareness emerged around the end of the second millennium BCE. The third hypothesis is about how this new mentality was linguistically constructed on the basis of metaphors.

While the first hypothesis concerns the behavioral aspects of bicameral mentality, the final hypothesis concerns the neurology of the double brain. If Jaynes’s contentions are valid about an earlier mentality, its underlying neurological system must be accounted for. This concerns not basic changes in neuroanatomy, but rather culturally-configured neuroplasticity and the lateralization of language. Given the inherent complexity of the human brain, this last hypothesis is admittedly the most controversial and contested.

**Small hinges swing big doors**

Counterintuitive ideas become believable when science moves forward from hypothetical claims to in-the-trenches experimentation, empirical data collection, and statistical support. In the case of Jaynes’s hypotheses, this means scholarly engagement with ancient texts, archaeological findings, linguistic analyses, in-depth anthropological studies, as well as neuroscientific experiments. Like all viable theories, the devil is in the details, and Jaynes utilized analytical tools that are useful in their own right. The first is “authorization,” or the human predisposition to seek out and follow instructions and commands. It concerns being granted permission to act in a certain way or to believe something, or how our actions are legitimized or justified. Having evolved from primates, we instinctively establish dominance hierarchies, with a leader allowing—or authorizing—our behavior. History has followed a grand trajectory, moving from divine authorization (originating in gods and ancestors) to what might be called a more secular self-authorization. Being social beings, we prefer authorization in the form of persons. However, in principle, [anything, including elaborate ideologies (any political “ism”), can provide authorization](https://iai.tv/articles/truth-is-dead-and-thats-a-good-thing-auid-3354). As a species, we still seek some form of ultimate authorization, whether in religion, science, or politics.

“[Aptic structures](https://www.julianjaynes.org/2021/01/26/appreciating-other-facets-of-jaynesian-psychology-part-2/)” are the evolved, innate neurological basis of aptitudes that guide responses in a general way when wedded to environmental, developmental experiences. Such structures make the organism apt to behave in a certain way under specific conditions.

The “[general bicameral paradigm](https://loc.closertotruth.com/theory/jaynes-s-breakdown-of-the-bicameral-mind)” is the psychosocial structure behind a class of holdovers from an earlier mentality. These vestigial behaviors, e.g., hypnosis, glossolalia, mediumship, and spirit possession, have been culturally repurposed. This paradigm has four aspects: (1) collective cognitive imperative (socially agreed-on expectancies that determine forms of behavior and the roles to be acted out within that form; more than just a belief system, it highlights the socializing impact of cultural prescriptions); (2) induction (procedures that temporarily suspend Jaynesian consciousness); (3) trance (diminished narratizing “I” and eroded imaginary mental space), and (4) archaic authorization (to which the trance is directed or related, such as a god or ancestor, but sometimes a personage who is accepted by the entranced and the community as an authority over the individual, and who by the collective cognitive imperative is believed to be responsible for controlling the trance state).

Jaynesian consciousness is given life by words that over the centuries sedimented into psychological vocabularies. These “[preconscious hypostases](https://www.julianjaynes.org/book/ooc/en/the-intellectual-consciousness-of-greece/)” are quasi‑agentive “seats of reaction and responsibility” that operated in the transitional period between bicameral and conscious mentality. They were neither gods nor voices, but physiological sensations that ancient peoples interpreted as causing their actions as godly commands decreased in frequency. Terms for mental events accumulated from the “outside in”: (1) objective (external things borrowed from the environment); (2) internal (sensations); (3) subjective (incipient psychological events); and (4) synthetic (full-blown conscious mentality). Analyzing the etymology of these linguistic layers would count as a subhypothesis of Jaynes’s third hypothesis—that conscious mentality was linguistically constructed from metaphor.

Jaynes’s “paralogic cognition” describes suspending an external standard of truth (rules of logic) and overlooking contradictions, especially when [assertions about reality are verbalized by powerful authority figures, such as deities](https://iai.tv/video/new-gods-and-lost-rituals). This type of cognition is seen during hypnosis and hypnoidal states.

Jaynes saw “[structions](https://www.julianjaynes.org/2021/01/26/appreciating-other-facets-of-jaynesian-psychology-part-2/)” as highlighting how most thinking is conscious-less. We give ourselves an “instruction,” triggering an automatic train of thinking; such cognition then utilizes materials—i.e., “construction”—to find a solution or complete a behavioral sequence. Jaynes distinguished “set,” “determining tendency,” and “struction.” A set is inclusive, being an engaged aptic structure which among mammals can be ordered from a general limbic component of readiness to a specific cortical component of a determining tendency, while for cognitively more complex humans, the final product is a struction.

**Countering the criticisms**

A common criticism of Jaynes is that while his ideas are certainly intriguing, they cannot be rigorously evaluated through standard scientific methods and are hard to falsify. However, the scientific validation of many entities that are not directly observable has been inferred through the convergence of indirect evidence, predictive success, and mathematical consistency: black holes (the motions of nearby stars; X-ray emissions from accreting matter; gravitational effects impossible to explain otherwise), neutrinos (regarded as real because they elegantly solved conservation-law problems; direct detection came only in 1956); atoms (chemical stoichiometry; gas laws; Brownian motion; thermodynamics), the Earth’s core (seismic waves; density calculations; magnetic-field behavior). Obviously, we cannot conduct experiments using fMRI on individuals from ancient times, and the amount and quality of evidence required to support Jaynes varies by place and period. However, overall, we have enough evidence to extrapolate, discern patterns, and fit together the pieces of the puzzle. If judged by Occam’s Razor, no theory to date rivals the explanatory sharpness of Jaynes’s hypotheses, which explain so much by means of so few principles.

Some contend that formulating testable hypotheses (the bedrock of scientific inquiry) that demonstrate what people experienced psychologically is impossible. Of course, this is nonsense, since entire academic endeavors—history, ethnography, anthropology, sociology, clinical psychology, etc.—rest upon providing an account of what others thought or think.

Another criticism is that no one has followed up on Jaynes’s ideas. This is not true. In addition to numerous articles, about a dozen book-length treatments have been directly inspired by Jaynes. His ideas have been tested in ancient Israel, Egypt, Japan, China, Tibet, and Mesoamerica. The therapeutic applications of Jaynesian psychology have also been published.

**The plausibility of Jaynesian psychology**

The problem with the Jaynesian agenda is its sheer ambition. It is intellectually demanding, requiring a familiarity, at least to some degree, with archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, history, psychology, religion, and neuroscience. Many researchers advance their fields (and careers) by hyper-specialization, so being conversant in multiple disciplines that seem incommensurable (psychology and history) is unrealistic. In any case, one does not have to be an expert in so many fields, but one must be intellectually adventurous enough to be able to connect a large number of seemingly distant dots.

Another challenge is the current terminology employed to analyze human psychology; it is simply inadequate. It is time to admit that the very language we utilize to explore the psyche is outdated. Above, I have already commented on the unworkability of “consciousness” when undefined. Another example is “[hallucinations](iai-academy/courses/info?course=hallucinations).” This word has negative denotations, so another expression is required to convey its Jaynesian sense as an originally adaptive form of mentation.

We accept as fact the magical-seeming transformation of species (evolution), the warping of spatiotemporal fabric (astrophysics), and the superposition of particles and “spooky action at a distance” (quantum mechanics). So surely, one would think, acknowledging the cultural roots of subjective introspectable self-awareness, as well as how supernatural visitations and visions (hallucinations) once governed societies, seems rather tame. Compared to other well-established scientific ideas, I submit, Jaynes’s arguments do not seem implausible.
