# Time to abandon the falling in love fairytale

*Why you should stop falling in love*

**By Sam Shpall** | *5th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,034 words*

![Time to abandon the falling in love fairytale](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/time-to-give-up-on-the-falling-in-love-fairytale2.webp)

*Falling in love is dangerous. Philosopher **Sam Shpall**, Associate Professor of Philosophy at the University of Sydney and author of the upcoming book* Limerence and the Future of Desire*, makes the radical case that the notion of "falling in love" isn't just an imprecise idea, but a hazardous one. Buried inside it is a forgotten concept called 'limerence': an involuntary, obsessive craving for reciprocation, distinct from romantic love. Conflating the two convinces us our relationships are lesser than they are. Instead, we should retire the idea of "falling in and out of love".*

In 1979, the American psychologist Dorothy Tennov published a book called *Love and Limerence: The Experience of Being in Love*. Simone de Beauvoir called it a book that “deals with the subject in an entirely new way.” Notwithstanding this extraordinary endorsement, and a recent resurrection of Tennov’s ideas by content creators and journalists, philosophers have mostly ignored the topic of limerence. This is unfortunate, as the concept can do real philosophical work.

Tennov was interested in understanding a distinctive romantic experience. She thought it was essential to coin a term for this experience. “Limerence” is an intense, largely involuntary craving for a particular person’s romantic reciprocation. It is not mere desire, or a crush, but a more complex and [existentially altering state](https://iai.tv/video/love-and-other-drugs) in which your mood, attention, and sense of what matters become hostage to how that person feels about you. It involves intrusive thoughts about the person that you cannot suppress, a tendency to see them through rose-tinted glasses, and volatile swings between powerful emotions like euphoria and dread. Limerence has a natural lifespan too: on average about two years, Tennov thought, and rarely less than six months in full bloom. After this period, limerence fades—whether you want it to or not.

\_\_\_

Confusing limerence with love is among the most consequential (and common) mistakes that human beings make.

\_\_\_

 Most people would call this being in love. The title of Tennov’s book can suggest she thought this as well. But she did not. In fact, she thought that confusing limerence with love is among the most consequential (and common) mistakes that human beings make.

Love and Limerence arose from extensive qualitative psychological research, that is, from interviews Tennov conducted with hundreds of people about their romantic histories. In the early stages of this work, Tennov made a discovery about her friend Helen Payne that proved decisive. Payne was the first romantically experienced person Tennov encountered who had never undergone the limerent process I’ve just described. Tennov’s first instinct was to find this strange and puzzling. If she hadn’t lacked romantic opportunities, was her friend in some way romantically disordered? Soon Tennov discarded that hypothesis. Not having experienced limerence is, she came to think, neither more nor less pathological than having experienced it. Moreover, not having experienced limerence is compatible with having experienced romantic love. In fact, limerence is neither necessary for love nor sufficient for it.

Doris Lessing offers a memorable expression of the instinct Tennov overcame in her novel *The Summer Before the Dark*. Lessing describes a woman named Mary Finchley who “had never been in love in her life” and couldn’t understand what people meant when they talked about it. Another character says that “she really believes that the way everyone goes on about love, being in love, is some sort of conspiracy, the emperor has no clothes.” The character’s verdict: Finchley is a “savage woman.” A delusional pessimist, about love no less!

\_\_\_

If half of us, or some large proportion, have never had this experience, why does our shared romantic vocabulary assume it as an ideal, perhaps the ideal, in the romantic sphere?

\_\_\_

But if Mary Finchley was thinking about limerence, then the data suggest she is not a rare case at all. The best current estimate, from a large self-test and a separate randomized survey [reported by the neuroscientist Tom Bellamy](https://livingwithlimerence.com/how-common-is-limerence-the-numbers/), puts the proportion of adults who have never experienced limerence at somewhere close to half, with no meaningful difference between men and women. It is difficult to support the accusation that half the adult population is pathological. It is also a curious irony that among the few researchers and journalists currently writing about limerence, a large proportion tend towards pathologizing characterizations. In other words, whether or not you’ve experienced limerence, there are plenty of people who think you are diseased on account of it.

Here is one question raised by what I’ve said so far. If half of us, or some large proportion, have never had this experience, why does our shared romantic vocabulary assume it as an ideal, perhaps the ideal, in the romantic sphere?

Consider the sentence: “I love you, but I’m not in love with you.” Everyone recognizes this sentence, and you may well have used it (or had it used on you) in romantic relationship contexts. Yet almost no one can explain what it means in a satisfying way. Isn’t being in love just experiencing the romantic form of love? Isn’t falling in love just coming to experience the romantic form of love, and falling out of love ceasing to have that experience? Then the fact that I’m in love with you would follow from the fact that I love you romantically. And from the fact that I have fallen out of love with you, it would follow that I do not love you. Our sentence is puzzling.

Here is my diagnosis. The concept of “being in love” is doing two jobs at once, and we routinely fail to notice which job it’s doing.

On one reading, being in love just means being a romantic lover. On this reading, the sentence above is close to nonsense, since it is impossible to love someone romantically without being a romantic lover. Uttering the sentence looks like asserting a blatant contradiction. Since it often appears to be used more meaningfully, there must be another interpretation available.

On a second reading, being in love means being limerent. On this second reading, the sentence makes perfect sense. It means this: I love you romantically, but I am not limerent for you. Often it means: I love you romantically, but my limerence for you has dissipated (“I love you, but I’m not in love with you anymore”). That happens to almost all limerence eventually. It is not, contrary to popular belief, evidence that anything has gone wrong.

Let me give you a poetic test case. Here’s a passage from Pablo Neruda’s incredible “Love Sonnet XI”:

*I crave your mouth, your voice, your hair.*

*Silent and starving, I prowl through the streets.*

*Bread does not nourish me, dawn disrupts me, all day*

*I hunt for the liquid measure of your steps.*

Ask a room full of people what this poem is about, and they will likely say: the subject of the poem is being in love. Fair enough. Then ask the long-married people in the room whether it describes the way they feel about their romantic partnership, and they will say: obviously not. What do these reactions tell us? I think they suggest that the poem is describing limerence: the visceral craving, the obsessive prowling, the monomaniacal focus and dependency on one human object of desire. The poem is not describing romantic love as such. The root of the confusion is that we have one phrase for both and use it interchangeably without noticing.

Is this just a linguistic curiosity? No. Here are four reasons to think that overlooking the love/limerence distinction is prudentially and morally dangerous.

First, people who have never experienced limerence, something like half the adult population, are routinely told, or come to suspect on their own, that their romantic lives are fundamentally impoverished, indeed that they have never really experienced romantic love. After all, being in love is a necessary condition for being a genuine romantic lover. This is a bad inference as well as an inference that’s bad for us. Limerence is not a necessary condition for romantic love; we infer that it is because of flawed social narratives and ideologies. And the view causes real harm to non-limerents. It licenses the marginalization of their kind of romantic love, making many people unnecessarily anxious about the value of their relationships and impoverishing collective hermeneutical resources that are needed for improving our conceptions of relationship possibilities.

\_\_\_

Limerence is oriented toward securing someone’s romantic reciprocation, not toward that person’s flourishing.

\_\_\_

Second, conventional wisdom overstates the epistemic connection between limerence and love, for example by assuming that “falling in love” is simply the first stage of the romantic love process. Because the connection between limerence and romantic love is contingent and causal rather than necessary and constitutive, conventional wisdom makes many people irrationally optimistic about their romantic love prospects when they are limerent.

Third, partners who have experienced limerence, and especially mutual limerence, often panic when it fades. Sadness may be appropriate (that’s a larger conversation), but panic is not. Limerence reliably fades, usually within a few years, and this waning is an arational phenomenon. It happens on its own schedule and will happen independently of anything partners do or don’t do. Its disappearance is not a verdict on relationship quality, chemistry, longevity, or anything else. This is one way that limerence is very unlike the romantic love with which it is so often confused.

Finally, limerence is oriented toward securing someone’s romantic reciprocation, not toward that person’s flourishing. Unlike genuine love, it need not be in tension with possessiveness, coercion, and worse. In response to a moral violation like coercive control, the familiar claim that “He only did it because he loves her” already makes you suspicious. We’ve now provided a conceptual tool for vindicating that suspicion. The assertion (often) depends on mistaking limerence for love—and gains some apparent plausibility thanks to the mistake. Limerence is a causal explanation of much romantic misbehavior; “He only did it because he is limerent for her” is often true. Whereas love does not cause selfish, possessive cruelty. More carefully: coercive control is a perversion of love’s character, since love is concern for another for her own sake. But it need not be a perversion of limerence’s character, since limerence is concern for another for the sake of your own desire.

\_\_\_

My proposal, however, is that we retire a core piece of our romantic vocabulary. Being in love, falling in love, falling out of love: these phrases should go. They produce widespread, practically significant confusion. And they are superfluous.

\_\_\_

I develop these and related arguments at length in the book this essay draws from. Here I can only state them briefly and emphasize that I am not in the business of passing an overarching judgment, positive or negative, on the value of limerence.

My proposal, however, is that we retire a core piece of our romantic vocabulary. Being in love, falling in love, falling out of love: these phrases should go. They produce widespread, practically significant confusion. And they are superfluous. The concepts of limerence and romantic love, when suitably distinguished, suffice. Limerence is the short-lived, involuntary, idealizing craving. (This is of course a gloss; I describe the experience in much more detail elsewhere.) [Romantic love is the more durable caring relation](https://iai.tv/video/happily-ever-after) that, unlike limerence, tends to deepen with time—and whose waning we rightly expect to be explicable. Once we have these concepts, there is nothing left over for the in-love concepts to do.

This is an instance of what philosophers call conceptual engineering. The claim is not that we should revise our romantic concepts because they are imprecise. We should revise them because their imprecision is costly. The traditional concepts are bad for nonlimerents who doubt the value of their romantic relationships, and for limerent partners who mistake an unstoppable tide for a negative verdict on their relationship. They are bad for anyone trying to think clearly about [desire and love and relationship possibilities](https://iai.tv/video/love-is-more-than-magic). A fresh conceptual scheme stands to improve both our theorizing and our practical reasoning about some of life’s most important choices.

But you don’t have to take my word for it. Try it out. The next time you or a friend reaches for an in-love concept, take a philosophical beat. Ask yourself whether love or limerence is being described. And meditate on the different consequences of each designation. You may find, as I have, that the new vocabulary prompts some intriguing conversations.
