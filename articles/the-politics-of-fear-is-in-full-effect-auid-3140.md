# The politics of fear is in full effect

*Freud, Jung, and fear as a political tool*

**By Nicolai Gellwitzki** | *15th April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,611 words*

![The politics of fear is in full effect](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-politics-of-fear-is-in-full-effect.webp)

---

*It seems like our politics is designed to make us fearful at every turn. In an age of polycrisis with threats around every corner, some politicians are more than willing to direct this state of fear to their advantage. But far from being just an emotion, fear should be thought of as a liquid, which is channelled and dammed by politicians to invent fears where previously there weren't any and dam them before they threaten the powerful. Nicolai Gellwitzki and Charlie Price argue that fear is so dominant because we lack a clear positive political vision, to move forward we must utilise and transform our fear in the pursuit of a better future.*

Fear and anxiety dominate our time. From climate anxiety to the more general mental health crisis that is ever-present in the news. These feelings are either discussed sympathetically as a medical problem or dismissed as just that, feelings. But imagining fear and anxiety as just mere emotions hides a web of political manipulation that sits at the core of many of the political issues we see today. Why is the modern populist right so concerned with immigration, DEI, and progressive social issues? Why does the technocratic left in the Global North complain of a ‘vibecession’ and loudly pontificate about how regular people don’t understand their own circumstances despite feeling that something is wrong? The key problem is a misunderstanding of fear and anxiety, not as just individual emotions, but as a liquid that can be channelled and directed by politics to create problems where they don’t exist and distract us from evidence and, ultimately, the truth. Until we understand the centrality of liquid fear, and provide a tangible counter to it, politics will become further dominated by anxiety.

One may may feel anxious that weather conditions may become dangerous, when sailing across the Atlantic, but it is not unless the storm hits that one is afraid.

\_\_\_

How does fear work on a political scale? There are two key ideas at play here that we [explored in a recent article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03058298241265264). Temporally, we fear immediate threats approaching us in the present, while we feel anxiety about the unknowns of the future. Cognitively, fear is tied to an identifiable object that we can react to, whereas anxiety is a more generalised, object-less feeling, the exact cause of which we struggle to pinpoint. Think of the frustration when you’re feeling anxious but have no idea why!

We are afraid of everything from nuclear war to election outcomes or whether our water is safe to drink; and we are anxious about the future of our world and questions concerning the virtue and meaning of our lives. Whilst we may feel both, we rarely feel anxiety and fear together as one often gives way to the other. One may may feel anxious that weather conditions may become dangerous, when sailing across the Atlantic, but it is not unless the storm hits that one is afraid. One could ask why this matters: it is obvious that a more immediate threat produces a definitive sense of fear rather than a more generalised sensation of anxiety. However, these embodied feelings go beyond the individual; fears and anxieties can be mobilised and called into being by political actors to suit their ends. Governments and media prey on our anxieties and channel them into concrete fears in order to justify potentially controversial or repressive policies. How, then is anxiety and fear motivated at a collective level, and how does one become the other?

Psychoanalytic theorists from Sigmund Freud to Carl Jung to Jacque Lacan have used the conceptual metaphor of “liquid” to describe affective experiences in general. Indeed, references to channels, flows, and dams are scattered throughout their work and point towards the volatile nature of the human embodied experience. Psychoanalytic work has highlighted that humans are not only highly emotional beings, but that affects are often rechannelled from their original source to render them more actionable or socially acceptable. For example, incidences of [domestic violence increase when a national football team loses](https://www.simononthestreets.co.uk/news/domestic-violence-and-football-the-campaigns-fighting-for-change). There is not a logical cognitive connection between the footballers and the family member; the emotion is simply channelled to an unrelated end. Similarly, existential philosophers have suggested that the generalised state of anxiety is often transposed into fear which is tied to specific objects or situations. As Paul Tillich argued, “anxiety strives to become fear because fear can be met with courage.” Put differently, by channelling diffuse anxiety into tangible fears humans can engage and cope with otherwise overwhelming dread.

\_\_\_

Political actors can, and often do, capitalise on the fact that there are generalised anxieties that can be channelled into specific fears for political gain and mobilisation.

\_\_\_

Sociologist Zygmunt Bauman, in turn, coined the term “liquid fear” to describe the movement of fear. Fears are often decoupled from their original source, they spill over from one of their original sources to another that may be cognitively entirely unrelated. Drawing on these diverse intellectual traditions, we may say that anxieties and fears are, in fact, inherently connected affective states that are fluid – our embodied experience oscillates between anxiety and fear, a perpetual ebb and flow. Political actors can, and often do, capitalise on the fact that there are generalised anxieties that can be channelled into specific fears for political gain and mobilisation.

But why anxiety and fear? At the heart of this lies a paradox. For us to *feel* secure as a society, we prefer to be afraid of threats that can be contained by political means. In other words, collective anxieties can be alleviated by channelling them into collective fears, collective fears that can be addressed and managed. For example, a general anxiety about the state of the world can be channelled by politicians into a fear of outsiders or a fear of an elite. Thus, to understand the role of fear in politics, it is essential to recognise that fear is liquid. Political entrepreneurs from governments to corporations feed us fears of threats, whether real or imagined, along with ready-made “solutions” to these threats. Fear, in other words, can be instrumentalised, even weaponised, for political mobilisation and transformed into policy. Fear manifests politically as ‘security’. Security is fundamentally about the implementation of emergency powers and legislation to combat an existential threat to society or the state. For this to be effective, populations must be afraid of the threat. Once they are, they become willing to accept exceptional measures, such as new rules, regulations, and policies, often at the expense of their own liberties, or more commonly, the liberties of others. Yet the role of fear in politics is not necessarily undemocratic or inherently problematic; rather, it may lie beyond such simplistic normative judgments.

Fear can be instrumental in addressing what might be considered reasonable security concerns. Take, for example, Covid-19, where governments around the world harnessed fear to ensure compliance with lockdown measures and social distancing. This seemed prudent at the time, and once fear ebbed away, people began breaching those regulations intended to protect public health. Thus, political actors capitalised on fear to implement and enforce security measures that may have saved countless lives. However, Covid-19 also serves as a vivid example of the potential problems associated with fear in politics. In the UK, for instance, the general fear-based messaging by politicians and the media may have inadvertently led to affective spillovers. Not only was the virus understood as a threat, but segments of the population also came to perceive individuals of East Asian ancestry as a threat, or more specifically, as a “source” of the virus. Consequently, violent hate crimes across the UK skyrocketed compared to pre-pandemic levels. Once you open the Pandora’s Box of fear, it's very difficult to put it back in.

Moreover, fear’s liquidity is open to abuse by political entrepreneurs. The case of Covid-19 is illuminating in understanding how liquid fear is used in emergency politics, ostensibly for the necessary protection of human life. Spillover may happen unintentionally, as was potentially the case with Covid and people of East Asian ancestry in the UK, but more nefarious consequences can also be the ambitions of the political entrepreneurs. One only needs to look at how the increase in migration across the Mediterranean in 2015 was framed to see this. Migrants were referred to as coming in “waves” or “hordes,” seemingly unstoppable inhuman or violent entities, that constituted a “crisis.” Certain European states initially rejected this narrative, such as Germany, but later fully adopted it and used it to stir fear and animosity amongst citizen populations in order to justify restrictive and violent practices against certain peoples considered to be “other”.

\_\_\_

To carry forward the liquid metaphor, fear can be channelled towards minority groups as well as dammed to prevent it from spilling onto groups with privilege, especially those who can shape public conversations.

\_\_\_

Framings of migration produced anxiety and this was channelled into concrete objects of fear, migrants and those assumed to be of migrant background. Therefore, whilst fear may be necessary in facilitating emergency measures for the better (Covid-19 lockdowns may be an example, albeit with many caveats), its liquidity allows it to be used for far more nefarious purposes by political opportunists. This is still apparent in contemporary far-right politics, one only needs to look to racist riots and “stopping the boats” in the UK, or border walls in the USA to see this. These are more than just scapegoats, they are rendered objects of fear as relief from contemporary neoliberal anxieties.

What does that mean for our contemporary political moment? For one, it is important to remember that fear is liquid and to recognise when the politics of (liquid) fear are at play. When politicians and the media start framing entire groups as supposed threats to society, we can witness the channelling of anxiety into fear. Images attempting to portray faceless migrants in boats crossing the Channel. Headlines suggesting that “Muslims” are endangering British culture. General group identity markers are attached to discussions of broad threats – Black, gay, trans, Muslim, and so on. Debates like these serve to create and reinforce fears of historically minoritised others. Fear flows more easily through established channels. To carry forward the liquid metaphor, fear can be channelled towards minority groups as well as dammed to prevent it from spilling onto groups with privilege, especially those who can shape public conversations. It's convenient that the ills that come of hegemonic economic structures are [blamed upon migrants](http://iai.tv/articles/do-refugees-have-obligations-auid-3008), LGBT and poor people, rather than those who actually benefit from and are responsible for the status quo.

Liquid fear also has ramifications for progressive politics. Specifically, generalised anxieties need to be addressed. In our day and age, this often takes the form of leftist, liberal, and conservative parties adopting the talking points of the far right - ostensibly due to vague public moods, the will of the people, or the need to tackle societal fears. [If you’re afraid of something, being told not to be afraid doesn’t work as a political strategy](http://iai.tv/articles/the-metaphysics-behind-putins-war-on-liberalism-auid-3120), and results in people supporting parties that take their fears seriously, regardless of the truth of the matter.

Simply put, in this logic, “the people” are afraid of existential threats that more often than not happen to be minorities or minoritised group. Yet, despite attempts to address public fears, they never seem to be alleviated, and our politics continue to shift further to the right. Why is that? Because fear is liquid, and fearing something does not necessarily mean that this something is the cause of the fear. We may endlessly attend to the object of fear without ever addressing the underlying anxieties that gave birth to it.  To counter the politics of liquid fear, we need more than technocratic solutions or attempts to reason away legitimate anxieties – we need a politics that both acknowledges our collective anxieties and channels them towards a constructive vision of change. But how do we get such a vision?

There are at least two ways in which anxiety may be harnessed in a manner that benefits society. The first is to channel anxieties into fears that are at least related to these collective anxieties, rather than scapegoating minorities. For example, austerity, the degradation of the welfare state, and the dismantling of the NHS all contribute to the anxiety-inducing state of the world. Rolling back some of neoliberalism’s worst excesses may actually address some of the root causes of collective anxieties rather than merely their symptoms. Using fear in this way, of course, still lends itself to populist demands and does not necessarily result in prudent or constructive policies. Yet it may offer an alternative future and politics compared to the status quo, as well as a narrative that appeals to the broader population. In recent years, we have seen politicians channelling anxieties into fears of corporate greed and the ills of late capitalism, advocating for systemic change and economic justice. Figures such as Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez in the US, as well as Jeremy Corbyn in the UK, have mobilised public anxieties about wealth inequality, workers’ rights, and the erosion of public services, offering alternative visions of a more equitable society.

The second possibility rests upon another insight from existential philosophy and psychoanalytic theory. As it turns out, anxiety does not *exclusively* channel into fear but also into other negative emotions, albeit somewhat less frequently. Politicians, media, and activists, therefore, can leverage anxieties for purposes beyond fearmongering. Most relevant in this context is the emotion of shame, which can arise when there is a disconnect between our desired self-image, whether as individuals or as a collective, and our actual behaviour. This conflict between who we want to be and what we do is a powerful motivator for change.

\_\_\_

It is introspective, demands self-reflection and the willingness to confront uncomfortable truths.

\_\_\_

Nobody likes to feel ashamed, and people naturally seek to resolve shame either by changing the stories they tell about themselves or by changing their behaviour. Collective anxieties, in other words, can be translated into shame and shaming, as demonstrated by footballer Marcus Rashford, who successfully shamed the government into providing free school lunches for children during the Covid-19 pandemic. Shame, however, is difficult and painful to endure. It is introspective, demands self-reflection and the willingness to confront uncomfortable truths. While fear externalises responsibility and shifts blame onto others who are supposedly responsible for our plight, shame forces individuals and societies to reckon with their own actions, values, and complicity in the status quo. Where fear can be politically expedient by rallying people against a common enemy or threat, shame can be politically unappealing as it demands deeper self-reflection and the courage to make difficult decisions that lead to meaningful change.

Nonetheless, narratives of shame can be just as simple and compelling as narratives of fear. Who are we as a country and a society? Who do we want to be? A place where children go to bed hungry, people die on the streets because there are no ambulances, elderly people freeze in the winter because they cannot afford to heat their homes, minoritised communities are scapegoated for every ill of society, and the arts and humanities are increasingly marginalised - all in the name of austerity, whose validity and efficacy have been questioned by economists for years? Or a community that values compassion and solidarity, where no child goes to bed hungry, where social programmes, healthcare and emergency services are properly funded, where the elderly can heat their homes without fear of financial ruin, where diversity is celebrated, and where the arts and humanities are recognised as vital to our collective well-being and identity? Are we afraid of others or ashamed of our collective shortcomings? The choice is ours.
