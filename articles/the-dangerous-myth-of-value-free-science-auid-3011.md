# The dangerous myth of value-free science

*Scientists should be more open about their biases*

**By Kevin C. Elliott** | *28th November 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,044 words*

![The dangerous myth of value-free science](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/bias-vs-truth-paart1.webp)

---

*Scientists working for the World Health Organization recently found no evidence for links between cellphone radiation and brain cancer. But other scientists argue that there is good evidence linking cellphone use with increased tumor risk. Disagreement runs deep throughout science, so how can we trust its results? Some claim that to be trustworthy, science should strive to be unpolluted by ethical and political values. This is a mistake, argues Kevin C. Elliott. Aiming for the ideal of value-free science makes scientists less, not more trustworthy. It sweeps under the carpet the values that are unavoidably part of interpreting evidence and choosing between different scientific models. Instead, these values should be brought into the open, so that they can be subjected to much-needed scrutiny.*

*This is Part 1 of a 2-part series. Part 2 is available [here](https://iai.tv/articles/bias-in-science-can-and-must-be-exposed-auid-3012).*

**How to make science worthy of trust**

At the end of August 2024, an international team commissioned by the World Health Organization (WHO) [published a systematic review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412024005695) that found no compelling links between cellphone radiation and brain cancer. This report was covered widely by international media, with headlines like “[Scientists Make Definitive Call on Whether Mobile Phones Cause Brain Cancer](https://www.smh.com.au/technology/no-your-mobile-phone-is-not-giving-you-brain-cancer-20240904-p5k7pg.html)” and “[Truth about Cellphones and Brain Cancer Link, According to Major World Health Organization Review](https://www.dailymail.co.uk/health/article-13809469/cellphones-brain-cancer-radiation-World-Health-Organization-review.html).” However, other scientists have arrived at [different conclusions](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/21/8079).

These sorts of disagreements among scientists are common, and they raise the question—is science worthy of trust? What makes it trustworthy? Some would say that we should try to make it more objective, and thus worthy of trust, by striving to keep values (particularly ethical, social, and political values) out of science. But this won’t work: values can’t be kept out of science, and striving for this impossible ideal is counter-productive.

Instead, scientists should pursue a multi-pronged strategy to generate trustworthy yet value-laden science: being open about their reasoning, developing standard ways to handle value-laden decisions, engaging with those who will be using their research, and setting reasonable expectations for what science can actually accomplish.

**The fantasy of value-free science**

Value-free science is a fantasy because scientific practice requires a host of choices that cannot be settled solely by available evidence. Consider the WHO study. At the outset, researchers have to frame the project. Should the research focus solely on brain cancer, or should it include other cancers? Should it include other potential health effects, such as impacts on reproductive health, in addition to cancer? And should the focus be solely on cellphone radiation, or should it consider other sources of radiofrequency (RF) exposure, such as wifi routers or cellular transmission towers? Scientists might make these sorts of decisions based on a wide variety of practical constraints, but these decisions can have profound impacts on society and thus ought to be scrutinized as value-laden choices.

Once the problem is framed, researchers have to decide what methodologies to employ and how to interpret ambiguous data. For example, the WHO study included a systematic review of the results from a number of previous studies. When doing such reviews, researchers have to decide what sorts of studies to include. Should they include only human epidemiological studies, or should they also include animal studies and/or in vitro studies? In addition, they have to develop criteria for including or excluding particular studies. What kinds of study designs are so weak or misleading that they should not be included? Again, these choices can have major impacts on society, and reasonable scientists can disagree about how to handle them.

Once researchers analyze the data, they face still more decisions. How much evidence should they demand in order to conclude that cellphone radiation might be having a harmful effect? If there does not appear to be an effect, how much confidence should the researchers express in their finding of no effect?

Then, when communicating their results, the researchers have to decide whether to provide caveats about the potential limitations of the study, and if so, which limitations to emphasize. Should they emphasize that their findings apply only to brain cancer and not to other potential health effects? [Which of their findings and limitations should they put in the abstract or executive summary of their report](http://iuhpst.org/media/pdf/Elabbar_IUHPST_Prize_2023.pdf), and which should they “bury” in the fine print?

[As philosopher Zina Ward has emphasized](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0039368120301783), these choices have the potential to be value-laden in a variety of different ways. First, researchers might be *motivated* by their values to approach the choices in particular ways. For example, a researcher who greatly valued the societal benefits of cellphone technology and wanted to minimize public concerns about it might provide extensive caveats before communicating any findings about potential health risks.

Second, things that we value could be *affected* by these choices. For example, most of the choices discussed above have the potential to incline the study in one direction or another—either for or against the conclusion that cellphones are safe. On one hand, if the results err on the side of declaring cellphones to be safe, it promotes the values of economic growth for the cellphone industry and convenience for consumers. This might happen if, say, researchers set aside [animal studies that are suggestive of potential health risks](https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/whatwestudy/topics/cellphones) because they do not think the studies are sufficiently reliable. On the other hand, if the results err on the side of declaring cellphones to be unsafe, this promotes the values of consumer safety and public health.

\_\_\_

Third, given the potential for these research choices to affect society, ethical or social values might provide *good reasons* for approaching the choices in particular ways. For example, the value of promoting public health might provide a good reason to include not only epidemiological studies but also animal and in vitro studies (even if those studies might not be as relevant to real-life human scenarios), so as to avoid setting aside information that could potentially be useful. As the philosopher Heather Douglas argues, scientists have [the same sorts of ethical responsibilities as everyone else in society](https://cspo.org/news/science-values-and-democracy-rightful-place-science-douglas/), so they ought to consider the foreseeable social impacts of their choices rather than, say, just tossing a coin to decide how to make these decisions.

Ward’s analysis is helpful because it clarifies that even if the scientists involved in this research tried as hard as they could not to be *motivated* by values, their choices would still be value-laden in at least one of the two other ways.

**The ideal of value-free science makes science less trustworthy**

In my view, then, it actually makes policy-relevant science less trustworthy to try to make it value-free. To do so merely shoves all these value-laden choices under the rug rather than bringing them into the open so they can be dealt with responsibly.

Professor Jacob Stegenga disagrees with my assessment. In his response to this article, he argues that proponents of the value-free ideal for science want to expose value-laden choices so that values can be eliminated from science. As an example, he points to scientists’ efforts to hedge their conclusions and acknowledge uncertainties so they are less likely to be influenced by values. I agree that [it is often advisable for scientists to hedge their claims](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/to-hedge-or-not-to-hedge-scientific-claims-and-public-justification/B58C1B12B2DB74225DBCCFAE2CCBB0F0) to make them less idiosyncratic, but I do not think this completely eliminates values from their work. In fact, deciding how best to hedge claims or acknowledge uncertainty is itself typically a value-laden decision.

Professor Stegenga contends that claims like “Diamonds are made of carbon” are value-free, but I am dubious. We can agree that such claims are very likely true while acknowledging that there is still a slight chance that we are mistaken, and thus there are values involved in deciding to accept them. And while these influences of values might seem minimal in some areas of science, they become highly significant in cutting-edge areas of science that influence public policy.

**How to make science trustworthy**

So if we do not strive to make science value-free, what should we do instead to foster value-laden science that is worthy of trust? There are multiple promising strategies.

First, researchers can foster transparency about their choices, enabling robust discussion about how to handle them. This is an aim of the “[open science movement](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25116/open-science-by-design-realizing-a-vision-for-21st-century),” which strives to make data, methods, and many other aspects of scientific practice more open for scrutiny. This helps other experts to identify value-laden choices made by their colleagues, which in turn helps non-specialists to make better decisions about which scientific findings to rely on.

Researchers can also adopt standard ways of handling value-laden decisions. In the case of systematic reviews, for example, a number of guidelines have been developed, partly because this kind of research incorporates so many consequential choices that can dramatically influence the results. Importantly, these general standards are still value-laden, just like the individual decisions they are meant to guide. But the consistency engendered by these guidelines can enhance trustworthiness by ensuring that individual scientists are not making their own idiosyncratic choices without being clear about them.

In some cases, it may also make sense for researchers to align their choices with the values of those who will be making use of their research. In the case of the WHO report, for example, the overall framing of the study was probably guided by those who commissioned it. In other contexts, such as climate science, [scientists can tailor their modelling practices to the needs of specific communities](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/100/9/bams-d-17-0325.1.xml?tab_body=fulltext-display) that want to obtain particular sorts of information about future climate impacts. Nevertheless, this can easily create confusion and distrust when different audiences approach research with different values, which is why open communication about important research choices is so important.

In addition, the scientific community can foster a general culture that roots out problems and engenders public trust. For example, when research is funded by groups with major conflicts of interest in the outcome, [people understandably tend to be suspicious of the results](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0175643&type=printable). Therefore, when studying topics like cellphone radiation, where private companies have significant financial interests in research outcomes, it is important to encourage public-sector research funding and to clarify whether there are major discrepancies between the outcomes of publicly- and privately-funded research.

The scientific community can also foster venues for critically evaluating scientific findings. [This is a point emphasized by philosopher Helen Longino](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691020518/science-as-social-knowledge?srsltid=AfmBOooVH3rNqc258SDZhiYQV8NAMWudwZGkZ9bvD45buWlPZpUep90D), who has argued that scientific objectivity stems not primarily from the efforts of individual scientists to be “value free” but rather from the efforts of diverse groups of scientists to uncover and critically assess each other’s assumptions. Thus, the scientific community becomes more trustworthy when it incorporates scientists with diverse backgrounds and perspectives and provides opportunities for fruitful deliberation among them.

Admittedly, the public could become confused and distrustful when hearing diverse viewpoints and critical perspectives within the scientific community. Therefore, one of the most important steps for fostering trust between the scientific community and the general public is to promote more realistic expectations about what science can reasonably accomplish. Too often, science is regarded as a source of definitive conclusions. This is usually unrealistic.

As science policy expert Daniel Sarewitz [has](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1462901104000620?casa_token=rm9CYRjP8msAAAAA:89XQnChKyRMPh-VzhzkkzhnCIRGmN08HqR2_3cmOCL3EyDFbAvUB7DGesIpjU0-NPdOvLUyRW1Q) argued, scientists are unlikely to speak with a unified voice about complex issues—and this is precisely because scientific practice incorporates so many choices that are not completely settled by evidence. When dealing with complex issues like environmental pollution, pandemic preparedness, or the effects of climate change, scientists often have to evaluate complex bodies of evidence that draw on many different scientific sub-disciplines. It is no wonder that different scientists can reasonably arrive at different conclusions.

But Sarewitz has also emphasized that this is not a devastating problem as long as we are appropriately prepared for this challenge. Fundamentally, we need to be willing to make thoughtful decisions in the face of scientific information that is always incomplete and value-laden. In the case of cellphones and cancer, for example, the latest study from the WHO is encouraging, but given the range of viewpoints within the scientific community, people might still want to take [reasonable precautionary measures](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00139157.2024.2293631?src=exp-la#abstract) to lessen their exposure to radiofrequency radiation.

Thus, promoting trustworthy science should not be about chasing the illusory dream of value-free science; rather, it should be about managing the complexities of value-laden science as responsibly as possible.
