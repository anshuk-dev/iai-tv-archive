# Kierkegaard, Bob Dylan, and how to become yourself

*On masks, meaning, and the philosophy of the unfinished self*

**By Christopher Barnett** | *14th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,904 words*

*Søren Kierkegaard claimed that a self is not a static object, but something one must become, by struggling through distinct, often incompatible modes of existence. Author of* Bob Dylan and the Spheres of Existence*, **Christopher Barnett**, argues that this model of the self is the key to understanding Bob Dylan’s career. Critics often dismiss Dylan’s reinventions—from folk prophet to electric provocateur to born-again preacher—as masks concealing an elusive core, but Kierkegaard suggests the opposite. Rather than being an evasion of authentic identity, Dylan’s masks capture a self in the act of becoming.*

A recurring motif in the 2019 documentary film *Rolling Thunder Revue: A Bob Dylan Story* by Martin Scorsese is that Bob Dylan is a trickster. In [*The Encyclopedia of Fantasy*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Encyclopedia_of_Fantasy.html?id=eLKCQgAACAAJ), John Clute and John Grant describe tricksters as shapeshifting figures who, whether in animal or human form, expose the instability of ordinary life. They may not be “good” in a conventional sense, but even in their untrustworthiness, they are capable of providing insight into a world that is itself insecure.

In [*Rolling Thunder Revue*](https://variety.com/2019/film/columns/why-did-martin-scorsese-prank-his-audience-in-rolling-thunder-revue-1203243856/), Scorsese playfully appropriates Dylan’s reputation for manufacturing his own image. Famously, Dylan grew up in a middle-class Jewish family in Minnesota, moved to New York City, and fashioned himself as a hardscrabble folk singer. This may have been his first mask, but it was hardly his last. Over a decade later, on the Rolling Thunder Revue tour, Dylan performed onstage in white mime makeup, placing one mask over another. With this in mind, Scorsese incorporated several minutes of invented documentary footage into the film’s otherwise real-life narrative. Such a blurring of fact and fiction is, however, consistent with the trickster’s societal role. In one key scene, Dylan himself seems to validate this point: “We didn’t have enough masks on that tour,” he recounts, “we should’ve had masks for everybody. When somebody is wearing a mask, he’s gonna tell you the truth. When he’s not wearing a mask, it’s highly unlikely.”

Scorsese’s film, while clever, was by no means the first to advance this understanding of Dylan. Indeed, Dylan’s status as a Janus-faced trickster was developed in the 2007 film *I’m Not There*, in which different actors are used to represent different phases of Dylan’s career. According to director Todd Haynes, *I’m Not There* is an exploration of [a crisis of meaning](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-news/bob-dylan-times-six-an-interview-with-im-not-there-director-todd-haynes-67251/) that Dylan represents in his life and in his art. On this reading, Dylan’s adoption of sundry personae—an indigent, weary folk musician here, a sleek pop star there—constitutes a rebellion against the notion of individual identity, which is “a straitjacket for people, for societies, for cultures, for historical moments.” Thus Dylan’s songs and his efforts in cinema, literature, and the visual arts do not serve a grand design or a higher purpose. Instead, they bear witness to the artist’s “healthy erraticality.” For Haynes, Dylan’s reinventions involve the death of one identity and the emergence of another: “Each of those transitions from character to character . . . is also a liberation into a new self, a new identity.”

In 2019, I began teaching a course at Villanova University called *Bob Dylan and Theology*. It was not at all difficult to find material for my students: there are biographies, literary studies, philosophical analyses, theological interpretations, and historical accounts. At this stage of his career, Dylan has become not merely a celebrated songwriter but a cultural institution. He received the National Medal of Arts in 2009 and the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2016. In May 2022, the Bob Dylan Center opened in Tulsa, Oklahoma, to preserve and extend his legacy.

Still, as alluded to above, the real challenge in teaching Dylan is how to make sense of his life and work. The notion that Dylan is an elusive shapeshifter whose art represents a “[dissolution of meaning](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-news/bob-dylan-times-six-an-interview-with-im-not-there-director-todd-haynes-67251/)” would doubtless puzzle a roomful of undergraduates. But there was another problem: *was this perspective even correct?* While Dylan has certainly changed throughout his career, there are also significant points of continuity. Some scholars emphasize Dylan’s Jewish heritage, others his moral seriousness or poetic achievement, still others his well-publicized (and much criticized) conversion to Christianity in the late 1970s. Then there are the songs themselves, which repeatedly return to questions of love and betrayal, justice and corruption, sin and redemption, judgment and hope. Beneath an ever-evolving exteriority, then, lies an artist persistently wrestling with the deepest questions of human existence. This is, in fact, the issue that Sir Christopher Ricks circles in his 2003 book [*Dylan’s Visions of Sin*](https://canongate.co.uk/books/1361-dylans-visions-of-sin/). According to Ricks, Dylan is a poet in the tradition of a Shakespeare or a Milton, whose diverse oeuvre is nevertheless centered on “sins, virtues, and heavenly graces.” On this reading, there is no doubt that Dylan’s songs mean *something*. No, the real question is: What “[is] the right way to take hold of the bundle”?

I read *Dylan’s Visions of Sin* with a combination of sympathy and dissatisfaction. Like Ricks, I was not inclined to view Dylan as a mere cipher for postmodern deconstruction; I felt that there must be some way to “take hold of the bundle.” Moreover, I appreciated Ricks’ suggestion that the themes of sin and grace help us get just such a “handle” on Dylan’s vast corpus. And yet, for just that reason, I couldn’t help but find Ricks’ treatment of sin and grace to be wanting. By his own admission, he is not a philosopher, much less a theologian. As someone with a background in theology and philosophy, I wondered if Ricks’ basic insight—that sin and salvation lie at the heart of Dylan’s songwriting—might be approached in a more robust fashion. The answer, it turned out, was already under my nose.

As a longtime scholar of the great Danish thinker [Søren Kierkegaard](https://iai.tv/articles/kierkegaard-against-the-herd-auid-1826) (1813–55), it occurred to me that Kierkegaard’s famous concept of “existential spheres” (*Existents-Sphærer*) provides the right framework through which to read and to understand Dylan’s authorship—pliable enough to account for the crisis of meaning in Dylan’s shifting personae and voices, but also sufficiently purpose-driven to explain Dylan’s interest in religion and his repeated professions of faith in God. Indeed, from a Kierkegaardian perspective, Dylan is not unique in this multiplicity, for the human self develops through the cultivation of distinct yet overlapping [existential concerns and passions](https://iai.tv/articles/human-consciousness-a-tragic-misstep-auid-2352). In other words, as Kierkegaard sees it, all human beings must confront the tension between the existential spheres and, in turn, learn to negotiate among them. Dylan’s life and art beautifully and incisively reflect this process, and that is why his work endures. Kierkegaard wrote philosophy; Dylan writes songs. Kierkegaard lived in Golden-Age Copenhagen; Dylan is a product of twentieth-century America. But both explore the same perennial question: *What does it mean to be a self or, even more pointedly, to become oneself?*

I later developed this line of inquiry in my 2023 book *Bob Dylan and the Spheres of Existence*. Across four chapters and an epilogue, I argue that each of Kierkegaard’s three spheres of existence—namely, the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious—captures an essential aspect of Dylan’s songwriting. The aesthetic life seeks beauty, novelty, passion, and experience. The ethical life centers on responsibility, commitment, and moral accountability. The religious life confronts humanity’s relationship with God, recognizing that [ultimate meaning](https://iai.tv/video/in-search-of-oneself) cannot be found in pleasure or morality alone. These are not rigid compartments but recurring dimensions of human existence through which people struggle, mature, regress, and yet try again.

Kierkegaard’s spheres of existence explain why Dylan can sound like several different artists without being several different people. His songs move freely among aesthetic longing, ethical protest, and religious faith precisely because they dramatize the various possibilities of human existence itself. The restless experimentation that many listeners regard as inconstancy actually reflects a deeper existential movement—a sustained exploration of what it means to live authentically. For example, Dylan’s 1965 hit single “[Like a Rolling Stone](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwOfCgkyEj0)” centers on a beautiful yet aimless socialite identified only as “Miss Lonely.” For Kierkegaard, the aesthetic sphere can manifest in any number of ways, but it is marked, above all, by the pursuit of self-gratification. So it is with Miss Lonely, whose frenzied, boozy lifestyle has rendered her a shell of her former self. Once an “it girl,” she is now a wandering stranger.

Is there a way out of Miss Lonely’s predicament? Dylan’s ethical songs suggest that the answer is “yes,” albeit in complex ways. While tracks such as “[The Times They Are a-Changin’](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=90WD_ats6eE)” (1964) have an unmistakable political edge, urging senators and congressmen to adapt to the emerging social movements of the 1960s, Dylan would later abjure partisan politics and, in songs such as “[Sign on the Window](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QfgUkGCyuKU)” (1970), frame familial life as the pinnacle of ethical existence. Yet, by the end of the 1970s, even domestic happiness seemed inadequate in the face of global tragedy. In “[Black Diamond Bay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=73N211qQCZU)” (1976), Dylan’s narrator watches a report of a natural disaster on the evening news and, with insouciant detachment, goes to the fridge for another beer. All human plans—even well-intentioned, ethical ones—are destined to crash against the rocks of sin and finitude.

But it is just this insight that impels Dylan, no less than Kierkegaard, to probe religious life. In “[Every Grain of Sand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bV5z_rVR6Ms)” (1981), almost certainly Dylan’s best religious song and arguably one of the very best tracks of his entire career, Dylan proclaims that God is intimately related to each and every person, no matter the frailty and venality of the human condition. Dylan returns to this point on “[God Knows](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hO2l9f3IIKc)” (1990), in which he juxtaposes human anguish with divine love. Neither song is explicitly doctrinal, though they both articulate a general theological foundation for his Christian music. Indeed, even if Dylan’s so-called “gospel period” proved divisive, there is no denying that it addresses a recurring question in Dylan’s canon: How can human beings, buffeted by aesthetic desires and anxious about ethical shortcomings, find transcendent meaning and ultimate happiness? As early as “[Señor (Tales of Yankee Power)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lrxzJL8z0W8),” the sixth song on *Street-Legal* (1978), and up to and including “[Property of Jesus](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5LoSJF04Ygw)” (1981), Dylan consistently portrays Jesus as *the* transcendent disruption of the world’s attempt to commodify or rationalize the sacred. For Dylan, in short, Jesus represents the irreducible paradox of divine revelation, which Kierkegaard, too, understood as a radical encounter with God whereby the ostensible self-sufficiency of the individual is overcome.

If we read Dylan’s songs in this way—that is, as exemplifications of Kierkegaard’s *Existents-Sphærer*—then the songwriter’s celebrated transformations are neither pointlessly haphazard nor merely theatrical. On the contrary, they are expressions of an artist continually probing different ways of being-in-the-world, even as he remains preoccupied with humanity’s eternal questions. Life and death, love and sin, freedom and servitude, grace and judgment, salvation and damnation: these concepts and themes recur throughout Dylan’s work because they belong to the permanent structure of [human existence itself](https://iai.tv/articles/lifes-mysteries-cannot-be-explained-away-they-are-to-be-lived-auid-3238).

In sum, this Kierkegaardian perspective acknowledges the multiplicity that so many commentators emphasize in Dylan’s work, while also recognizing the profound spiritual seriousness that others have found there. On this understanding, Dylan is more than a chronicler of his generation or a master lyricist. He becomes one of modernity’s most perceptive explorers of the human condition, reminding us that beneath the changing fashions of every age lies the enduring question that Kierkegaard never ceased asking: *How should a human being live*? Yes, Dylan wears masks, but only so that we may [confront the truth](https://iai.tv/video/navigating-the-unknown).
