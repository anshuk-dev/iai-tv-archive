# Our culture needs to defeat dystopia

*A new Renaissance is here, and it needs storytellers*

**By Samuel McKee** | *17th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,969 words*

![Our culture needs to defeat dystopia](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/our-culture-needs-to-defeat-dystopia.webp)

---

*Discussions about the future are dominated by dystopian anxiety, leaving younger generations paralysed by despair. But research scientist and philosopher **Samuel McKee** argues this pessimism ignores our technological reality. The real barrier to a brighter future is not a failure of science, but of the humanities. Just as the Apollo missions once united society and sparked a wave of innovation, today's breakthroughs must be translated into compelling cultural narratives. Filmmakers, writers and educators must champion visions of a solvable future—and we should all become futurists.*

**Why we should all be Futurists**

Futurism is a position of science optimism, or techno-optimism. A futurist is one who strives toward the vision of the best possible future that STEM can give us, who peers over the horizon and envisions what could be in the greatest possible terms. Futurists dream of life at the end of the twenty-first century being reshaped by artificial intelligence, robotics, genetic engineering, personalized medicine, and spacefaring into a world beyond the wildest dreams of most. Furthermore, futurists advocate for that vision as well as working towards it themselves. They communicate this optimism in the media, in education, and in academic forums.

When asking people what the future may look like, you are inevitably going to find a [mixture of optimism and pessimism](https://iai.tv/articles/nick-bostrom-a-frightened-optimist-on-the-future-of-humanity-auid-1257). However, demographics matter. Generation Z and Generation Alpha are riddled with the highest levels of anxiety and poor mental health of any recorded generation. They struggle to see a future without despair as they look at economic crises, global unrest, a rapidly changing landscape from that their parents faced, and environmental catastrophe. Barack Obama once said that, despite global problems, if you could choose to live at any moment in history, you would choose today. Many are now not so sure and point to the optimism of the 1980s and 1990s as better days.

\_\_\_

If the public knew what is already possible, then they would have every reason to be optimistic about the future that can be built.

\_\_\_

If one were to push back from the 1980s a decade or so, the Moon landings of the Apollo program brought a wave of techno-optimism that swept record numbers of young people into STEM education, forming the Silicon Valley generation. Going back further, the interwar physicists uncovered a radically different world in the revolution of the new physics, leading many to believe that the greatest problems of the world could be solved. Biology saw its own revolution in the 1950s with the same perspective generated by discoveries of molecular biology, cloning, gene sequencing, and technologies such as [PCR](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK589663/). This led to the ambition of the Human Genome Project and the birth of molecular medicine, which gave us the life sciences revolution we are enjoying at present.

Why did this surge of optimism in the sciences not seep into the humanities? Why has the tide of futurism turned back? There are several reasons. The first is that this is in part a science communication problem. More talented, professional science communicators are needed to convey the great advances currently made in, for example, the life sciences, to excite the public and inspire the next generation. Secondly, the world is rampant with dystopian visions across news media, entertainment and industry. Finally, the pandemic has left a sense of distrust in science and authority in its wake.

Futurism, as a name, was once an artistic movement. Italian optimists painted a future of speed and endeavor. The First World War battered its image, so its second wave became more pessimistic in its fixation on “the machine age.” Compare and contrast the ideals of human progress that followed the Victorians with the yearnings of the art of the Romantics, and we see the push and pull of human desire to retain the purity of nature yet improve the world around us. Inevitability permeates: are we destined to fall with the empires of old into ruin and disrepair, forever battling our fallen nature in a fight to the death? Can our angst, our [confrontation with *Dasein*](https://iai.tv/articles/travels-with-heidegger-auid-914) as Heidegger formulated it, be overcome by ingenuity? In many respects, the times have changed but the questions remain the same.

**Advocates**

There are advocates for a form of futurism today that is science-optimistic. Of course, we have great science communicators who attempt to rally the public into a vision of futuristic ambition, such as Neil DeGrasse Tyson, Bill Nye, or the late greats Stephen Hawking and Carl Sagan. If I were to single out three whose writing has done well, I highlight Yuval Noah Harari, Steven Pinker and Lord Martin Rees. Each comes from a different discipline and weighs their writing with caution, but each point to a future that could be phenomenal if we succeed. Their books will be found on the top shelf of any bookstore as best-sellers.

Harari, a historian from Israel, burst onto the scene a decade ago with *Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind* and *Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow*. His writing paints a big picture of [where we have come from as a species, where we are currently, and where we could be heading](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/homo-deus/characters/yuval-noah-harari). He draws on the best of evolutionary biology, ecology, psychology and paleobiology to mix with the humanities. There are undoubtedly elements that brought us here by aiding our survival as individuals and communities that may not serve us as well going forward. Given our technological advancement, evolution may have ground to a halt, as [Richard Dawkins concurred](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF5O1zza2qI). Likewise, religion may have held a strong survival benefit but could hold us back rather than take us forwards. We have a lot to ponder but great reason to be optimistic if we are as wise as our namesake *homo sapiens* implies.

Pinker has long [called for a new Enlightenment](https://stevenpinker.com/publications/enlightenment-now-case-reason-science-humanism-and-progress) to help us take our next great leap. A professor of psychology at Harvard, Pinker’s books include *The Better Angels of our Nature*, *Enlightenment Now*, and *Rationality*. More than any other writer, [Pinker battles the dystopian pessimism](https://iai.tv/video/the-enlightenment-and-its-alternatives-steven-pinker-john-mearsheimer) that is rife in society, rallying statistics to show that, despite the impression created by the news media, humans are getting better at not being generally savage. If we commit ourselves to a new Enlightenment—a strident vision of a world built on the progress generated by technology, science, medicine, and a spirit of cooperation—humans have shown that we can do anything. Considering the great leap forward brought about by the Renaissance, the Industrial Revolution and the Enlightenment, there is [every reason to be immensely optimistic](https://iai.tv/video/why-things-get-better) about what another great leap forward could bring. We have tools that we never possessed before, and lessons learned over the continual crawl of history.

Rees is the most lucid writer of the three when it comes to science itself. The Cambridge astrophysicist, last remaining of the golden generation of Hawking, Hoyle et al, Rees headed the Royal Society for many years and has strongly advocated for science in the UK House of Lords. A [phenomenal mind](https://iai.tv/video/the-life-and-philosophy-of-martin-rees), he has encouraged hundreds of early-career scientists into pushing the boundaries of STEM further. Rees [mixes caution and optimism](https://earth.org/interview/watch-earth-orgs-conversation-with-lord-martin-rees/#:~:text=Martin%20makes%20the%20example%20of,ideal%20solution%20in%20Martin's%20view.) in good measure but [never resorts to pessimism](https://iai.tv/articles/dreams-of-a-rational-world-auid-1732). His works include *Our Final Hour*, *From Here to Inifinity: Scientific Horizons, On the Future Prospects of Humanity*, and *If Science is to save us*. Each balances the extraordinary growth of science against what we could do with it, leaving the reader well informed on [our progress and our prospects](https://press.princeton.edu/ideas/martin-rees-on-on-the-future). In his twilight years, Rees has taken science communication and advocacy very seriously and is concerned about the gap between scientific advancement and public understanding.

What this gap brings is an oversized lack of confidence in our ability to solve our biggest problems. Can we cure cancer, beat climate change, create AI that builds rather than destroys civilization, or control antibiotic resistance? You don’t know what you don’t know. If the public knew what is already possible, then they would have every reason to be optimistic about the future that can be built.

**Religion and Futurism**

When examining the writers above, you cannot help but notice their humanism. These thinkers are card-carrying humanists (some [winning awards](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/3/5/0-pinker-humanist-award/#:~:text=March%205%2C%202018-,Psychology%20professor%20Steven%20A.,called%20humanism%2C%E2%80%9D%20he%20said.) for it). This is interesting but not surprising. It is odd because at the birth of the Royal Society, the room was filled with Christians. These religious “scientists” (before that term was invented) believed in a nature that was ordered, behaved according to laws, and could be comprehended by humans because they believed in a God with such character that had created humans in his image. They pursued an understanding of nature from a sense of awe and worship, but also because they were eager to utilize this knowledge for the good of their common man. The drive to be rid of disease, improve the standard of living, and solve huge problems permeates the writings of Bacon, Boyle and many others. Likewise, it is interesting because such a large percentage of Nobel laureates are religious.

However, it is no longer surprising. The [explosion of fundamentalism](https://iai.tv/articles/science-and-religion-are-not-in-conflict-auid-2896) in the past two centuries has seen religious attitudes to the future take on an apocalyptic nature. The attitude that characterized the early Royal Society has largely given way to a view that everything will end anyway, and a new world or new heaven will come where the problems won’t exist. Why bother fixing it when it’s going to break and be replaced?

I have [written elsewhere](https://philpapers.org/rec/MCKCAF-2) that it is a [deeply Christian thing](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/642134/) to commit oneself to ridding the world of disease, improving the lot of humanity, and making the future as bright as possible. However, that mantle has been taken up with enthusiasm by the humanists, and power to them for it.

**The Artemis effect**

On April 1st 2026, four brave astronauts launched to the Moon for the first time in over a half century on NASA’s Artemis II mission. As with Christmas Eve 1968, people stopped on the street to gaze up at the night sky, knowing that humans were 200,000 miles away. One wonders how many students this will turn towards STEM. Once again, people marvel at what the free world can accomplish, and the future seems a little brighter.

Societies such as the British Interplanetary Society and the Mars Society exist to champion the future through STEM. For decades, they have advocated for the best possible future. The Artemis effect reaches beyond STEM to all of society as a powerful demonstration of technological ingenuity while serving as a unifying force that temporarily overrides the dystopian news cycle. At times like these, the futurist is vindicated.

**Utopia or dystopia?**

It is prudent to operate from a position of caution, but to reach the brightest possible future the world needs futurists.

We can live in a world where diseases like smallpox and polio that have plagued humanity since its advent are a thing of the past. We can and should live in a world of personalized medicine—we have the tools to see this become a reality sooner rather than later. AI and robotics can reshape our lives in the most extraordinary ways. Rather than ushering in global catastrophe, AI governance could end all wars and bring justice free from human corruption. Robots could relieve us from the burden of work and allow humans to work as little or as much as they want. Genetic engineering can one day remove all genetic disease, beat cancer, and alleviate the misery that comes with aging. Spacefaring can see humans colonize the Moon, then Mars, and then the Solar System, saving us from the inevitable end that comes with being a one-planet species. Futurists are committed to this vision in advocacy and work.

What must happen next is a translation into the humanities. The Renaissance and Enlightenment flourished in part because they were expressed through the arts, literature and music. The interdisciplinary reach of humanism decides whether it is limited to those at Google conferences or succeeds in trending the majority towards [utopian or dystopian visions](https://iai.tv/video/human-nature-and-the-possibility-of-utopia-paul-bloom).
