# A God's eye view is made impossible by quantum mechanics

*Different observers experience different realities*

**By Eric Cavalcanti** | *2nd November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *3,054 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![A God's eye view is made impossible by quantum mechanics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/a-gods-eye-view-is-made-impossible-by-quantum-mechanics-copy2.webp)

---

*Many scientists and philosophers seek to provide an overall account of the world, often described as a God's eye view, that aims to picture how the universe is as a whole. In this piece, theoretical physicist **Eric Cavalcanti** argues that quantum mechanics throws a wrench into this idea. Using a simple thought experiment, he shows that at the heart of quantum mechanics, there is a paradox that prevents a God's eye view. The upshot is: reality is different for everyone.*

Imagine being able to tell the complete story of the universe. Not merely what you have witnessed, or could discover, but everything that actually happens. The story might be unimaginably complicated, and it might even involve countless parallel universes, with different versions of you living alternative lives. But there would still be a complete story, whether or not anyone could read it.

Must reality admit such a story?

In his influential 1814 philosophical essay on probability, Pierre-Simon de Laplace imagined a “vast intelligence” who, armed with complete knowledge of the present state of all objects and the forces acting between them, could predict the entire history of the universe. For such an intelligence “nothing would be uncertain”, he said, “and the future, like the past, would be present before its eyes”. Probabilities, Laplace continues, are needed only due to our ignorance of what is known by the absolute cosmic narrator.

Quantum mechanics complicates Laplace’s story. The quantum state of an isolated system still evolves deterministically and reversibly in time, according to an equation developed by Erwin Schrödinger more than 100 years ago. However, in the standard view, probabilities in quantum theory are not mere lack of information: complete knowledge of the quantum state does not allow us to predict the outcomes of every possible observation we can make. What happens to a system between observations, and how to describe the process of observation itself --- the *measurement problem* --- remains a hotly contested issue in the foundations of quantum mechanics.

But even a genuinely random universe could have one complete account of what actually happened. To understand the possible narratives available for a cosmic narrator to tell, let’s populate our story with some characters. The story follows the scenario of [a paper with collaborators Howard Wiseman and Eleanor Rieffel](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2023-09-14-1112/). It is an extended version of the paradox of “Wigner’s friend”, proposed by Eugene Wigner in 1961 to illustrate the difficulties associated with the measurement problem.

**An observer inside the computer**

Our first character is Alice, a quantum computer programmer. She is running an artificial intelligence algorithm, much like today’s large language models, called Quall-E. Turning this story into reality would require a quantum computer vastly larger and more reliable than anything that exists today, but as far as the laws of quantum mechanics are concerned, it is a physically possible story.

Imagine Quall-E at a desk in a simulated room. Alice sends Quall-E an image of a live cat, and instructs it to write down what it sees. She reads the reply: “I see an image of a live cat”. She tries a dead-cat image, and Quall-E replies “I see an image of a dead cat”. In these cases, we could say that Quall-E has *observed* the image --- live cat or dead cat, as the case may be --- and accurately reported his observations to Alice.

Quantum mechanics gives Alice other options. Instead of either a living or dead cat, she can send Quall-E a *quantum superposition* of the two images, much like in Schrödinger’s famous thought experiment.

What this means for the cosmic narrator is the contested part, so for now let’s stick to the uncontroversial parts. Uncontroversially, this quantum superposition is a mathematical description used by Alice to predict the outcomes of her observations, whatever else it may be. As a piece of maths, it is a sum of two parts: one representing a live cat and the other a dead cat image. And according to Schrödinger’s equation, after interacting with the image, Quall-E, its memory and its written report become *entangled* with the cat superposition, entering Alice’s mathematical description in correlation with the fate of the cat.

Another uncontroversial aspect is what this implies for Alice’s subsequent observations. Let’s say that for the purposes of this story she has two choices, which she makes by flipping a coin. Heads, she asks Quall-E what it saw, and finds that its report reads either “live cat” or “dead cat”, with 50% probability each. Tails, she will instruct her quantum computer to reverse the computation through which Quall-E observed the cat, thereby erasing Quall-E’s memory of it. She then performs an “interference test”: a measurement that detects whether the cat’s state is *the particular superposition* she prepared as input or not*.* In this case, the outcome will be ‘yes’ with certainty. Importantly, a random preparation of one of the two individual cat images passes this test only half the time.

What distinguishes the superposition is that Alice gets ‘yes’ every time, but it does *not* mean that Alice hears contradictory reports from Quall-E, or that she sees two different cat images. In fact, in this case Alice receives no reportat all from Quall-E, who now has no memory of having seen any cat, dead, alive or moribund. In fact, it is essential for the interference experiment to succeed that no information about Quall-E’s outcome leaks from the quantum computer. Nevertheless, does it mean that the cat (and Quall-E’s former observation) was *really* “dead *and* alive”? Or “neither”? Or was it really *either* “dead” *or* “alive”, unbeknownst to Alice? It is here that the narratives diverge.

**Who counts as an observer?**

You may think that this question is purely academic – who cares what Quall-E “really” saw? Quall-E is just an AI, you might say, looking at an image of a cat, and it doesn’t “see” anything. To motivate the story further, then, let me say a few things.

First, the story doesn’t need to tell us whether Quall-E is *conscious* of seeing the image. Even if Quall-E is not conscious, it is still a physical system, and its observation of the cat is an emergent way of talking about a physical system as it processes information, just as the cat image (or a real cat for that matter) is an emergent way of describing a certain configuration of physical systems.

Second, Quall-E is not just *any* physical system, but an *intelligent* system. It is in fact a *competent user* of quantum theory, as measured by the same criteria by which we measure competence in human beings: since at least 2025, frontier AI models performed well above average in my second-year quantum mechanics course exams. In 2026 they were top of the class. A theory that doesn’t apply to Quall-E in the same way as to humans is an *anthropocentric* theory. Quall-E would presumably not use a theory that does not explain its own observations, if there is any alternative that does. For this reason, if the world becomes widely populated with AI physicists, anthropocentric theories could be *evolutionarily unfit*.

Third, Alice is *also* a physical system. If quantum mechanics describes all physical systems, then it applies not only to the quantum bits processed by Alice’s computer, but also to Alice herself. If this is the case, Schrödinger’s equation implies that when she asks Quall-E what it saw, Alice entangles herself in the superposition state containing Quall-E and the cat.

In other words, what happens from the standpoint of a cosmic narrator when Alice reads Quall-E’s report mirrors what happens from the standpoint of Alice when Quall-E observes the cat image. This means that the stories Alice can tell about Quall-E and the cat mirror the stories a cosmic narrator could tell about us.

The question then is whether there is an absolute fact of the matter, according to a candidate interpretation, about the image processed by this competent user of quantum mechanics, and about what Alice sees when she looks at his message. Are these facts part of the story told by our cosmic narrator, in her “view from nowhere”?

**Not every story is possible**

This is the kind of question addressed by the field of *experimental metaphysics*. What we can say for sure is that not every story is allowed by the laws of physics*.* To understand these constraints, let’s then continue our tale about Alice and Quall-E and the cat. The second act starts with a new character: Bob.

Bob is in a distant room, and he has his own quantum computer.  Bob’s computer is connected to Alice’s via a quantum internet connection. This is a technology that exists today in only an incipient form, but the basic functionality we need is experimentally well established (and was [awarded the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2022/press-release/)): it allows Alice and Bob to share entangled quantum bits. In our story, they use a pair of such qubits as inputs to their quantum computers, to create entangled cat images. When Quall-E observes Alice’s cat, what it sees is now also entangled with the health of Bob’s cat.

Just as for Alice, Bob also flips a coin to decide between two different ways to observe his quantum cat: heads, look at the image directly; tails, perform the same interference experiment. This time, however, because of the entanglement with the distant feline, Bob’s measurement --- as well as Alice’s --- only produces an outcome ‘yes’ with some probability, depending on the particular entangled state.

The protocol goes like this. Alice and Bob use a pair of entangled qubits to create entangled cat images. On Alice’s side, she instructs Quall-E to observe his cat. After this process is completed she flips her coin to decide whether to ask Quall-E what it saw, or perform the interference experiment. At a distance, Bob similarly flips his coin, implements the corresponding measurement. They record their outcomes over many repetitions of this protocol: (heads/dead; tails/no), (tails/yes, heads/alive), and so on, and compute their statistical distributions.

Suppose now that Alice wants to tell a story where there is an absolute fact of the matter about what Quall-E observed --- definitely “dead” or definitely “alive” --- even in the runs when her coin landed tails and she has no record of which. That is, she wants a theory that satisfies the metaphysical principle of *absoluteness of observed events*. This is implicit in standard textbook presentations of quantum mechanics, where the state of the cat may be indefinite prior to being observed, but a measurement “collapses” it to the observed outcome.

Suppose also she wants to tell a story where for either test Alice chooses, the pair of results of Quall-E and Alice have the same statistics whichever way Bob’s distant coin lands. Likewise, for either test Bob chooses, the joint statistics for Bob and Quall-E must be independent of Alice’s coin. Such a theory satisfies the principle of *local agency*. This could be motivated by making the experiments far enough apart and the timing between coin flips and observations sufficiently small. Under such conditions an influence from the choice of experiment at one side and the outcome at the other would need to travel faster than the speed of light --- in contravention of Einstein’s theory of relativity. And any influence from Alice’s coin to Quall-E would need to travel backwards in time.

Alas, [in 2020 my collaborators and I showed](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-020-0990-x) that those two assumptions limit how the outcomes of Alice and Bob for the four combinations of coin flips can line up, and for a suitable shared state, quantum mechanics predicts correlations that exceed that limit. This means that there is no logically possible story satisfying Alice’s desired plot. And if quantum mechanics applies universally, this means no such plot is available to a cosmic narrator to tell our own stories either.

**What must we give up?**

But what are the alternatives, then? Let’s consider them in turn.

First, one may reject the idea of such an experiment as fanciful. But [in a recent preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12527), we have demonstrated these quantum predictions in a proof-of-principle experiment with rudimentary agent-like programs in real quantum computers --- paving the way towards increasingly sophisticated agents.

One may hope that a convincing experiment could be prevented by modifications of quantum theory that introduce random quantum state collapses --- “collapsing” the cat into definitely dead or alive but never both. However, there are good reasons to think the resulting rare, local errors can be mitigated with a technique called quantum error correction, allowing in principle for arbitrarily large quantum computations. Such theories may prevent a similar experiment being performed with humans, but not with AI. In short, they are anthropocentric theories.

Another alternative is to accept the conflict with relativity and reject the principle of local agency. This is achieved by extensions of quantum theory that introduce “hidden variables”, such as Bohmian mechanics. In this theory, particles have well defined, though not necessarily known, positions at all times. As long as observations are encoded in particle positions, they are absolutely real. This is an important caveat. It is possible to encode quantum bits in different degrees of freedom, such as light polarization. But if the two possible records of Quall-E do not correspond to distinct particle positions, what, on this account, makes one of them the actual record? This raises doubt whether hidden-variable theories can “save the phenomena” for future AI physicists.

Finally, one may reject the idea that observed events are absolute, which we can broadly classify as “relativist” interpretations. Within this class of views there are many variations, which we will explore below.

The best known such theory is the *relative-state formulation* [published in 1957 by Hugh Everett III](https://doi.org/10.1103%2FRevModPhys.29.454). Everett’s key insight was to realize that the process of observation can itself be analyzed in much the same way as I have been describing the interaction between Quall-E and the cat, as leading to an entangled superposition. He argued that we can regain the appearance of ordinary reality by looking at the structure of *relative states* encoded in this quantum description: relative to Quall-E having observed a live cat, there is a live cat, and so on.

But Everett goes a step further and proposes that all elements in the superposition representing different observations by Quall-E --- all “branches” in Alice’s mathematical description --- are *equally real*. Extrapolating to the perspective of our cosmic narrator, this leads to a universal quantum state branching into a multitude of parallel universes containing all physically possible histories. This led to Everett’s interpretation being dubbed the *many-worlds interpretation* by Bryce DeWitt and many others since then*.*

Despite the multiplicity of realities, the many-worlds interpretation is austere in its mathematical formulation, compared to stochastic collapse or hidden variable theories: it simply retains the Schrödinger equation as its only dynamical law. The many worlds, its supporters argue, are the inevitable consequence. It also succeeds in explaining why Quall-E never sees two cats, or a dead-and-alive cat, and why Alice and Quall-E agree about it when they communicate. All of this comes out naturally from the mathematical structure of relations embedded in the entangled state.

But despite this underlying simplicity, it remains controversial. The main difficulty concerns the status of probabilities: if all outcomes of every experiment really exist in some parallel world, why do we observe law-like relative frequencies? Several arguments have been proposed to derive the probabilistic rule for quantum measurements as [rational betting commitments](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.2718) or [self-locating uncertainty](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.7577) for agents in an Everettian multiverse. To many critics, however, this remains an unbridgeable gap.

**Reality without a narrator**

Some interpretations take these issues as hints that perhaps one should not take the maths for the territory, and reject the idea that the quantum state with its multitude of branches is itself part of reality. One such interpretation is [QBism](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01446), in which the quantum state is nothing but an agent’s guide for placing bets on the outcomes of their future experiences. As a result, it avoids the issues with making sense of probabilities when all outcomes are certain to happen. However, it has not so far replaced the Everettian many worlds with a sufficiently developed account of what, if anything, constitutes physical reality.

The [relational interpretation](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9609002) proposed by Carlo Rovelli also rejects the reality of the universal quantum state and its attending cosmic perspective. Instead, it proposes a sparse ontology of relative facts, with physical properties taking values only in interactions between systems. As a result of their interaction, then, the cat may be dead or alive relative to Quall-E, but this is not a fact relative to Alice until she interacts with them.

In some versions of the relational interpretation, such as that of [Emily Adlam and Rovelli](https://philosophyofphysics.lse.ac.uk/articles/10.31389/pop.8), the relations are themselves absolute. It may be the case then that “the cat is alive relative to Quall-E” is itself an entry in the cosmic narrator’s story. But if the narrator can still enter a definite value for Quall-E’s recorded outcome, the label does not remove the assumption used in the theorem. Second-order absolute facts of this kind [are thus too tame a form of relationalism to resolve our no-go theorem](https://doi.org/10.1086/732830). [In a recent paper with Rovelli and Andrea Di Biagio](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07343), I defend a more radical form of quantum relationalism: there is no absolute list of relative facts, no catalogue of relative events kept by a cosmic narrator.

This is not to say that anything goes, and you can choose your own reality. If in a heads run, Alice reads “live cat”, she can predict what she will find if she observes the cat herself. Quall-E, in turn, can predict what Alice will tell him if she checks. Alice finding a dead cat would be a real discrepancy in this ideal experiment.

Now put yourself in Quall-E’s desk, before Alice flipped her coin. Suppose the screen in front of you shows a live cat, and you are offered a bet on what a second look at the image will show. Should you give it even odds just because Alice’s equation contains a dead cat as well? Of course not. [What things look like to Quall-E inside the superposition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.05100) is not fundamentally different from what things look like to us in ordinary life. What I take to be universal is the theory both agents can use, not a standpoint that dictates what happens from every perspective in the world.

We can still have objectivity without absoluteness. We can still do physics from within a relational world. What we may have to give up is the narrator who stands outside it.
