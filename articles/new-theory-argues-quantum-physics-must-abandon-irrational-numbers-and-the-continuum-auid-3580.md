# New theory argues quantum physics must abandon irrational numbers and the continuum

*The mysteries of quantum physics belong to maths, not nature*

**By Tim Palmer** | *20th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,372 words*

![New theory argues quantum physics must abandon irrational numbers and the continuum](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/new-theory-argues-quantum-physics-must-abandon.webp)

*Quantum mechanics works perfectly in experiments, but Oxford physicist **Tim Palmer** argues it rests on a mathematical fiction: building the theory on the continuum of real numbers, including irrational numbers like √2. Palmer’s radical alternative abandons the continuum of real numbers and eliminates quantum mysteries that aren't actually physical—from Schrödinger's cat, to Einstein’s “spooky action at a distance.” The theory makes a testable prediction: quantum computers will fail beyond 400 qubits, hitting a fundamental limit set by the discrete structure of nature itself. The coming quantum computing race will determine whether Palmer has identified a basic mistake in our most successful theory.*

If you know one thing about quantum mechanics, the atomic theory formulated just over 100 years ago by Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger, it is that quantum systems evolve by “jumping” discontinuously from one state to another; indeed, the words “quantum jump” and “quantum leap” have become part of everyday language.

If you know one other thing about quantum physics, it is that it describes a [deeply mysterious world](https://iai.tv/articles/tim-palmer-quantum-theory-eliminates-quantum-uncertainty-auid-2263) where cats can be simultaneously alive and dead and where choices about how we measure quantum systems on one side of the galaxy can instantaneously alter the states of quantum systems on the other side of the galaxy. [Such weirdness](https://iai.tv/articles/the-quantum-world-reveals-reality-is-made-of-relations-not-objects-auid-3501), we are sometimes told, is beyond the capability of the puny human brain to understand.

In this brief essay, I am going to try to persuade you that you are wrong about both of these apparent facts. On the first, I will argue that quantum mechanics depends much more vitally on the continuum of numbers than do the classical theories of physics that quantum mechanics replaced. Indeed, the iconic quantum jumps only occur at the endpoint of quantum evolution when we measure or observe a quantum system. Before then, quantum systems evolve absolutely continuously according to Schrödinger’s eponymous equation. On the second point, I argue below that if we “tweak” quantum mechanics by banishing the continuum in favour of a more discrete structure, all the so-called incomprehensible mysteries vanish in a puff of smoke. I refer to this theory, where the continuum has been banished, as [Rational Quantum Mechanics (RaQM)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2523350123).

The existence of the mathematical continuum goes back to the fifth century BCE, when Hippasus of Metapontum showed that not all numbers can be described as the ratio of two whole numbers. Hippasus famously showed that the square root of 2 cannot be such a rational number. He was punished for having uncovered such a disturbing truth by being drowned at sea (either by his fellow Pythagorean followers or by the Gods, depending on which story you read). Today we have become rather blasé about the irrational numbers. However, I believe we have been lulled into a false sense of security.

Until Newton and Leibniz invented the calculus, one could view Hippasus’s irrational numbers simply as an oddity. But the calculus required us to make sense of these irrational numbers not just here and there, but everywhere. Eighteenth and nineteenth-century mathematicians like Augustin-Louis Cauchy did their best to tame the irrational numbers, and in so doing made respectable the continuum field of real numbers, which combines the rational and irrational numbers into one set of numbers. Nowadays we hardly bat an eyelid when using the real numbers—not least because they provide precisely the tools needed to analyze mathematically the classical geometry of [Euclid](https://iai.tv/articles/tim-palmer-quantum-computing-is-the-key-to-consciousness-auid-2410).

But weird stuff lies just under the hood. As the twentieth-century mathematicians Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski showed, if you take the continuum seriously, it is possible to take a solid sphere, divide it into five pieces, and reassemble the pieces to make a sphere twice as big. Clearly this is not physical! Something goes wrong if you take the continuum too seriously.

But for physicists, the calculus was a boon, and its utility far outweighed any weirdness that resulted from embracing the irrationals. Newton was of course able to formulate his laws of motion using the calculus. In the nineteenth century, Claude Navier and George Stokes used the calculus to formulate the laws of fluid mechanics (still used today in, for example, weather prediction). Later in the century, James Clerk Maxwell formulated the laws of electromagnetism using the calculus. And in the twentieth century, Einstein used it to derive his general theory of relativity.

And so, when Erwin Schrödinger formulated his version of quantum mechanics using the calculus, physicists leaped at it as they had already developed the mathematical tools needed to solve his eponymous equation.

The continuum became hard-baked into the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics a few years after Heisenberg and Schrödinger’s papers when John von Neumann formulated his famous axioms of quantum mechanics. The first of these stated that the state of a quantum system is described by a vector in a continuum vector space, named by von Neumann after his mentor, the famous mathematician David Hilbert. As it evolves under the Schrödinger equation, the vector describing the quantum state moves around continuously in the continuum Hilbert Space. Only when an experimenter measures the quantum state does it finally jump, seemingly randomly, to one of a finite number of possible measurement outcomes.

I find Von Neumann’s naming of the state space of quantum mechanics as “Hilbert Space” deeply ironic because Hilbert famously asserted “the infinite is nowhere to be found in reality.” In his 1925 lecture “On the Infinite,” he argued that while infinity is a necessary, non-contradictory concept in mathematics, it is a theoretical “idea” rather than a physical reality. And yet here was von Neumann proposing Hilbert Space, where the infinitesimal—the number one divided by infinity—plays a vital role as an element of physical reality!

In RaQM I have developed an approach to quantum physics which eschews the continuum. Here the double-meaning behind the word “rational” is deliberate. RaQM is, on the one hand, based on rational numbers, and on the other hand, its interpretation is totally comprehensible.

Not only that, RaQM is a much simpler theory than quantum mechanics. For example, in von Neumann’s axioms for quantum mechanics, one axiom relates the probability of a measurement outcome to the squared amplitude of a (projected) Hilbert vector. Why this so-called Born Rule should be, we are not allowed to ask—it is simply axiomatic in quantum mechanics.

By contrast, Born’s Rule is an emergent property in RaQM—much more satisfactory in my view. Why should this be?  Ultimately, this has to do with the way complex numbers are represented in RaQM. Readers may recall that a so-called complex number involves the quantity i=√(-1). When such complex numbers form a continuum field, then i can only be defined by axiom—it cannot be related to something simpler or intuitive (if you square any normal integer, positive or negative, it is always positive). However, if complex numbers form a discrete set of objects, then it is possible to define i in a more comprehensible and constructive way. To see this, let me use the symbols a and b to stand for either of the numbers 1 or -1.  Define the operator i by the equation i(a,b) = (-b,a). Here (a,b) denotes a pair of numbers, or a string comprising 2 bits. If you apply i twice (try it for yourself!) you will get i^2 (a,b) = (-a,-b) = -(a,b). Hence, as an operator acting on bit strings then i is indeed a square root of minus one. But we can see where it comes from—it does not have to be introduced axiomatically. In RaQM, the quantum state of a qubit, the basic element of a quantum computer, is representable as a bit string (very long – see below), the frequency of whose digits necessarily satisfy Born’s Rule. Hence, in RaQM Born’s Rule is emergent from a more intuitive definition of complex numbers. Surely this is more satisfactory and simpler than a theory where both i and Born’s Rule can only be defined by axiom.

Let me give an example of how RaQM is so much more comprehensible than quantum mechanics. In 2022, the three physicists Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger won the Nobel Prize for experimentally demonstrating the violation of the so-called [Bell inequality](https://iai.tv/video/astonishment-fear-and-quantum-physics-tim-maudlin), named after physicist John Bell. The media proclaimed that these physicists had finally disproven Einstein’s belief that the laws of physics are locally causal. So, it seems, we must simply accept this incomprehensible idea that the choice of measurement setting that one experimenter makes on one side of the galaxy can instantaneously affect the outcome of an experiment that another experimenter performs on the other side of the galaxy.

But in RaQM it isn’t necessary to believe such an absurdity. To explain this, I need to describe Bell’s experiment in a little detail. In particular, the violation of Bell’s inequality found by Aspect et al results from combining correlations found experimentally on four separate sub-ensembles of entangled photons. For the sake of argument, we can suppose the four sub-ensembles of photons are created and measured on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday, respectively. On the Monday, the polarizers measuring the first sub-ensemble of photons are set with Monday’s polarizer orientations. On the Tuesday, the polarizers measuring the second sub-ensemble are set with Tuesday’s orientations, and so on. Hence, we obtain four different entanglement correlations which together can violate a bound which, it is claimed, could not possibly be violated if the world was locally causal.

This claim is wholly incorrect in my view. If we have a theory and we want to know whether it necessarily predicts Bell’s inequality (which would make it inconsistent with experiment), we need to know whether, if Tuesday, Wednesday or Thursday’s particles were measured with Monday’s polarizer settings, the theory produces well-defined measurement outcomes (we don’t need to know the outcomes, we just need to know whether well-defined outcomes exist). But these latter settings are counterfactual: in reality, Tuesday’s particles were measured with Tuesday’s polarizer settings and not Monday’s.  To be more precise, in order to derive Bell’s inequality, for each individual entangled pair of photons, we must assume the well-definedness of three counterfactual worlds with counterfactual polarizer settings (e.g. a pair of Monday’s photons, with Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday’s polarizer settings).

Normally, physicists invoke the idea that experiments are free to choose measurement settings as they like, in order to justify the assumption that these counterfactual worlds have well-defined outcomes. Hence, they say “but on Monday the experimenters were free to have chosen Tuesday’s polarizer settings”, and they dismiss the idea that some of these counterfactual worlds might be ill-defined.

However, in RaQM, such matters are quite subtle and the argument above about free choice is irrelevant. If you go carefully through the [mathematics](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/3189/1/012006/meta) (which I can’t do in this short article), you find that for each entangled photon pair, the exact polarizer settings for at least one of the three counterfactual worlds is necessarily associated with an irrational number. In RaQM, measurement outcomes in such “irrational’ worlds are ill-defined by definition. Importantly, this has nothing to do with experimenter free choice. It is clearly impossible for experimenters to choose to set their polarizers with irrational-number orientations. When experimenters set their apparatuses, they can only do so to some nominal accuracy. For any nominal accuracy, there always exist exact rational-number measurement settings consistent with their choice. Free choice is a red herring!

So what does the violation of Bell’s inequality mean, if not nonlocality? It’s that the laws of quantum physics are [more holistic](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-primacy-of-doubt-9780192843609?cc=kh&lang=en) than the laws of classical physics. There are many deep implications of this conclusion. But it would take too many words to do justice to the idea by writing more about it here.

A crucial question for any physicist proposing a new theory of physics is [how it can be tested experimentally](https://iai.tv/articles/mathematical-beauty-should-not-eclipse-truth-auid-3534). Since RaQM is a deeply holistic theory, it makes sense to estimate the degree of granularity of Hilbert Space by assuming it is set by gravity, which binds the whole universe together. (The continuum of Hilbert Space is then associated with the quantum mechanical limit where gravity is set precisely to zero). With this one obtains an explicit estimate of the amount of information that can be held in a qubit—equal to the length of the bit string described above. The number is humungous—about 2^400 bits—but finite nevertheless. Curiously (but not a coincidence given the holistic remark above), this number approximates the amount of information in the observable universe.

If you entangle N qubits in a quantum computer, the number of dimensions of Hilbert Space equals 2^N, at least when N is large. On this basis, even though 2^400 is an enormously big number, when N>400 there won’t be enough bits of information in the quantum state to cover all of Hilbert Space. The quantum advantage of any quantum algorithm that utilizes all of Hilbert Space will saturate in computers that utilize more than 400 (error-corrected) qubits. Shor’s algorithm is an algorithm that utilizes all of Hilbert Space.

In recent months, there has been much excitement about the development of quantum computers and quantum algorithms to break modern encryption codes in the coming decade. I can’t wait for such computers to be built and for computer scientists to try to break such codes. Their expectations assume quantum mechanics remains valid for large-dimensional Hilbert Spaces. My prediction, based on RaQM, is that quantum mechanics will fail in such a regime, and quantum computers will never be able to break such codes. If this happens, it will be the first time quantum mechanics has ever failed—and I guess that would be quite a red-letter day!

If my prediction is correct, by far the most important application of quantum computers will be in finding new theories of physics—importantly, in finding theories which synthesize quantum and gravitational physics. This surely will be more important than any commercial applications in code-breaking. RaQM is not just [another interpretation of quantum mechanics](https://iai.tv/video/the-quantum-unknown) (like Copenhagen, Many Worlds, Pilot Wave Theory, the Subjective QBist interpretation, and so on). It is a new theory—one where the continuum of Hilbert Space has been banished. I think Hilbert himself would be pleased.
