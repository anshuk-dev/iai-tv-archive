# The psychedelic origins, and future, of Western thought

*The psychedelic genealogy of morals*

**By Ricky Williamson** | *9th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 20 mins* | *3,967 words*

![The psychedelic origins, and future, of Western thought](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-psychedlic-origins-of-western-thought.webp)

---

*It is now widely accepted by researchers such as Michael Pollan and Brian Muraresku that the Ancient Greeks took a psychedelic-like narcotic at their Eleusinian mysteries, which were annual ritual initiations. The taking of this narcotic led to what William James called ‘the mystical experience’. Plato is known to have attended these mysteries and would have taken this psychedelic-like narcotic, named Kykeon. The influence this had on Plato, and as a result, Western culture as a whole, was seen by Nietzsche, in ideas like Plato’s cave and in religion more broadly. In this piece, philosopher **Ricky Williamson** argues that Nietzsche's critique of Plato, Christianity, and the morality that still shapes our lives today, all have the psychedelically-induced mystical experience at their core. The modern psychedelic prohibition has left thinkers afraid to draw this clear and obvious conclusion in Nietzsche’s thought, argues Williamson. Western thought was shaped and has its origins in these psychedelically-induced mystical experiences. With the psychedelic renaissance in full swing, its future may lie in them too.*

“Under the influence of narcotic potions hymned by all primitive men and peoples… those Dionysiac urges are awakened."

—Nietzsche, *The Birth of Tragedy*

The coming psychedelic age represents an unpredictable future, with psychedelics increasingly legalised and re-entering the mainstream. In this future the very nature of human consciousness will change. A person is not the same person before and after a psychedelic experience. A culture in which a growing number of people have that experience cannot be expected to remain the same either.

What can we learn from the psychedelic philosopher, Friedrich Nietzsche, that could help us cultivate the positive potential of this approaching psychedelic age? And how are the history of morality and the history of psychedelics intertwined, and how will this connection re-manifest in our coming future?

Nietzsche’s references to intoxication and narcotics are among the most misunderstood and underappreciated aspects of Western philosophy. This article, among other things, hopes to go some way to correcting that.

**Morality as power**

Since Nietzsche, it has been widely accepted that morality is historically contingent and that what we call “the good” may just be an accident of our culture—and therefore, not *really* “the good.” Nietzsche’s *On the Genealogy of Morality* outlines how our morals came to be—not as the result of some divine judgement or, as Kant would argue, arrived at through reason—but as the result of a power struggle.

Our morality is born of the relationship between two historical groups: the masters and the slaves.

Nietzsche argues that once upon a time, “the good” just meant “what we masters think is good” and “the bad” just meant “what we masters think is bad.” This makes sense. If you have power, you also have the power to indiscriminately judge. This is how Nietzsche claims the Ancient Greeks lived, and he admired this in them. This carefree living. This carefree seeking after whatever “good” they desired, without any guilt or shame for doing so.

In order to overthrow their masters, the slaves—unable to defeat their masters by means of force—instead set out to poison their masters’ minds. The slaves persuaded the masters that the good was not really “what we masters think is good,” but rather the good was ‘“what God says is good” or “what reason says is good”; which just so happened to be the lifestyle and values of the slaves in the masters’ world. Meekness, selflessness, equality... for how else could the slaves live under their masters’ rule?

Instead of overcoming the masters by force then, the slaves *implanted* morality into the minds of their masters. Yes, kind of like the movie *Inception*. A morality that, if followed, would make the masters impose meekness, weakness, and selflessness upon themselves.

By making the masters believe certain things were wrong in the eyes of God or reason—two forces very difficult to argue with—the slaves gained an advantage. Instead of indiscriminately doing whatever they pleased without guilt—such as the very having of slaves for example—the masters now felt that certain things, whether they got pleasure from them or not, were *wrong*. Obviously, slavery continued. The birth of morality did not end all morally wrong acts. Rather, it was the very idea that certain things could be such a thing as “morally wrong” that had been created.

Furthermore, although it may have started in the mind, as a kind of ancient psy-op, the birth of morality did not come without the use of pain later. Nietzsche uses examples like “a pound of flesh” being taken to repay moral debts. Tortuous punishments were dished out, for example, for crimes like stealing until the expectation of the punishment became internalised, argues Nietzsche. The pang of conscience you feel when you consider stealing, the guilt you feel after stealing, may actually be an internalised expectation of real physical pain. That is why the pang of conscience comes with that visceral physical sensation—one that almost *hurts*. Through a kind of inherited trauma or PTSD, the pang of conscience, the urge to be moral, has been literally branded into us.

While Nietzsche doesn’t provide much historical evidence for his claims in *On the Genealogy of Morals*, the contemporary popular historian [Tom Holland’s book,*Dominion: The Making of the Western Mind*](https://www.amazon.com/Dominion-Making-Western-Tom-Holland/dp/1408706954), offers a historical backing to Nietzsche’s story.

It is important to note here that doing the “moral thing” does not necessarily mean “doing the *actually* good thing.” It might be seen as moral to give every dime you have to the next homeless person you see, for example, but this might not turn out to have been the best thing for you to have done in the long run. Helping an old lady cross the road or feeding the starving; these things likely are good. Stealing is very likely actually bad. But the morality of concern here refers to that feeling, that despite our instincts, we *ought* to do something else. This *ought*is an internal pull away from our primary instincts, instincts which might often seek and lead to the good had we followed them, and toward the outer authority that is morality. This *ought* might even be how self-consciousness—reflective, ego-consciousness—arose.

Moral progress over history has been a general force for good in this world. Nietzsche does accept this in places. No one, certainly not this author, is arguing for an “anything goes.” kind of morality. Rather, it is being argued that to be as good as we can be, morality must be transcended. To be transcended does not mean to be completely left behind, but to be incorporated while being radically improved upon.

**Psychedelics, Ancient Greece, and Nietzsche**

What has this got to do with psychedelics? Before drawing the connection, first, some history, and a little mysticism.

In 392 CE, Roman emperor Theodosius I enacted the first recorded psychedelic prohibition. He closed the Eleusinian Mysteries, a festival in which the [Ancient Greeks took kykeon](https://nah.sen.es/vmfiles/abstract/NAHV1N1201328_38EN.pdf), a psychedelic potion synthesised from ergot (which can also be used to make LSD).

The Ancient Greek philosopher, Plato, is known to have attended these mysteries and wrote that he and his fellow mystery-goers, “saw the blessed sight and vision and were initiated into that which is rightly called the most blessed of mysteries, which we celebrated in a state of perfection.” Sound familiar, psychonauts?

This “blessed sight,” “initiation” and “state of perfection” suit the description of the mystical experience. Psychedelic experience can include all kinds of contents—visual, auditory, etc.—but that moment in a psychedelic experience when the ground falls out from beneath you, and you are suddenly held in a great white light, and you know all the secrets of the universe, bliss, knowledge, truth… that particular highlight and climax of the experience has a name, “the mystical experience.”

The American philosopher William James outlined seven core features of the mystical experience: Unity, Noetic Quality (objective insight or truth is revealed), Transcendence of Space and Time, Sense of Sacredness, Deeply Felt Positive Mood, Paradoxicality and Ineffability.

[Knowledge and experience of the mystical experience](http://iai.tv/articles/the-reality-of-the-mystical-experience-auid-2620) are what’s missing from most people’s understanding of religion, history, and philosophy, mostly for the simple fact they have not had the experience for themselves. Furthermore, the Enlightenment meant that we no longer trusted people’s first-person reports of their mystical experiences—much of religion is just such a report. But in the scientific age, we do not believe anything until we experience it for ourselves. The lack of a belief in the testimony of others is therefore one of science’s greatest strengths and greatest weaknesses.

Nietzsche, it appears, was aware of the mystical experience and was aware that the Greeks were having it at their Eleusinian and Dionysian festivals. As evidence for this, first of all, Nietzsche used drugs. It is well-reported that Nietzsche suffered from various ailments during his life and spent much of his life in ill health. Less well reported is the fact that Nietzsche used an array of drugs in order to deal with these ailments, and the drugs he used are known to have psychoactive effects.

It is known Nietzsche used, at the least, opium, potassium bromide and chloral hydrate. These drugs can induce intense hallucinations; to claim that these drugs had no effect on his consciousness would be to claim that Nietzsche was extremely out of the ordinary.

Indeed, there is an account of Nietzsche hallucinating “an abundance of fantastic flowers… constantly growing and changing forms” —inspiration for his philosophy of the eternally in-flux, forever creating will to power, perhaps? Nietzsche himself even credits taking a “huge dose of opium” for allowing him to “come to reason.”

Comically, Nietzsche often acquired his drugs by pretending to be a medical doctor, using his title as an academic doctor as cover. Further evidence of Nietzsche’s drug use can be found in [Peter Sjöstedt-H’s](http://www.philosopher.eu/) essay on this subject, “[Anti-Christ Psychonaut](https://psychedelicpress.co.uk/collections/books/products/noumenautics),” which I must credit for the collection of the above research.

Nietzsche then, was aware that extraordinary drug-induced experiences were possible, as he had them himself. And he seems to have been aware that [the Ancient Greeks were having them](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/05/26/this-is-your-priest-on-drugs). The god Dionysus, whom the Dionysian festivals were named after, is the ancient Greek god of madness, religious ecstasy, intoxication and the union of paradoxes. Dionysus therefore has much in common with William James’s features of the mystical experience. Nietzsche speaks of the Dionysian revealing the “oneness amidst the paroxysms of intoxication”. Elsewhere, Nietzsche comments that, “In Dionysian intoxication there is… the retardation of the feelings of time and space”, again this echoes the transcendence of time and space in the classical mystical experience. Nietzsche clearly focuses then on ‘intoxication’ and it seems clear that he clearly knew that drugs were a possible means toward attaining the mystical experience, writing that “Under the influence of narcotic potions hymned by all primitive men and peoples… those Dionysiac urges are awakened.”

When was the last time you saw a “blessed vision in a state of perfection” down the pub on a Friday night?

\_\_\_

Since Dionysus is also the god of wine, it has been argued that Nietzsche is talking about alcohol here. One may argue that Nietzsche is writing about standard alcohol-induced drunkenness here, not a psychedelically-induced mystical experience. However, the complete mystical experience, as defined here, is rarely achieved through alcohol consumption alone. When was the last time you saw a “blessed vision in a state of perfection” down the pub on a Friday night? Although, to be fair, maybe you have witnessed “the retardation of time and space”.

Furthermore, Nietzsche is known to have had a distaste for alcohol. His descriptions of the Dionysian are much more in line with some kind of psychedelic intoxicant rather than alcohol: “one dreams and at the same time experiences the dream as a dream”; “nature discloses itself in pleasure and suffering and insight all at once”. There are parallels again here with James’s mystical experience: the paradoxes and the “noetic quality.”

Furthermore, [Brian C. Muraresku’s](https://iai.tv/video/how-to-die-before-you-do-brian-muraresku) work [*The Immortality Key*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Immortality-Key-Uncovering-History-Religion/dp/1250207142)argues, among many other fascinating things, that what used to be called ‘wine’ had many more psychedelic ingredients and properties than what we call ‘wine’ today.

Finally, as mentioned previously, it is now widely accepted, including by figures like [Michael Pollan](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/05/26/this-is-your-priest-on-drugs) and [Norman Ohler](https://www.instagram.com/normanohler/?hl=en), that the Ancient Greeks in their Eleusinian mysteries, in which the god Dionysus was celebrated, consumed not alcohol but the psychedelic kykeon, which would have produced the mystical experiences Nietzsche describes. What Nietzsche, writing before James, calls the “Dionysian experience” is therefore what we now call the “psychedelically-induced mystical experience.”

**The psychedelic overcoming of power**

So, in the pre-moral world of the Ancient Greeks, a psychedelic potion was being taken at the Eleusinian Mysteries and inducing mystical experiences. But then, in 392 CE, Theodosius I, enacted his psychedelic prohibition and closed the Mysteries down.

It is not a coincidence that the psychedelically-induced mystical experience was outlawed at the same time as a power struggle between the masters and slaves was giving rise to morality.

Psychedelics-takers attest that power-structures, like organised religion and governments for example, seem rather silly following their experiences. In the 1960s and ’70s the psychedelic movement co-developed with the anti-war, anti-Nixon movement. There are of course many historical reasons for this co-development. But there are phenomenological reasons too. The psychedelically-induced mystical experience is one antagonistic to power-structures, including the internalised power-structure that is morality. The late psychedelic researcher at Johns Hopkins, Roland Griffiths, commented in line with this that, “there is so much authority that comes out of the mystical experience that it can be threatening to existing hierarchical structures.”

These features tell us that the mystical experience contains a mystical truth. But it is one that is paradoxical and unable to be communicated in language—even to oneself. Another key feature of the experience is that it ends—William James called this the experience’s “transiency.” The mystical truth one learns can’t be put into words and is necessarily forgotten. People can have lasting “awakening” experiences, but even then, the mystical truth they “know” cannot be described.

These features, I argue, are antagonistic to the power-structure of morality because morality relies on the appearance of being *true*.

Remember, morality is an internalised power-structure, so it requires no outer enforcement because we enforce it upon ourselves. And we do this partly due to the inherited trauma already mentioned, but also partly because we believe morality is true.

Recall that under morality, the good becomes “what God says is good” or “what reason says is good.” God and reason. Reason is a tool which we use to reach truth. So, a morality that claims to be reasonable also claims to be a *true* morality. And what about God? Well returning to Plato, Nietzsche suggests Platonists hold that “God is the truth” and that “the truth is *divine*”. God acts as a kind of universal authority, very similar to truth – neither can be argued very well against once accepted. While the phenomenology of the mystical experience feels at once like a mystical truth is revealed and like you are meeting God; it therefore connects God and truth intimately in a way many do not appreciate. Therefore, claims of authority using God and claims of authority using truth use the same referent—even though there are two forms of truth at play: truth found through reason and the experience of the revelation of mystical truth in the mystical experience. For more on the connection between God, truth, the mystical experience, psychedelics, and morality, read my book on these topics [*The Psychedelic Nietzsche*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Psychedelic-Nietzsche-Twain-Traherne/dp/B086P9BCM5/ref=tmm_pap_swatch_0?_encoding=UTF8&qid=&sr=)*.*

\_\_\_

God is not an all-powerful man with a white beard. God is an experience you can have.

\_\_\_

The phenomenology of the mystical experience, however, shows us that any group claiming to know the *truth* is making a false claim. For, the experience of noetic truth in the mystical experience shows us that truth is paradoxical (it is literally *un*reasonable, so truth—and morality—cannot be come to via reason), ineffable and incommunicable (and so it cannot be used legitimately in human, social power-relations which require communication in one form or another), and it is necessarily forgotten (and so cannot be legitimately used in power-relations when everyone is back within normal, non-mystical consciousness).

While many do experience what they call “God” on psychedelics—I have experienced this myself—the God experienced is that same paradoxical, ineffable *thing*that one sees in the mystical experience. God is not an all-powerful man with a white beard. *God is an experience you can have.*

Therefore, this God, although it can be experienced, provides no authority or legitimacy to a supposed morality or power base once we are back in non-mystical consciousness. To be sure, in a sense, those claiming to “know God” really have experienced God if they have had a mystical experience—this is where the first powerful church members likely really, semi-legitimately, claimed their knowledge of God from. But if that God is paradoxical, ineffable, and necessarily forgotten once back in normal consciousness, any claims to derive power from, or to be in cahoots with, that God, are false claims.

Following Theodosius I’s psychedelic prohibition in 392 CE and the closing down of the Eleusinian Mysteries, the mystical experience was un-democratised and those who had had it could use their experience as a false claim for power and a semi-legitimate claim to “know God.”

\_\_\_

But a historical mistake was made. We made the method for reaching the mystical experience divine, instead of the mystical experience itself.

\_\_\_

Furthermore, with psychedelics outlawed, early Christians and priests had to turn to ascetic practices—fasting, meditation—in order to bring about the mystical experience in place of psychedelics. But a historical mistake was made. We made the method for reaching the mystical experience divine, instead of the mystical experience itself. People saw ascetics having mystical experiences, attempted to emulate them, and became ascetics themselves. The mystical experience, Nietzsche states, became “the value of values” and “the positive as such”… God of course being the central value of Christianity, and with the mystical experience being an experience of perfection, it is unsurprising that experiencing God became the most central aim of life. And it is also therefore unsurprising that a morality formed around the attempt to emulate those who had the mystical experience.

Of course, not everyone is willing to meditate in a cave all day. Some followed the ascetic life of the priests only loosely, therefore leading to the ascetic-based morality of everyday people. We made asceticism itself divine and the basis of morality—meekness, equality, selflessness (this final one, “selflessness,” has a double meaning when linked to both the mystical experience—loss of self—and morality—putting others first—showcasing my arguments about the connection here). This divination of the method for reaching the mystical experience is also, by the way, why golden statues of the Buddha meditating are found all around the world.

A preference for mystical experience brought on through psychedelics rather than through Christian asceticism can be seen as summing up Nietzsche’s whole project. Summing up his own thought, at the end of his autobiography (of-sorts) *Ecce Homo*, Nietzsche signs off, “Have I been understood?—Dionysus against the crucified.” The god of mystical intoxication against the god of mystical suffering.

**Our psychedelic future**

So, the slaves made their asceticism the basis of morality for two reasons:

1. As a power play against their masters to get them to relinquish their power willingly and of their own accord in pursuit of “the good” of reason and favour with God.

2. As the divination of a method for reaching themystical experience—asceticism (which, following Theodosius I’s psychedelic prohibition, was the only remaining method on offer).

But psychedelics—that other method used to reach the mystical experience—do not require asceticism, and so, in their return to mainstream culture, our morality need not be based on asceticism—neither for reasons of power nor mystical reasons.

This is why the re-introduction of psychedelics in the coming years will lead to a new morality. We will see that the old morality was based on false claims about truth, reason and God, and false claims of power and authority. Once we see God and truth for ourselves in the psychedelically-induced mystical experience, we are free to choose our own morality, seeing that the creators of the old one were hoodwinking us. Psychedelics democratise God and thereby free up truth and morality.

Without legitimate claims to know God, truth or the results of pure reason to back up morality, and without the divination of ascetic practice, the apparent authority of morality crumbles.

We have no more reason to internally enforce morality upon ourselves if we no longer believe it is true. Remember, morality was implanted into our minds. Morality is a mental construct. And mental constructs don’t function so well when they are no longer believed in. Psychedelics then really do help us come to our senses.

Yet, we’ve had this morality for 2,000 years; it has become ingrained in our flesh and bones, and has become something more than habitual. Simply learning that morality isn’t true on an intellectual level is not enough. It must be experienced as untrue: the ground on which morality claims to stand must fall out from beneath it, and beneath us. That ground falls out in the psychedelically-induced mystical experience.

What’s more, in psychedelic studies people with PTSD who take a psychedelic are having positive outcomes—particularly those that have the mystical experience. And thus, if morality is also the result of inherited trauma, the psychedelic experience may be able to help us overcome the traumatic origins of morality too. These traumatic origins are not just found over the course of human history, but over the course of all our lives. You know that fighting with your sister is wrong, because your father yelled at you if you did so. We all get our morality, and learn what is right and wrong, from more or less painful punishments. So again, morality on the individual level, as well as morality on the level of humankind, has been imprinted on us by the use of trauma, pain, and force. There are [some](https://www.behold-retreats.com/post/ancestral-trauma) who argue this healing from both personal trauma and inherited trauma is occurring due to the reintroduction of psychedelics.

Once the trauma of morality is transcended, Nietzsche argues we become much more artistic beings. When you aren’t trying to conform to a set “truth,” you can be much more creative.

One of my favourite lines in Nietzsche is this: “it is only as an aesthetic phenomenon that existence and the world are eternally justified”. What does this mean? It means that instead of being conformers to some apparent set truth, or even truth seekers like scientists or mystics… we should become artists; artists that seek to justify existence—make life worth living—by making it aesthetically awe-inspiring. So let us hope that the coming psychedelic age will be an ever-more creative and artistic one.

**Conclusion**

In the absence of moral authorities, we can gain something akin to the style of living of the Ancient Greeks that Nietzsche so admired. Instead of living by what we think we ought to do, or doing what some authority judges we *should* do, we can instead once again simply say, “I think this is good, so it is good” and “I think this is bad, so it is bad.” The result is not evil, chaos, or anarchy. As suggested earlier, we should transcend morality, not completely do away with it. We should still have laws and social norms. However, they should be pragmatic and aim at the aesthetic, instead of seen to be fundamentally true. This might help us get rid of silly laws and social norms—such as drug laws, for example.

Furthermore, most of the evil done in this world is done by those following orders, people who think they know truth, or by people who are trying to force their version of “the good” onto others. The result of a psychedelic post-moral world, I hope, will be one of kindness and increased well-being (as psychedelic studies are showing it could be). Nietzsche’s philosophy may help us get there—let us not allow it to be used to take us to those dark places it has historically; such a paradoxical vision mirroring that paradoxical feature of the mystical experience. With any luck, the coming psychedelic age will create a more peaceful, loving, more creative, affluent, happier world—one which really is… good.
