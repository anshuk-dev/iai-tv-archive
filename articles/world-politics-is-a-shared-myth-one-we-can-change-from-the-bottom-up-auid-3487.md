# World politics is a shared myth, one we can change from the bottom up

*Now is the time of monsters*

**By Jeremy Moulton** | *6th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,544 words*

![World politics is a shared myth, one we can change from the bottom up](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/political-myth2.webp)

---

*As the liberal international order unravels, global politics is entering an interregnum where old institutions persist but no longer persuade. Political theorists **Jeremy Moulton** and **Nicolai Gellwitzki** argue that what is collapsing is not just global order but the political myths that once gave it meaning. As empire returns to public discourse, shifting stories of power and legitimacy are reshaping what states see as possible in world politics.*

In 2026, the world finds itself in a moment of transition, an interregnum. To borrow an often cited line from [Antonio Gramsci’s](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gramsci/) Prison Notebooks: “the old world is dying, and the new world struggles to be born: now is the time of monsters.” Much can be said about this moment, about transformations of the global order, material capacities, late capitalism, or the rise of fascism around the world. These are all urgent concerns. Yet, there is one crucial dimension that has remained underexplored - a dimension that might explain many of these wider themes - the role of political myths and how such myths are changing in the contemporary world.

In everyday language, as well as in media and official discourse, the word myth is often used to dismiss an event, a claim, or a narrative as mere fiction. Something described as myth is treated as untrue and used to delegitimise particular claims. Myth becomes synonymous with “fake news.” Yet a closer look, informed by anthropology and philosophy, reveals a different picture. [Stories become myths not because they lack factual accuracy but because they convey significance](https://iai.tv/video/myths-modernity-and-meaning-betty-sue-flowers). They meet a human need for order, transform chaos into cosmos, and offer coherence in moments of uncertainty. They are collective stories, told and retold across generations, subtly shifting over time and place. All narratives convey meaning, but only myths convey the significance that shapes our understanding of the world, explaining we live as we do and helping us make sense of the world around us. Political myths are powerful because people act as if they are true, whether or not they are.

\_\_\_

A new age of myth is taking shape.

\_\_\_

Myths are not new. They have shaped human societies since the first civilisations and remain central to religion and politics. Consider ancient Greek myths, from the age of heroes to the Trojan War. These events did not unfold as recounted but this is not what matters. What matters is that the telling of those stories, those myths, shaped political imagination and action for contemporary audiences. They conferred legitimacy on some authorities, justified certain actions, and delegitimised others. Social, religious, and political life were ordered within a cosmos defined by myth. Crucially, myth is not a thing of the past. Human beings, as existentialist Hans Blumenberg argues, have never overcome the need for significance and are unlikely ever to do so. From the audiences of Ancient Greece to those of today’s overwhelming media landscape, people seek to understand their world through the stories they tell and retell. Myths persist, even as their form and content change.

At this interregnum, where we see many of our seemingly long-established global norms beginning to falter, our concern lies with political myths about the international realm - a vital site of inquiry we began to explore in a [recent article published in *International Organization*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/new-age-of-myth-political-narratives-and-the-reconstitution-of-world-order/165309F74E32FF153A36D3A0CC6058A8). After World War Two, the so-called Liberal International Order was established through institutions such as the United Nations and the UN Security Council. Beyond institutions, this order rested on a certain political mythology: stories of peace and a rule-based order grounded in formal equality among nations. The institutions of liberal order were underpinned by myths. [After great conflicts, what Ikenberry calls “big bangs,” victorious nations have historically created global orders in their image](https://iai.tv/articles/america-from-the-ashes-auid-1691). What is unusual today is that the formal institutions remain, yet the myths that gave them meaning have eroded, and new ones have yet to form. Discussions of the Liberal International Order are now conducted with a backwards-looking analysis, wondering what went wrong. But this leaves a gap in our mythic landscape, one where we can readily identify the old world which is dying but cannot be sure about what new world is struggling to be born.

\_\_\_

Hierarchy has always existed beneath the principle of sovereign equality.

\_\_\_

Where does this leave us? A new age of myth is taking shape. The global order is being reinterpreted through a profound shift in its symbolic foundations. Just as the age of heroes in Greece or the aftermath of the Second World War generated narratives that endured, so too will today’s stories shape institutions, ideas, and foreign policy for decades. This raises urgent questions. Which political myths will define this new world? Which forms of global order will they legitimise, encourage, or normalise? What visions of possibility will they open, and which will they close? Anne Carson warns that “to live past the end of your myth is a perilous thing.” Without widely accepted myths to order the international realm, politics enters unstable and dangerous territory.

One can see this most readily and recently in [the US’s intervention in Venezuela.](https://iai.tv/articles/us-national-security-strategy-venezuela-and-why-the-world-just-turned-upside-down-auid-3463) The Trump administration’s decision to send the US army’s Delta Force into Caracas to kidnap the President of the country could, through a very generous reading, be interpreted as an institution of order by a powerful state looking to combat the criminality of another. However, Trump’s subsequent declarations that the US will “run” Venezuela, potentially for years, would quickly put paid to such claims. The brazenness and transparency of such interventions, in contrast to, for example, the Bush administration’s War on Terror, mark a sharp shift in global politics and the narratives surrounding international action.

From Donald Trump’s talk of territorial acquisition, whether Canada, Greenland, the Panama Canal, or Gaza, to Vladimir Putin’s assault on Ukraine, imperial aspiration has returned to public discourse with force. These developments signal the erosion of long-standing commitments to equality, sovereignty, and territorial integrity, alongside the collapse of liberal myths that once shaped global politics. Hierarchy has always existed beneath the principle of sovereign equality, yet commentators often note that the conduct of the United States, Russia, Israel, and others increasingly resembles nineteenth-century empires. Journalistic, academic, and official accounts describe a new imperial era and often declare that the liberal international order has ended. As the BBC’s International Editor, Jeremy Bowen, recently summarised the contemporary global situation, we are at risk of the world going back to the “age of empires”.

\_\_\_

Recognising the power of political myths demands profound reflection on how we understand global politics.

\_\_\_

These stories do more than describe the world; they shape it. Political myths are not static. As Hans Blumenberg writes in Work on Myth, they evolve, adapt, and gain power through reinterpretation. Chiara Bottici emphasises that myth is produced across multiple sites of repetition and circulation. Contemporary narratives of imperial resurgence and liberal decline are not neutral observations. They participate in a wider process that gives collective meaning and direction. Their force lies in their ability to offer orientation. They explain the present and suggest responses that appear sensible or inevitable. Myths do not describe the world with factual precision; they express collective will. Critical self-awareness is essential. The stories societies tell themselves guide action, but they can also lead into dangerous territory.

Talk of a new age of empires does more than describe events. It reshapes global politics. It makes territorial expansion seem reasonable and revives older ways of imagining space and power, such as spheres of influence and buffer zones. These ideas belong to the vocabulary of nineteenth-century imperial politics, and their return signals a deeper shift in how power is conceived today. In some commentary, [Russia’s invasion of Ukraine is framed as understandable, even justified](https://iai.tv/articles/russia-putin-and-the-new-geopolitical-order-auid-2398), with international law treated as secondary to the importance of the games played between the great powers. Such arguments assume that these great powers have rights denied to smaller states. The novelty lies not in hierarchy itself but in the growing acceptance of imperial ambition as normal and inevitable. The narrative of a new imperial era brings together Russian aggression, Chinese influence, and renewed Western interventionism into a coherent story. It presents great power rivalry as the natural state of world politics, privileging containment, bloc formation, and balancing while marginalising multilateralism, pacifism, and global solidarity. The power of this story comes not from its truth but from its capacity to shape imagination and guide action, much as Cold War myths once did.

Recognising the power of political myths demands profound reflection on how we understand global politics. They are not simply top-down narratives; they are collective stories that confer meaning and shape the world we inhabit. The Liberal International Order is in steep decline, or perhaps it has already perished - a death humanity has enacted collectively. In the spirit of Nietzsche, we confront a world unmoored from inherited values, a world in which the old gods of universalism and neoliberal capitalism are dead. In this time of monsters, we cannot return to the old liberal order or its governing mythology. Yet within this void lies the possibility of creation. We are called to decide, to assert new myths that give form to the chaos, to will new values into being, and to prevent the logic of great power politics and the age of empires from setting the horizon of what is possible.
