# Remembrance Day, Veterans Day, and the philosophy of forgetting

*A moment to put politics aside*

**By None** | *7th November 2025* | *Read time: approx. 16 mins* | *3,248 words*

![Remembrance Day, Veterans Day, and the philosophy of forgetting](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/25-11-07.remeberance.ata.webp)

---

*This Remembrance Day and Veterans Day, a minority of people will know someone who served in WWII. As time creates distance, many commentators fear the next World War is becoming increasingly likely. Who we remember is a political act, as shown by figures like Putin currently. But to truly honour the dead, former intelligence officer in the British Army Andy Owen, argues we must leave politics aside.*

On 11th November, the UK will mark [the eightieth Remembrance Day](https://www.britishlegion.org.uk/get-involved/remembrance/about-remembrance) since the end of the Second World War (the same day the US will mark [Veterans Day](https://department.va.gov/veterans-day/history-of-veterans-day/) that honours all those who have served. However, [Memorial Day](https://www.cem.va.gov/history/Memorial-Day-history.asp), which commemorates America’s fallen military personnel across all its wars, is more comparable). This year, there will be few veterans left from the Second World War, and none from the First. My generation will be one of the last where nearly everyone had a grandparent who fought in one of those two wars. It is now a minority who know someone who has served; the personal connection for most has been severed. Younger generations, who lack this connection, place less significance on the remembering of the dead of these conflicts. For older generations in the UK, Remembrance Day became synonymous with a personal remembering of lost loved ones. As the war generations leave us, Remembrance Day can no longer serve that purpose. At the same time, the white, Portland stone of the Cenotaph has increasingly become a site of protest, as groups attempt to colour it with their own narratives, antithetical to those original architects of remembrance. Remembrance Day, though, is a modern phenomenon. There are older festivals that remember the dead that have been overshadowed. By reviving these festivals, we may be able to offer a more personal celebration of the dead, and return Remembrance Day to an apolitical occasion to remember a specific sacrifice.   
  
Across the UK there is a network of triangulation pillars sitting atop prominent hills, always in line of sight of two other such points. These help you locate yourself in the physical space of these isles. There is another network of stone markers: the war memorials on every village green and town square. At the heart of this network is the Cenotaph in Whitehall. For much of the last century, if the view from green to green, or square to square, were unobstructed, you could use these memorials to locate yourself in the national consciousness. This network is, in large part, down to the work of one man.

Now, a 2023 Ipsos poll in the UK found that only 33% of young people, including Gen Z and Millennials, knew that Remembrance Day commemorates the First World War Armistice.

\_\_\_

When Sir Fabian Ware attempted to sign up in 1914 he was told he was too old to fight, so he obtained command of an ambulance unit and spent the war marking and recording the graves of those killed (in doing so he was mentioned in dispatches twice). His work evolved into the founding of the then Imperial War Graves Commission (now Commonwealth Grave Commission (CWGC)) in 1917, of which he was co-chair. After the war, the commission also became responsible for monuments. Ware attempted to use the commission’s work as a tool for peace, giving annual Remembrance Day addresses to urge against future wars.  
  
On the first Remembrance Day in 1919 (then Peace Day), Ware observed that when troops marched past the first temporary Cenotaph, if the dead had marched four abreast, it would have taken this ghost army of slaughtered poets and doomed youth three and a half days to pass. There was no triumphalism or singing of victory songs. Ware ensured the Cenotaph was designed to be a secular memorial to create an equality of remembrance and bring together those in mourning regardless of class or creed. Ware saw the project as a unifying one, not just for the British public but all across the then empire (in which he was a firm believer). Most families of the fallen were unable to visit distant graves, but they could at least touch the cold communal stone and run their fingers over the names of their loved ones chiselled into our country’s memorials: it provided a physical anchor amid a void of absence for them, and their loss became chiselled into our national consciousness.

In the US, President Wilson proclaimed November 11, 1919, as the first Armistice Day. In 1954, it became Veterans Day, a day to honour American Veterans of all wars. There was already a day set aside for remembering the fallen. Memorial Day, the nation's foremost day to mourn its deceased service men and women, had emerged from the collective grief at the end of the American Civil War, which claimed more lives than any conflict in US history and required the establishment of the country’s first national cemeteries. Originally Decoration Day (a day to decorate the graves of the war dead with flowers), it was formalised by a "Memorial Day Order" in 1868. The modern proclamation calls on Americans to observe it by “praying, according to their individual religious faith, for permanent peace". The traditions associated with Memorial Day, held the last Monday of May, include the wearing of poppies, the visiting of cemeteries or memorials, family gatherings and participating in parades. An American friend told me that growing up, “we spent Memorial Day cleaning up grave sites and memorials etc. of our family... [it is] probably the day I learned the most about my ancestors”.  
  
Now, a [2023 Ipsos poll](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2023/11/11/young-people-remembrance-day-first-world-war-survey/) in the UK found that only 33% of young people, including Gen Z and Millennials, knew that Remembrance Day commemorates the First World War Armistice. At the same time, there are those who, ignorant to Ware’s intent, are making Remembrance Day less a time for reflection on those lost and more of an opportunity to display a certain type of patriotism, linked to a nostalgia used for political purposes. The use of the fallen for political purposes is not new.   
  
Ever since Greek general Pericles, in his funeral oratory, praised the war dead of 5th-century Athens to encourage others to keep on fighting, remembrance has been used for political purposes. In 1919, the German War Graves Commission proposed a People’s Day of Mourning. In 1934, the National Socialists created a Day of Commemoration of Heroes, shifting the emphasis from reflection to hero worship. The Nazis used the dead as a political tool, claiming they had been betrayed by Jews, Communists, and a conspiracy of victorious nations. In Russia, the Day of Remembrance and Sorrow commemorates the anniversary of the axis invasion, but there is also Victory Day on 9 May. It has been celebrated on and off until revived by President Putin, who has been using the memory of the Russian war dead to promote his own version of history, that suggests that both the Second World War and the war in Ukraine were both against Nazis threatening the motherland, and blames NATO for repeated humiliations.  
  
Who you do not remember can be as much of a political choice as who you do. It was only in 2021 that a report by the CWGC concluded that “pervasive racism” was behind the failure to properly commemorate non-white First World War troops who died for Britain. At least 116,000 predominantly African and Middle Eastern casualties “were not commemorated by name or possibly not commemorated at all”. We should be more familiar with the stories of soldiers from all countries that suffered the tragedy of the Great War. Indian poet Sarojini Naidu wrote in 1915 of Indian soldiers “Scattered like shells on Egyptian sands” imploring us to “honour the deeds of the deathless ones/Remember the blood of thy martyred sons!” The poems of the war poets are perhaps the greatest memorial to the lost generation, but poems by commonwealth writers are largely absent, even as the African-American poet Walter E. Seward pointed out “Bullets have no special people/No one especially they hate/And the Germans’ large artillery/Sure did not discriminate”.

Journalist David Rieff, having witnessed the use of historical grievances as motivation to fight in the Balkans, asserts that “almost all the conflicts in the world are caused by too much remembering: refreshing religious divisions, tribal feuds, border conflicts, humiliations and expulsions”. Grudges will persist when your foes’ crimes are remembered but yours are forgotten. Successful truth and reconciliation commissions in Argentina, Northern Ireland, and South Africa have balanced the need for justice for those who have suffered against the need to avoid future injustices. The challenge is to acknowledge the injustice and move past it, while honouring the memory of the victims of injustice through remembrance. The two tasks are not antithetical, though they always exist in uneasy tension.   
  
The task of remembering the adversities endured and the sacrifices made by a group can give hope for the future continuity of that group. That hope can provide personal comfort. Philosopher Samuel Scheffler argues that the collective afterlife - the continued non-spiritual, actual existence of persons who share at least some of our values, aspirations, and needs after our death - is much more important to us than we realise. So much so that our understanding of our own life relies on an implicit assumption of it occupying a place in an ongoing human history. We understand our lives in the context of a temporally extended chain of lives and generations, those that come before us and those extending into the future beyond us.   
  
He explores his claim through two thought experiments: the Doomsday scenario and the infertility scenario. In Doomsday we are to imagine that we live to the end of our natural lifespan but thirty days after our death the earth collides with an asteroid and is destroyed. In infertility, humans have become infertile: everyone alive lives to the end of their natural lifespan, but no new generations follow.    
  
Through examining our reactions to these, he argues that our continuing to regard things as mattering to us is more dependent on our confidence in the survival of humanity than we realise. Many activities we engage in, such as trying to find a cure for cancer, developing clean energy sources, or contributing to a field of academic study, are undertaken with the understanding that their ultimate goals may not be achieved during our lifetime. That these activities still seem worthwhile reveals that what happens after we die matters greatly to us. In Scheffler’s scenarios, these projects would seem pointless and this would have a profoundly negative impact on the lives investing in them. Going further, he suggests that the absence of the collective afterlife would potentially put even simple pleasures - such as a good meal, being in nature, or intimate relationships - out of reach when no longer part of an ongoing collective human history.

How to maintain your hope in humanity
By Ian James Kidd](https://iai.tv/articles/how-to-maintain-your-hope-in-humanity-auid-3054)**

According to the old saying, you die twice: firstly when you take your last breath and secondly on the last occasion someone speaks your name. Maybe now it is the last occasion someone gets a social media birthday reminder for you, or even the final occasion someone interacts with an algorithm, created from your on-line activity, that mimics your responses for loved ones to interact with after you have died. The saying points to a fear of being forgotten. That there are people who will carry the memory of you on after you have died makes it possible to feel that you still have a place in your social world of the future, even if you will not be around to experience it. But Scheffler’s experiments suggest that this is more than personal remembrance. We want to be able to preserve one’s place in a web of social relationships with those we don’t know and may not have even been born yet, but have a commonality and continuity with us through shared values.   
  
One of the most important ways in which people attempt to preserve and sustain their values is by participating in traditions that support those values. Traditions are defined as collaborative, multi-generational enterprises devised by human beings to satisfy the deep human impulse to preserve what is valued. Remembrance Sunday is such a tradition. In subscribing to a tradition that embodies values one embraces, one seeks to ensure the survival over time of what one values. Taking part in traditions, you become the custodians of values that will be transmitted to future generations. Participation in a tradition can be a way of achieving a value-based relation to those who come after us. War memorials are so emotive because they do more than preserve the names of the fallen, leaving an immortal mark in stone; they become representative of national values, which for many are aligned with personal values they want to survive beyond their own lives.   
  
But what traditions and rituals are there for those whose values do not align as closely with the state’s? Before the state, remembrance was controlled by the church. Remembrance Day was only added in the twentieth century by the Church of England to Allhallowtide. This is the Western Christian season encompassing All Saints' Eve (Halloween), All Saints' Day (All Hallows') which honours Christian saints and martyrs, which was moved to 1st November in the ninth century, and, on the following day All Souls' Day (first celebrated in the tenth or eleventh century, becoming widely accepted by the fourteenth century), which focuses on honouring all faithful Christians "who are unknown in the wider fellowship of the church, especially family members and friends". The International Day of Prayer for the Persecuted Church on the first Sunday of November is also included. Today, All Saints and All Souls have become conflated, and many Christians remember all dead souls on All Saints' Day all around the Christian world. In the UK, both have been eclipsed by Remembrance Day.

In his comparative study of mythology and religion, The Golden Bough, anthropologist Sir James George Frazer, theorised that both All Saints Day and All Souls Day were attempts by the “Catholic Church to eradicate an old heathen festival of the dead”. The introduction of All Saints, an attempt to divert the devotion of the faithful from the souls of the dead to the exemplary lives of saints, proved a failure, so the Church reluctantly decided to bow to popular superstition by admitting a Church sanctified feast of All Souls. Frazer concluded that, “both efforts failed” as the old traditions persisted.   
  
In Celtic Briton, the old heathen festival of the dead, when the boundary between this world and the Otherworld blurred, was Samhain. It was marked by the opening of ancient burial mounds that were seen as portals to the Otherworld. Some Neolithic passage tombs in Great Britain and Ireland are aligned with the sunrise at the time of Samhain, suggesting even earlier festivals. Great gatherings and feasts were held, and the souls of dead kin were thought to revisit their homes seeking hospitality. A place was set at the table for them, and a chair was left empty. People went dwelling-to-dwelling, in costume to confuse any malignant wandering spirits, reciting verses in exchange for food.   
  
By the Middle Ages, a Christian tradition of giving of soul cakes - small round cakes with sweet spices made to commemorate the dead - had developed in Britain and Ireland. They would be set out with glasses of wine as an offering for the dead, and either on All Hallows' Eve, All Saints' or All Souls' Days, children and the poor would go "souling", begging for cakes door to door.   
  
Frazer describes similar festivals, from those held by Californian Indians to those in Assam in India, and from Day of the Dead rituals in South America to ancestor worship in Asia. There are common themes across them all, the most obvious being the blurring of the boundary between the dead and the living. The dead visit the living, or the living visit the dead. This happens most literally in Madagascar in the "turning of the bones," ceremony, where every five years or so, families exhume their ancestors' remains, rewrap them in fresh shrouds, and dance with them in celebration before returning them to the family tomb. Feasting is involved in most cultures, and fires, fireworks or candles light up the dark. These rituals often involve lamentation, but just as commonly end with rejoicing. These rituals provided an opportunity to celebrate the dead and even converse with them. The ubiquity of these festivals and common themes across them point to a universal human need.

By Alastair Norcross](https://iai.tv/articles/there-is-no-good-and-evil-only-better-and-worse-auid-3371)**

As infant mortality rates have decreased and life expectancy has increased, [we in the West have also become more secular.](https://iai.tv/articles/iris-murdoch-and-why-the-wests-spiritual-crisis-wont-be-solved-by-religion-auid-3395) We have become more used to life and more focused on heavenly rewards on earth rather than in an afterlife. Death is less common an intrusion, but for most of us, we see it as the end; there is no continuation post-mortem. Maybe it's time to rekindle our belief in an afterlife.   
  
National Remembrance falls between personal memorialising, first-hand and inherited memories, and objective history, linking the specifics of individual memory to the general of collective consciousness. Collective remembrance involves subjective interpretation, as past acts are imbued with different meanings by individuals and states, winners and losers, victims and perpetrators. We should remain alert to those who wish to repurpose it for their own aims in the present, hijacking our traditions to focus not on unity in loss, but division that will only lead to more loss. But, there is also a need for personal remembrance away from the national rituals. In the US, for some, the much older Memorial Day has provided a space to do this in a way Remembrance Day has not yet, but is still tied much more closely to national remembrance than more ancient festivals.

Many traditions of personal remembrance are designed to help the deceased on their journey into and through the afterlife, and they give us shared rituals to help those remaining move through their journey of grief, a journey destined to never reach an end. They also prepare us for our own demise, giving us a glimpse of our own earthly afterlife. Without these rituals, personal remembrance is pushed to the margins, forced to sneak up on us when we are not expecting: a song, a smell, a turn of phrase, a key date significant to a life that is no more and means nothing to anyone else.   
  
This year, before I donned my poppy and remembered those who I served alongside and those who made the ultimate sacrifice, I sat down on 2nd November with my family. We set one extra place and left one chair empty. We shared stories together of those who are no longer with us, keeping their memories alive, keeping them in our web of relations, imagining them for a moment in that empty space. We talked of the values they passed on to us and the values we hope we will pass on. I found comfort in the belief that there will be those to come that one day might set a place for me.
