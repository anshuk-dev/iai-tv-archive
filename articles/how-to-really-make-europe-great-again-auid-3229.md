# Trump, Europe, and Spengler's revenge

*Europe's decline and the rise of Caesarism*

**By Simon Glendinning** | *19th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,272 words*

![Trump, Europe, and Spengler's revenge](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/how-to-make-europe-great-again.webp)

---

*In a post-Trump world, many commentators point to this moment as the opportunity for Europe to coalesce around a shared vision. But is this really a time for optimism, or is this hope misplaced? Head of the LSE's European Institute, **Simon Glendinning**, argues that instead of optimism for Europe’s renewal, we should look to the interwar German philosopher Oswald Spengler as a prophetic guide to our new age of strongmen. If we are to navigate our new world of Caesars, we should look to Spengler and his record of uncomfortably accurate predictions.*

As a preference for so-called ‘strongman’ leaders begins to grow again across the European-West, a preference for leaders with the authority to ignore or override democratic institutions, Oswald Spengler’s writings on world history once again look increasingly prophetic. Spengler’s perspective certainly goes against the grain. Attempts to understand what is going on in the present generally only have a minimal historical framing. Explanations of significant events—changing voting patterns in national and European elections, or interest rate cuts by central banks, or an uptick in strikes over pay, for example—can be proposed without appealing to anything so extravagantly ambitious as a conception of world history.

\_\_\_

Spengler’s study of our world in decline is, in my view, increasingly unavoidable.

\_\_\_

There is, however, another way in which what is happening in our time might be taken as a suitable object for study: not simply explaining events now unfolding in the world of the European-West but attempting to provide a deeper understanding of the world within which these events unfold.

When I started to pursue that ambition, Oswald Spengler’s history of the [European-West in terminal decline](http://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-the-west) was hardly visible to me. But, today, I think his work demands our attention. If we ask, with respect to the European-West, *what is happening in our time*? We cannot but know that, today, the destiny of our world may itself be in question. And with its fate in the balance, Spengler’s study of our world in decline is, in my view, increasingly unavoidable.

Spengler began to develop his ideas as early as 1911, but the bulk of the writing for what became *Der Untergang des Abendlandes* (fairly translated as ‘The Decline of the West’ although the title can also be read more poetically, as ‘The Downgoing of the Evening-Lands’) took place during the war years, with the complete work appearing in two weighty volumes between 1918 and 1922. The book struck a powerful chord and became an unlikely bestseller.

\_\_\_

European-West, which emerged, he thinks, around AD 1000, is destined, like every culture, to run its course in a little over 1,000 years

\_\_\_

The centrepiece of Spengler’s text is a wholesale rejection of the idea of a single, linear history of ‘Man’ that encompasses all human cultures, a history of a transition for ‘Man’ from ancient primitiveness through to some [civilised modernity](http://iai.tv/video/the-rules-of-war) – with *des Abendlandes*, the nations of the European-West, at the head of that universal history. No, says Spengler, there is no such single, linear history. Instead, we simply see the blossoming across the earth of various great cultural formations: the Egyptian, Babylonian, Indian, Chinese, Arabian, Mexican (Aztec, Maya), Classical (Greco-Roman), Western, and perhaps still in its infancy, Russian.

Each of these formations has its birth, its cultural flourishing, and, as its last act, the realisation of a civilisation, a final flowering that is also the onset of its death. Spengler’s great claim is that the stage we are at now in the European-West is…*death*. The culture of the European-West, which emerged, he thinks, around AD 1000, is destined, like every culture, to run its course in a little over 1,000 years. For those of us living in the European-West during the first centuries of the 2000s, it is our fate to live through this end, to see the collapse of democratic modernity and the rise of what Spengler called ‘an age of Caesarism’—a time of the politics of the ‘strongman’.

It is hard not to think that Spengler is profoundly relevant today. We should also appreciate how, right now, we have virtually nothing else to orient us with respect to our history. Philosophical histories of emancipation and progress provided the civilisational discourse through which Europeans and Americans in the 18th and 19th centuries had made sense of their lives. Today, unless we are Marxists, we altogether lack such a discourse. Our contemporary condition is, as a result, profoundly unmoored from history. Let me say a few words about the condition that is facing us at this strangest of times.

After two devastating world wars of European origin and a Cold War that threatened global annihilation, the wave of optimism that greeted the fall of the Berlin Wall and the end of the totalitarian nightmare in the communist East seemed to herald a bright new era. German reunification, EU enlargement, the introduction of the euro, Obama’s election in the USA, and the Arab Spring all appeared to signal an upward turn—even a return to a robust sense of world-wide democratic progress.

Yet, a sequence of convulsions in societies across the post-War West soon made it clear that we were not entering an age of settled democratic achievement, let alone a coming democratic finality. In 2001, the strange date-named event 9/11 had already provided a moving image of a collapsing world, but as crisis followed crisis, the sense of an accelerating downfall became unavoidable.

\_\_\_

Spengler asserted that, in the face of the onrush of a new age of Caesarism, “optimism is cowardice.”

\_\_\_

In view here are not only crises that challenge the preservation of stable democratic societies in the European-West—the financial crisis, the eurozone crisis, Russian land seizure, the refugee crisis, the Covid-19 pandemic—but also a sequence of democratic challenges to existing democratic conditions—the votes against the European constitution, the Brexit referendum in the UK, Trump’s election in the USA, the #MeToo movement, the emergence of the BLM movement, trans-activism, climate activism, eco-activism, Indigenous activism, decolonisation activism, populisms of the left and right.

In these conditions, public opinion has become increasingly polarised and cynical and mainstream party politics has become increasingly hollowed out, with many citizens in the European-West feeling they are being left behind or disenfranchised by the de-localising forces of globalisation. In his history of the specifically European present, [*Homelands*](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/442543/homelands-by-ash-timothy-garton/9781529925074), Timothy Garton Ash cites the Austrian-German novelist Daniel Kehlmann reflecting that, in what Garton Ash dubs our now “post-Wall” world, “something is just coming irretrievably to an end.”

What if this is exactly right? What if these events in the post-Wall period belong to a world which is, as Spengler argued, fast falling apart?

In his book [*Man and Technics*,](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.53913) from 1931, Spengler asserted that, in the face of the onrush of a new age of Caesarism, “optimism is cowardice.” His own unflinching reflections on the downfall and collapse of our democratic modernity refused to accept that any other future might be possible for us. And he would live to see early Caesar-types arising in the Soviet Union, in Italy, and in Germany. Clearly, that sequence did not end there.

Twenty years after Spengler gave his disturbing prognoses on our time in *Decline*, in an essay from 1941 entitled “[Spengler Today](https://ctwgwebsite.github.io/assets/pdf/zfs/adorno-spengler.pdf),” Theodore Adorno began by noting that, despite his early celebrity, Spengler’s ideas had already been forgotten, and had been forgotten “with the speed that he himself ascribed to world history when he said that it was fast developing the momentum of a catastrophe.” But [Adorno](http://iai.tv/articles/what-michelangelos-david-can-teach-us-about-radical-politics-auid-1016) did not see this forgetting as the end of Spengler. On the contrary, “the course of world history has itself vindicated his prognoses to an extent that would be astonishing if these prognoses were remembered. The forgotten Spengler takes his revenge by threatening to be right.”

Spengler has mostly remained forgotten, but perhaps we cannot afford to continue to forget him. The profoundly difficult truth here is that *we can no longer say with confidence that our present-day democracies in the European-West have a long and secure future*. Already beyond the modern politics of hope that belongs to the old modern *archeo*-*teleo-eschatological* ideas of progress towards some perfected future democracy or an attained political and social perfection to come, we live today “between ages” as Spengler said in [*The Hour of Decision*](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.503389). In our present, the inner dissolution of the world of the European-West has been rapid, deep, and apparently irretrievably final.

\_\_\_

What if, as Derrida argues, this idea of democracy is essentially futural, and hence always retains within itself, even within its “ideal” concept, a void of sorts, a space left vacant for inventive political thinking to come?

\_\_\_

We can no longer hide from the fact that what [Francis Fukuyama had regarded as the history-ending “triumph of liberal democracy”](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/13399/the-end-of-history-and-the-last-man-by-fukuyama-francis/9780241991039) has, [as Jacques Derrida put it in 1993](https://www.routledge.com/Specters-of-Marx-The-State-of-the-Debt-the-Work-of-Mourning-and-the-New-International/Derrida/p/book/9780415389570?srsltid=AfmBOooCwUKmnYwxZHky0z3ct8Sic9yRl4lRW6Elcs65Ys7SoVWJTZnH), “never been so critical, fragile, threatened, even in certain regards catastrophic.” In our now unequivocally post-Wall times, it will belong to thinking concerned with what is happening in our time, not only to look at significant current events, but to try to make sense of the troubled condition of the world within which they take place, trying to make sense of a world falling apart. In such conditions, and when, once more, we have Spengler in view, I think we need to take our heading as thinking people from Adorno, who asks whether we can “take a stand…*against* Spengler without a false posture of strength and without the bad conscience of official optimism.” Adorno was right not to regard this task as an easy one, and I am not convinced by the self-confessed “utopian” character of his own attempt to do so.

Nevertheless, beyond utopian hope, there is, perhaps, an authentically Spenglerian alternative to Spengler’s own conviction concerning the end of democracy. Spengler’s characterisation of the “spirit” of the European-West is that it is ‘Faustian’, everywhere marked by an insatiable will to transcend all limits and boundaries: it knows no end. But what if [“the *uniquely* European idea of democracy”](https://iupress.org/9780253316936/the-other-heading/) is marked by just such a “spirit”? What if, as [Derrida](http://iai.tv/articles/derrida-and-the-trouble-with-metaphysics-auid-2089) argues, this idea of democracy is essentially futural, and hence always retains within itself, even within its “ideal” concept, a void of sorts, a space left vacant for inventive political thinking to come, and hence for the advent of what, in every here and now, can only appear as a “democracy to come” absolutely beyond the horizon of all present anticipation, beyond all present future-projecting visions of a yet-to-be-created ideal democracy? On this understanding, the idea of a final formation of a democratic condition makes no sense, whether that is some utopian perfection (Adorno’s hope) or some conclusive collapse (Spengler’s conviction). Perhaps there is still room to affirm something Spengler simply did not foresee: namely, that a genuinely Faustian idea of democracy might yet take hold.

\_\_\_

Party politics in liberal democracies today seems increasingly hollowed-out and pointless; the capitalist mode of management of capital in our time increasingly life-lacking and taskless; and the general outlook can seem utterly bleak.

\_\_\_

On the other hand, perhaps it is already too late for this. We in our day inhabit democracies that seem altogether to have lost interest in themselves beyond maintaining their present (past-created) form – they are “in form” (to use a phrase Spengler made his own) only for that form. But that is hardly to be “in form” at all. The democratic world as we find it seems, in this sense, altogether done in. Can it come back to life, take hold, and go on? Party politics in liberal democracies today seems increasingly hollowed-out and pointless; the capitalist mode of management of capital in our time increasingly life-lacking and taskless; and the general outlook can seem utterly bleak.

The USSR, Italy, Germany…and now the USA. What could fit better a standard Spenglerian treatment than the Caesarist stamp of the presidency of Donald Trump? And some have not had to wait for Donald Trump to arrive in person to see it coming to America. Recall Richard Rorty’s extraordinary prediction in [*Achieving Our Country*](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674003125)*,* published in 1998. Rorty’s title comes from an interview with the Black writer and activist [James Baldwin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/James-Baldwin), stating his confidence that despite ongoing social problems of profound concern, the hope of “achieving our country” was still alive for him. Rorty felt that such hope was draining away and had virtually disappeared in academia in America. In these cultural conditions, if the American middle-class came to feel that *things are simply not working for them*, America will have lost the cultural ballast from public intellectuals that is required to sustain public confidence in democracy itself:

At that point something will crack. The nonsuburban electorate will decide that the system has failed and start looking around for a strongman to vote for—someone willing to assure them that, once he is elected, the smug bureaucrats, tricky lawyers, overpaid bond salesmen, and postmodernist professors will no longer be calling the shots… [And] once a strongman takes office, nobody can predict what will happen. In 1932, most of the predictions made about what would happen if Hindenburg named Hitler chancellor were wildly overoptimistic. One thing that is very likely to happens is that…all the sadism which the academic left has tried to make unacceptable to its students will come flooding back. All the resentment which badly educated Americans feel about having their manners dictated to them by college graduates will find an outlet.

Wanting to come to terms with what is happening in our time, I think we are going to have to start reading *The* *Decline of the West* again to understand better the age of Caesarism that may now overwhelm us, an age which, Spengler predicted, “will occupy the first centuries of the coming millennium.” Here we are. I’m thinking of writing a new book—*Spengler’s Revenge*.
