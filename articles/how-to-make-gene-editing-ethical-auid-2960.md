*Eugenics has a terrible history. But if we now have the power to alleviate suffering using gene editing, when does the ethical pendulum swing towards inaction being more immoral than action? Samuel Mckee argues we need to rethink where we draw the line. Advancements like CRISPR-Cas9 have made gene editing cheap, accessible and accurate. It is time to put the technology to use. Doing so could have revolutionary consequences for personalised medicine, well-being, and even consequences for human space exploration.*

Eugenics – [the controlled selective breeding of human populations to improve their genetic composition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/eugenics) – is regarded as one of the dirtiest words of the twentieth century. Forever tied to the genocides and forced sterilizations of Nazi Germany, it has been taboo to use the word in genetics gatherings, seen as the worst of its past as a scientific discipline. If you want to tarnish someone’s ideas or research in genetics, one can simply utter the word and people will distance themselves.

Francis Galton, cousin of Darwin, invented the term in an evolutionary landscape to describe proactively directing nature towards stronger and fitter ends. Why not lend nature a hand? Fast forward to the early 21st century, and we find ourselves in a new genetic landscape, where public knowledge and the technological capabilities are often miles apart. First there came the Human Genome Project, which cost over $3 billion and took over a decade. The genomics arms race saw costs plummet and technology advance at an extraordinary scale. Today one can sequence an organism’s genome in the field on a handheld device in a few hours for $500. Secondly, CRISPR-Cas9 happened. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuel Charpentier shared the Nobel Prize in 2019 for the future shaping technology that can edit the genome of any living creature.

We live in a CRISPR world now, yet the gap between public understanding of where genetic engineering is, and what scientists are truly capable of is astonishing. Since 2011, the advent of CRISPR-Cas9 has brought us into the world of cheap, accessible and accurate human gene editing. In the past few years, base and prime editing have enhanced the accuracy of editing even further, to the point that one can now change a single nucleotide. The temptation to play with human genes is stronger than ever.

But *is* gene editing actually a form of eugenics (especially when individual autonomous choice is involved)? Many would certainly disagree, and I count myself among them. Doudna has been candid about her fears, even referencing a dream about meeting Hitler in the 2019 movie *Human Nature.* But in her book *A Crack In Creation*, which chronicles the thrilling story of the discovery and development of CRISPR-Cas9, she narrates the moment that she changed her mind on the ethical question of the use of this new power. What if we have in our hands the power to alleviate pain and relieve suffering and we choose not to use it? When does it become less ethical to fail to act, rather than act?

In recent years, across the Atlantic great strides have been made in using these tools in treatment of sickle cell disease, caused by a simple mutation in a single nucleotide base. CRISPR-Cas9 is used in research on tumour cells to great effect, as well as all the favourite models of mice and fruit flies to treat anomalies. When coupled with the advances in genomics, the race to personalised medicine now firmly dwells in science fact rather than science fiction.

Personalised medicine is the dream. Many universities, such as Cambridge, offer postgraduate courses in genomic medicine – treatment tailored to the patient’s genetic makeup. In order to be most effective, the desire is that each person receives a personalised response as much as possible. The genome, epigenome and proteome must be taken into account.

*But is it eugenics?* How easy the step becomes from treating the suffering of disease, to the suffering of discomfort, and then to the suffering of wishing one was a little taller, a little stronger, or a little faster. Would life be happier if I had a different natural hair or eye colour? What about unborn children? We have prenatal screening, why not prenatal editing to save time and money in order to design the babies we want?

We could edit the genes of our pain receptors (how about engineering soldiers immune to torture?), slow down the aging process, or enhance our intelligence in various ways. Where does one draw the line, and how does one define suffering and happiness? It seems all too easy. Has the modern eugenics caught us unawares by arriving too quickly? For the past twenty years, many voices from the NIH and the White House have called the new genetics revolutions “a new era in eugenics” with the strongest possible caution being urged. If this is true even in the slightest, why do we want to engage in this practice given the past? And are we asking the right questions when it comes to what we can and should do? Surprising voices are calling for us to reopen Pandora's Box.

After He-Jiankui edited the genomes of embryos in 2018, he was fined, jailed and publicly lambasted. Now he is free and researching again in China, still “proud” of his work. Despite the global outcry of this venture in human gene editing, much of the discussion I saw and heard was of the necessity to win the race. The argument was that China was going to do human germline editing anyway, so to hell with ethics, we should get there first to get the medical and financial benefits. Ethical red lines move surprisingly quickly once money or safety are involved. Francis Collins, head of the NIH, said this was unacceptable, as did Doudna and other research heavyweights. Will we hold our nerve for long?

The futurist in me has a different answer. I have argued [elsewhere](https://theconversation.com/if-we-want-to-settle-on-other-planets-well-have-to-use-genome-editing-to-alter-human-dna-235030) that Lord Martin Rees and space exploration minds have candidly spoken of how genome editing is necessary for us to populate the Solar System. Nature’s toolbox is full of amazing opportunities, from tardigrades that can live in the vacuum of space, to microorganisms that thrive in high-radiation environments, to extremophiles that exist in places no other life can tolerate. Now we have molecular scissors that can borrow from their genomes to help our own, or those of the plants we need.

Eugenics has a terrible past. It was ideologically driven with racism at its core in a context very different from our own. It was further derailed by nationalism, fascism and a dearth of information. The world we live in now is global, and the technology to help us understand is light years beyond what it was a century ago. There are interesting discussions around rescuing animals from extinction (as well as bringing back woolly mammoths) but our own survival on the moon, mars and beyond now looks more interesting as biology catches up with physics. The ideology now is one of personalised medicine, not racism, with global health and minimising suffering at the heart of the discussion. That discussion itself is now more open, with forums on bioethics, and even postgraduate courses at Oxford University and others for the general public to study the landscape. Ensuring that a technology as powerful as CRISPR stays directed towards personalised medicine is an enormous challenge that will require strict international regulations and focused policing. The minds and institutions behind the technology are committed to this vision and political powers must marshal their support behind them.

Furthermore, someone is going to go wild with CRISPR. We need to ensure that those breaking the boundaries and assessing the landscape are the people with the best knowledge, working collaboratively across borders, with peer-reviewed and ethically guided results. Moving forwards together will lessen the opportunities for rogues. Gene editing cannot be put back into the box. Let’s ensure we move forward as safely and transparently as possible for the good of all humankind.
