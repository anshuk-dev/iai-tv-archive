# Philosophy is an erotic endeavor

*What classical philosophy can teach us about love*

**By Sam Woodward** | *1st October 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,902 words*

![Philosophy is an erotic endeavor](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/philosophy-is-an-erotic-endeavor.webp)

---

*In ancient Greece, Eros played a central role in public life. But we have since largely forsaken the loving and connective potential of Eros, particularly when it comes to philosophy, perhaps in part due to the individualism and staunch rationalism derived from the Enlightenment. Sam Woodward argues we need to reclaim the potential of Eros, so that we might learn from Plato’s erotic framework to foster relationships based upon a love for obtaining wisdom. By exploring countless avenues of knowledge and virtue, we create the potential to leave behind something greater than ourselves after we die.*

In the *Symposium*, Plato’s dialogue on Eros (Cupid for the Romans), Socrates and other guests attend a drinking-party and take turns giving speeches in praise of Eros and his power. Aristophanes, the famous comic playwright, tells a myth. “Long ago”, he says, “the natural human form was not as it is now, but different”. There were not two types of human, male or female, but three: male, female, and androgynous. We were completely round, with two faces on a single head and two sets of arms, legs, and genitals, tumbling about at great speed because of our eight limbs. We were strong and contrived to attack the gods, and so Zeus punished us by splitting us in half. For each half created, he had Apollo construct the belly and chest and turn the face towards the incision, leaving the navel as a reminder of our previous state. Each half then yearned insatiably for the half from whom they had been separated, desiring to be rejoined as one. Zeus thus moved our genitals to the fronts of our bodies so we could enjoy the relief of sexual intercourse. “So it is from that far back in time that love (*eros*) of one another has been innate in human beings”. This also explains, continues Aristophanes, why today there are males who love females and females males, but also males who love males and females who love females. We desire the sex of the other half to whom we were joined in our primordially fused state.

Upon meeting our other half, both are struck with “mutual affection” (*philia*), “love” (*eros*) and the desire to live together inseparably in an intimate relationship regardless of our partner’s sex. Indeed, our Erosis so powerful that we would, without hesitation, agree to be melted down and welded together, fused from two beings into one whole and back to our original nature. This is how Aristophanes defines Eros: “the desire and pursuit of the whole has the name of *eros*”.

Eros as the “desire and pursuit of the whole” neatly encapsulates the philosophical life. Plato’s philosopher desires to move beyond the plurality and division of the sensory world towards the wholeness of the forms. Socrates, in the *Symposium,* articulates this philosophical journey as one fueled by Eros and towards the Form of Beauty, beauty itself. The Forms are the ultimate objects of knowledge for Plato. They are universal principles corresponding to each characteristic we perceive, whether mathematical (i.e., length or height) or abstract (i.e., courage or beauty). The sensory world cannot provide us with stable knowledge as any one thing we perceive exhibits contradictory properties. To take beauty as an example, Helen of Troy is beautiful compared to all other mortals, but compared to the goddess Aphrodite, she is ugly. For Plato she can never be purely beautiful because she is at one time beautiful, at others ugly. True beauty is defined by the eternal and unchanging Form of Beauty, beauty itself, which causes all things we perceive as beautiful to become beautiful. In Plato’s most homoerotic dialogues, the *Symposium* and *Phaedrus*, the beauty of one’s beloved catalyses the journey towards higher philosophical truth, goading the soul towards the Forms. Philosophy, “love of wisdom” (*philos* + *sophia*), is an inherently erotic endeavour involving the cultivation of lover and beloved’s souls through mutual intellectual pursuit. It is a generative process, a birthing of ideas and discourse induced by the beauty of another individual’s body and – more importantly – their soul.

From Aristophanes’ myth it is clear that Eros, Greek Love *par excellence*,cannot be conveyed adequately by our word “love”. An altar to the god Eros stood in “Academy” (*Akademeia*) in Athens, the grove in which Plato established his Academy of the same name. There was also a cult established for Eros and his mother Aphrodite in Athens. Eros possesses your soul and robs you of your wits, prodding and taunting you towards his mark. His power cannot be resisted even by the other gods (just look at Zeus’ multiple love affairs). Socrates in the *Phaedrus* describes the soul so possessed as trembling, sweating, and throbbing (yes this can in Greek refer to what you think it does) at the beauty of a boy. It even grows wings! Because Eros has enthralled the soul, the lover ignores his family, neglects his own welfare, and becomes enslaved to the beloved. Eros need not be sexual or romantic (in Homer for example, one can have *eros* for food and drink), but it often is. In the words of the scholar James Davidson, it is a “particular kind of targeting energy” [1], but not an end in itself. It is therefore also translated as “desire”.

The one struck by Eros is the *erastes*, and the beloved the *eromenos* (male) or *eromene* (female). *Philia*, another Greek word often translated as “love”, is used to denote “mutual (loving) affection” between boyfriends, spouses, family members, friends, or fellow citizens. It can sometimes entail romantic or sexual attraction, sometimes not. Eroscan be a desire to establish *philia*, but, unlike *philia*, it is not typically reciprocated. It is a desire to move from one point to another, towards the target of love, a movement symbolised by the wings of Eros. The target need not even know they have an *erastes* aiming at them. The myth of Narcissus highlights the untenability of perfectly reciprocated Eros: a lover in limbo; his Eros for himself sets him on an endless self-referential erotic trajectory. You cannot establish *philia* with yourself; there is no end goal for Eros in this instance. Davidson summarises: “Eros is an ambition, *philia* is a relationship”. [2]

For Plato’s Diotima, a seer whose reported conversation with Socrates comprises Socrates’ own speech in the *Symposium*, Eros as an ambitious force means that he is not actually a god. A god has no *desire* for wisdom or anything else; a god already possesses it absolutely. Eros is not mortal either, she explains, but something in between, a *daemon* or spirit. Diotima says he is not the son of Aphrodite, but born on her birthday to Porus (“plenty”) and Penia (“poverty”). This means that he is between each state, at times prosperous in his success but at others depleted and defeated by failure to acquire what his incessant need demands. He is “never without resource at any moment, but neither is he wealthy …as a lover of wisdom (*philosophon*) he is between wisdom and ignorance”. Eros is a *philosophos* because wisdom is one of the most beautiful things. Eros is ever in pursuit of wisdom, and desires to possess it.

If this is the nature of Eros – bountiful yet barren, wise yet ignorant – then what benefits can he provide for humans? Diotima names two things. The first is presented as self-evident. Eros is defined first and foremost as the desire to possess what is good, the highest form of which is identified as *eudaimonia*: a life fulfilling to the furthest possible extent. Second, Eros is not the desire of what is beautiful per se, Diotima says, but, “of procreation and giving birth in the beautiful”. That is to say, humans desire procreation as we are mortal and naturally seek immortality as far as our nature allows i.e., by leaving something of ourselves behind after we perish. She declares that all humans are pregnant both “in body and in soul”. Those who are oriented towards the female are pregnant more in body and give birth to children. Those oriented towards the male are pregnant more in soul and give birth to virtue and wisdom. Beauty acts as a conduit for the procreation of philosophical virtue; when a man, for example, beholds the beauty of his male beloved’s body and soul, instilled within him is the impulse to teach his beloved about virtue and educate him in what it means to be a good. The “children” this philosophical coupling raises includes poetry and laws, which have brought innumerable figures fame and glory: the poets Homer and Hesiod, and lawmakers Solon (Athens) and Lycurgus (Sparta).

But Diotima is not finished here. The ultimate stage for philosophical Eros is akin to the final rites of a mystic initiation:

Perhaps even you, Socrates, could become an initiate in these mysteries of love. But when it comes to the ultimate rites and highest mysteries – these being the end goal if someone progresses correctly – I do not know whether you would be able to do this (209e-210a).

From here the lover starts to love all beautiful bodies, not just the one, and then must consider the beauty of souls far more important. This more spiritual Eros will then enable him to see the beauty in all intellectual pursuits and elicit various types of knowledge in order to widen his contemplation of beauty.

In doing so, he will happen upon “the great ocean of beauty”, producing an “unlimited love of wisdom” (210d) and all sorts of beautiful kinds of learning. Despite identifying beauty in all its forms, the philosopher’s ocean of beauty is still a plurality. He is yet to arrive at the whole, the desire and pursuit of which Aristophanes defined as Eros. The highest mysteries culminate in a sudden epiphanic revelation of beauty itself, the Form of Beauty, the principle which causes and defines all beautiful things: true beauty. It is “simple, pure, unalloyed, and not defiled by human flesh and colour and all the rest of mortal trivia” (211e). Contemplated by the soul, true divine beauty enables the philosopher to beget true virtue, and the divinity of the Form of Beauty grants the soul a share in immortality.

Plato’s account of Eros here is a timeless one. It seems that we have largely forsaken the loving and connective potential of Eros, particularly when it comes to philosophy, perhaps in part due to the individualism and staunch rationalism derived from the Enlightenment. After all, Plato arrived to Enlightenment thinkers with the homoerotic passages edited or omitted during translation in the Renaissance. Today we might learn from Plato’s erotic framework to foster relationships based upon a love for obtaining wisdom, opening up the possibility of exploring countless avenues of knowledge and virtue to leave behind something greater than ourselves after we die. Whether this be creative output disseminated to all people for all time, or a fruitful discussion in private with a person whose beauty has engendered the procreation of ideas and virtues, Eros so directed might at the very least help us to produce healthy discourse and cultivate mutual understanding with those closest to us.
