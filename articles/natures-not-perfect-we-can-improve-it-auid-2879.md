# Nature is not perfect, we can improve it

*The danger of making nature into a God*

**By Arden Rowell** | *1st July 2024* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,153 words*

![Nature is not perfect, we can improve it](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Natures-not-perfect-we-can-improve-it.webp)

---

*We too often think of nature as an absolute good. It must be kept pure, pristine, and totally distinct from humans, who are at best a polluting influence. This mindset is dangerous, argues Arden Rowell. It means we only ask how our projects will degrade the environment, not how they might improve it. Desolate regions of Outer Space, she suggests, reveal that pristineness should not be valued, and that nature, in Space and on Earth, can be improved as well as harmed by human action.*

It’s too often assumed that [humans and nature are distinct from one another](https://www.britishecologicalsociety.org/are-humans-separate-from-nature/), and that humans are [polluting](https://scholarship.law.nd.edu/law_faculty_scholarship/313/) and nature is [pure](https://www.routledge.com/Purity-and-Danger-An-Analysis-of-Concepts-of-Pollution-and-Taboo/Douglas/p/book/9780415289955). On this view, humans are a source of degradation and ruin, and the best thing that people can do for the environment is to leave it alone. Such intuitions underlie calls to [save “virgin” forests](https://wwf.panda.org/wwf_news/?278590/Virgin%2DForests%2DSaving%2Dthe%2DLast%2DRemaining%2Din%2DEurope%2DPart%2D1), preserve “pristine” ecosystems in their “natural” state, and continued controversy over the value of [rewilding](https://www.scotlandbigpicture.com/rewilding-stories/the-trouble-with-reintroductions). They manifest in the belief – nowadays carefully conveyed to schoolchildren – that human impacts on the environment [comprise a long list of (only) negative things](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/resource-library-human-impacts-environment/): pollution, burning fossil fuels, deforestation, climate change, soil erosion, poor air quality, undrinkable water. And such intuitions are codified into environmental law and policy – the landmark [U.S. Wilderness Act of 1964](https://www.fws.gov/law/wilderness-act-1964#:~:text=Here%2C%20in%20the%20Wilderness%20Act,visitor%20who%20does%20not%20remain%20%E2%80%A6), for example, epitomized this view of humanity when it created special protections for the legal category of “wilderness,” which it defines as “an area where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor and does not remain.”

While thinking of the purity of nature when “untrammeled” by ([nonindigenous](https://www.sapiens.org/culture/pristine-wilderness-conservation/)) man may seem inspiring on its face, this way of thinking about humans’ relationship with the environment generates policies that are oversimplistic – and may even be dangerous. The danger comes from this subtly enervating fact: If human impacts on the environment are always and *only* bad, then there is literally nothing humans can do to save the environment, because everything we try will only cause additional harm. So long as humans are always and only ruiners, even the [best-case scenario](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3157287) for the Earth is [bleak and demotivating](http://articles/surviving-the-cult-of-climate-doom-auid-2874): a slightly slower march towards inevitable ecological degradation and loss. Little wonder, in this world, that [solastalgia](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00998-6), [climate despair](https://www.vice.com/en/article/j5w374/climate-despair-is-making-people-give-up-on-life), and [eco-anxiety](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/02/06/health/climate-anxiety-therapy.html) have seeped so deeply into modern sensibility.

\_\_\_

An Anthropocene – an age of humans – that is beautiful rather than blasted.

\_\_\_

How can we escape this vortex of environmental self-hatred? A key first step is to recognize that while human impacts on the environment can be (and have often historically been) harmful, and while the world does indeed face [multiple ecological crises](https://unfccc.int/news/what-is-the-triple-planetary-crisis), there is no natural law that requires every human impact upon the environment to always and only be damaging. On the contrary, the possibility of environmental harm embeds at least the theoretical potential for environmental improvement: if ecosystems can degrade by going from better to worse, then logically they can “improve” by going from worse to better.

Of course, the theoretical possibility of environmental improvement does not prove that humans are capable of effecting that improvement. It can, however, lead us to the question that we need to ask to make such improvement possible: to asking how people can make the environment better rather than worse. Answering this question requires us to imagine the possibility of a human-environment relationship that is nourishing rather than toxic; a world where humans are a source of powerful positivity rather than poison. Although specifying such an account may feel uncomfortable or even silly at first, there are real pragmatic and psychological benefits in imagining [best-case scenarios](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3157287) and from embracing a positive sense of responsibility and agency. Such approaches open up the possibility of anthropogenic environmental change as improvement rather than (just) degradation; of imagining an [Anthropocene](http://articles/rejecting-the-anthropocene-is-a-mistake-auid-2806) – an age of humans – that is beautiful rather than blasted. The vision of a happy, beautiful Anthropocene may, in turn, be the motivational tonic that allows society to move beyond disabling depression and [eco-paralysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9960236/) and take action to further a [better future](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4878815).

What would it take to develop a more positive perspective on the human-environment relationship? At a basic level, such an account might focus on the ability of humans to deliberately cultivate environmental qualities they believe are good. This might include both anthropocentric qualities – such as the ability to sustain life – and biocentric or Terracentric qualities, such as enhanced biodiversity. It might also include a reconceptualization of the human power to change our environment as something that makes our relationship with Earth both special and worthy of cultivation.

In developing a more positive account of the power of humans to impact the environment, one thought exercise involves putting the role of humans in context within the larger universe. Scientists estimate that the universe is something like 96 billion light years across, even as social scientists estimate that *Homo* human ancestors first wielded fire about 2 million years ago. If we imagine the light from our first ancestral fire beaming unobstructed into space, it can by now have traveled only two thousandths of a percent (0.002%) across the universe. In one direction. By the time our sun dies in 7 or 8 billion years, the light from that first fire could have traveled only 8% of the way across the cosmos; the equivalent of setting off to traverse the Earth and failing to make it all the way across the state of Texas. Thus, while humans are certainly the proximate cause of environmental degradation on Earth and – via [space trash](https://www.weforum.org/agenda/2022/07/environmental-impact-space-debris-how-to-solve-it/), [SETI transmissions](https://www.seti.org/), and [light pollution](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abq7781), our very local space neighborhood – we will have, and have had, absolutely no impact on the vast majority of the universe. For better or worse, most of space will never experience an Anthropocene.

Is that good? Perhaps – or perhaps not. It depends upon whether “pristineness” or non-interference by humans is a legitimate and positive environmental value—and if so, whether it is a value of such weight that it should overwhelm other environmental values. I am personally unconvinced that the portions of space that exist beyond the reach of *homo sapiens* are “better” because humans can never interact with them. In fact, it seems possible to imagine situations where even “pristine” reaches of space that are “untrammeled by man” would do better with some trammeling.

[Imagine](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4346659), for example, a near future where human scientists are controlling an advanced space probe as it explores a neighboring solar system. The probe detects a previously undiscovered (“pristine”) planet – “Bios” – rich with diverse extraterrestrial life. Unfortunately, the probe also detects a large asteroid on a collision course with Bios. The scientists calculate they can deflect the asteroid by slamming into it with the full force of the probe (a maneuver that the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Administration recently [confirmed](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-confirms-dart-mission-impact-changed-asteroids-motion-in-space/) can indeed shift the trajectory of an asteroid). If the scientists deflect the asteroid, the probe will be destroyed – but Bios will be saved. If they boost in the opposite direction, the probe will escape, but Bios will be blasted into bits of lifeless rubble.

\_\_\_

An environment is not equally good regardless of its characteristics; some environmental qualities are better than others.

\_\_\_

Should the scientists use the probe to save Bios? Doing so is a form of human interference with the environment of Bios. If they choose to interfere, then like Earth, every biosphere on Bios will be causally related to humans; at least in this sense, Bios’ will have entered an Anthropocene. Would the environment on Bios be better or worse as a result?

I believe Bios’ environment is clearly better if the scientists save it from destruction. In fact, I believe this an example of how humans can clearly improve their (proximate) environments – and how failing to recognize this power can lead to premature abandonment and even to tragedy. This conclusion requires three moves, however.

The first move is to reject [environmental relativism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3197/096327117X14809634978636). An environment is not equally good regardless of its characteristics; some environmental qualities are better than others. Most modern environmentalists share this intuition and have a concept both of environmental quality (i.e., what is good) and environmental degradation (i.e., progression from better to worse). It is possible that different space environments may deserve somewhat [different ethics](https://www.duo.uio.no/bitstream/handle/10852/69331/1/Owe-Andrea-15-05-2019-Environmental-Ethics-in-Outer-Space-MAthesis-.pdf), but as a general matter, it seems reasonable to believe that life is valuable, and that preserving life is good. So long as that is right, by saving Bios, the scientists are accomplishing the opposite of degradation: ensuring the quality of Bios’ environment does not move to a worse state (lifeless bits of rubble) from a better one (supporting a rich tapestry of life).

Second, human action has the power to affect the quality of human surroundings. On Earth, this power is obvious in the impact of climate change, microplastics, forever chemicals, and the construction of cities, roads, and farmlands. For Bios, the power to preserve is built into the hypothetical: humans have the agency to affect Bios’ environment by either diverting or ignoring the meteor. Accepting this power matters, however, as it distinguishes both the distant parts of space (where humans are irrelevant) and a world where our choices and obligations are constrained by the reach of our agency. Humans are not omnipotent, but in the areas of space where we have agency, we can choose to act in ways that make the environment better or worse.

The final move is to resist the temptation to equate human action with environmental ruin. Bios is not a Victorian maiden whose purity will be despoiled by human interference. The mere involvement of humans in Bios’ business does not pollute the planet; in this case, at least, the specter of real ruin comes only if humans flee the field prematurely, leaving Bios to its cataclysmic fate. In sum, Bios’ environment is better if it is preserved from the asteroid, and humans can act to save Bios and thus to move Bios’ environment from a worse state to a better one. Humans can, therefore, make the environment better.

Although this argument has been styled in regards to a distant planet, the same arguments apply when we consider potential human impacts on the quality of Earth’s environment. Most obviously, should Earth ever be threatened by collision with a giant asteroid – an outcome that both [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-partners-conduct-fifth-asteroid-impact-exercise-release-summary/) and scholars of [global catastrophic risk](https://scienceandsociety.duke.edu/events/asteroids-planetary-defense-and-global-catastrophic-risk/) have warned against – we will do well to prevent Earth from being turned into bits of lifeless rubble. Such an action would be a straightforward example of humans improving the environment on Earth – of a happy (or at least happier) Anthropocene.

\_\_\_

It is thus neither sufficient nor wise to ask only how a project degrades the environment. We must also ask how the project improves the environment.

\_\_\_

This is, of course, an extreme example of environmental improvement, but the same reasoning applies to less explosive policy scenarios as well. Acknowledging the possibility that human impacts on the environment *can* be good involves rejecting the assumption that all human impacts on the environment are always and only bad. This rejection opens up opportunities for meaningful engagement with hard but important policy questions: Do humans improve the environment when they [reintroduce](https://www.rewildingbritain.org.uk/reintroductions-key-species/reintroductions-and-bringing-back-species) species that were [locally](https://www.unsw.edu.au/research/ecosystem/our-research/conservation-practice/reintroduction-locally-extinct-mammals-landscape-ecosystem-approach) or [totally](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2024/03/06/1235944741/resurrecting-woolly-mammoth-extinction#:~:text=Colossal%20has%20been%20working%20to,closest%20living%20relative%3A%20Asian%20elephants.) extinct, or when they engage in “[assisted migration](https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/assisted-migration-helps-animals-adapt-climate-change#:~:text=In%20July%2C%20the%20US%20Fish,otherwise%20unsuitable%20to%20live%20in),” relocating species to new and less-threatened areas? What about when they purposefully ignite [prescribed forest fires](https://www.fs.usda.gov/Internet/FSE_DOCUMENTS/fseprd624550.pdf)? Do humans improve the environment when they construct [dykes or seawalls](https://climate-adapt.eea.europa.eu/en/metadata/adaptation-options/adaptation-or-improvement-of-dikes-and-dams) to protect coastal, urban, or agricultural ecosystems from flooding? Is purposeful human interference with the atmosphere via [geoengineering](https://geoengineering.environment.harvard.edu/geoengineering) – for example, via [carbon dioxide removal](https://www.energy.gov/fecm/carbon-dioxide-removal) or reflecting the sun’s rays into space – an improvement or a degradation? Answering these questions is difficult, not least because it requires us to do the work to identify our values about what makes environments better or worse. But the difficulty flows from recognizing authentic sources of challenge – and from rejecting the simplistic assumption that humans are always bad for the environment.

Environmentalists are right to ask skeptical questions about the environmental impacts of human actions. Such questions serve a vital function in focusing faulty human attention upon the elusive goal of environmental quality. In asking such questions, however, we must be conscious that exclusive focus upon degradation – that failing to recognize the possibility of improvement – can lead to tragedy: to the premature abandonment of our most hopeful scenarios. It is thus neither sufficient nor wise to ask only how a project degrades the environment. We must also ask how the project *improves* the environment: what would make the environment better than it is now, and how does this project move us towards that better future? Such questions require ethical courage, and the resolution to select and pursue intended consequences, even as we seek to reduce unintended ones. Recognizing that we *can* improve the environment is the necessary first step in answering the harder ethical questions of what a good environment will be.
