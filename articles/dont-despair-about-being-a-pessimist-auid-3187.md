# Don’t despair about being a pessimist

*Pessimists can change the world*

**By Ian James Kidd** | *10th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,392 words*

![Don’t despair about being a pessimist](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Dont-despair-about-being-a-pessimist.webp)

---

*Modern society prizes hope and activism, treating optimism as a moral ideal and pessimism as a flaw. Drawing on Buddhist thought and contemporary philosophy, Nottingham philosopher Ian James Kidd challenges this cultural habit, inviting us to explore forms of pessimism that resist despair, without collapsing into false hope.*

A curious feature of pessimism is that there are many reasons to *be* pessimistic and yet many people condemn *being* pessimistic. It only helps a little to point out that pessimism can apply to all sorts of things. I could be pessimistic about my meeting this afternoon, my financial future, the prospects for peace in the Middle East, or the prospects for humankind surviving into the next century. This includes specific and general concerns (the meeting; humankind), short- and long-term concerns (this afternoon; the 22nd century), and the different sorts of concerns (financial; existential). In these examples, we also see different *grounds* for pessimism – bad prior experience, inferences from the historical record, sober reflection on human nature, and so on. In some cases, I can also take action, doing things to try to change the outcome for the better – maybe going into the meeting with a “can-do” attitude. In other cases, the scope for my taking any meaningful action is zero.

Our everyday talk about pessimism, then, disguises all sorts of differences and difficulties. There are three important issues to consider. First, *content*—what, exactly, are we being pessimistic about? I recently asked a friend, a self-described “uber-pessimist,” what exactly she as pessimistic about. Her answer—“Absolutely everything!”—was concise but unhelpful. All but the deepest pessimists tend to think *some* things are better and *some* things might be improved. Second, *justification*—how does one justify pessimistic claims? What kinds of reasons and evidence are at work? Past experience tells me this afternoon’s meeting will be a waste of time. Justifying my pessimism about peace in the Middle East and the future of humankind, though, are different matters. History, sociology, political theory, philosophical reflection, and a judicious geopolitical understanding would be needed to justify those claims.

The third difficulty for pessimists is a *practical* one: what are the implications of one’s pessimism for one’s life? Announcing the conviction that “[the world is fundamentally flawed](https://www.reddit.com/r/Pessimism/comments/1h56lo0/welcome_to_our_philosophical_pessimism_community/)” automatically poses the question of how to *live* in such a world. Of course, much depends on the content of one’s pessimism. Unfortunately, there is a tendency to focus on the most extreme, dramatic implications —like [advocating the extinction of humankind](https://www.vhemt.org/), or, closely related, the [anti-natalist proposal](https://academic.oup.com/book/32901) that we voluntarily cease procreating to gradually bring the human population down to zero. While these options shouldn’t be dismissed, we should emphasise other ways of being a pessimist. In a [recent book](https://www.agendapub.com/page/detail/pessimism-quietism-and-nature-as-refuge/?k=9781788217705), the pessimistic philosopher, David E. Cooper argues for a kind of “quietism”—accepting with a mood of disquiet the dreadfulness of human life, but still working to live virtuously and tranquilly within its constraints. Abandoning ambitious conceits to be able to rectify the world, seeking “refuge” in natural places, cultivating a more modest sense of the good one can do—these are cooler ways of living out a pessimistic vision of life, free of extreme and nihilistic ambitions.

The pessimistic quietism advocated by Cooper is difficult and will not free a person from their dark vision of life. Virtue is tough, tranquillity apt to be disturbed, and the refuges the quietist needs are fragile. The sense of disquiet at the heart of pessimism cannot be dispelled. The exemplars of pessimistic quietism Cooper draws on all appreciated these problems. In many ancient philosophical traditions—from Buddhism to Daoism to Epicureanism—the wise person pays a heavy price for their quietude. The Buddha, for one, warns that pursuing a virtuous life requires a person to “go forth into homelessness”—withdrawing from the “unwholesome” social world dominated by ambition and desire, adopting the disciplined bodily and mental habits of monastic life, and training oneself to see worldly life as “a cesspool,” full of temptation and corruption.

From experience, I know contemporary Buddhists react with horror at the very idea [the Buddha was pessimistic and quietist](https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iupjournals/index.php/jwp/article/download/4913/348/21677&ved=2ahUKEwiggIr2hbKNAxUWWkEAHTvJKVsQFnoECBYQAQ&usg=AOvVaw24Ferh8h7PqT6QVBxSzghe). I sympathise, since modern presentations of Buddhism relentlessly portray it in positive terms. Every popular book on Buddhism, and every book by a Buddhist, in my local bookstore speaks of “peace,” mindfulness, compassion, enlightenment, and the “overcoming of suffering.” I understand the attractions of this upbeat image, even if it shows little fidelity to the actual teachings of the Buddha. The discourses, after all, tell a [very different story](https://works.hcommons.org/records/2xzb3-n5n94), a darker one which clashes with the ethos and expectations of Buddhism’s admirers, most of whom interpret Buddhism through the lens of their prior commitments to environmental action, social equality, optimistic attitudes to political reform, and so on. In this case, the pessimism and quietism built into the Buddha’s vision of human life is quietly removed.

There are several reactions to these claims about Buddhism, pessimism, and quietism. Some simply reject the claims outright. Some remind us that “Buddhism” refers to different traditions and teachings, arguing the teachings of the Buddha are historical artefacts which ought to be interpreted and put in context. Others care less about fidelity and more about usefulness. They see what the Buddha taught as resources to be put to work in resolving the problems of the day. For them, the question of whether there are pessimistic-quietist themes in the discourses is just irrelevant. In a modern world of climate crisis, capitalist hazards, and social injustice, pessimism and quietism are the last things we need. The tradition of “[socially-engaged Buddhism](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/introduction-to-engaged-buddhism-9781350129085/)” names the attempt to transform Buddhism into a vehicle of environmental, social, and political change. Optimism not pessimism, activism not quietism.

The case of Buddhism tells us a lot about modern resistance to pessimism. In many societies, what is wanted is a positive ethos that speaks in a language of action, hope, optimism, and progress, which are presented as the attitudes definitive of a moral person. At the same time, pessimism and quietism are condemned. Being pessimistic and quietist is associated with apathy, laziness, lack of concern for others, selfishness, and other vices. Many students in my Buddhism class, for instance, automatically associate *being a good person* with *being a social activist*. If they express pessimistic attitudes, they quickly castigate themselves, as they also do when they express the modest hopes of quietism. The entrenched habit of condemning pessimism and celebrating optimism is difficult to unpick, too. Even the students who judge that the Buddha was an actual pessimist – after studying the discourses and scholarship – often reject him for that reason. “Just say NO! to pessimism” might be their slogan.

I think those of us who want to defend pessimism must deal with two different problems. First, our pessimistic convictions must be carefully articulated. Slogans about the awfulness of existence are not enough. Our pessimism needs content and justification and a set of instructions given for how to live out this vision of life. I have [elsewhere](https://blogs.cardiff.ac.uk/openfordebate/being-pluralistic-about-philosophical-pessimism-part-1/) [tried](https://blogs.cardiff.ac.uk/openfordebate/being-pluralistic-about-philosophical-pessimism-part-2/) to make clear how to do this sort of work. Second, the pessimist must reckon with the anti-pessimistic attitudes and defences I illustrated with the case of Buddhism. Granted, one can rightly condemn certain [extreme kinds of pessimism](http://iai.tv/articles/human-consciousness-a-tragic-misstep-auid-2352), like the ones encouraging suicide or violence. There are, though, other gentler, more reasonable forms of pessimism. In these cases, pessimisms are available without violence, nihilism, suicide, or abandonment of moral effort or procreation. If that sounds counterintuitive, the reason is that your definition of pessimism is too narrow, perhaps because it came from someone with an anti-pessimist agenda.

I cannot persuade you, in this short essay, to become and to live as a pessimist. I can, however, hope to make you rethink both the nature and implications of pessimism. In doing so, I follow the example of recent champions of kinds of pessimism, including the quietist variety defended by Cooper or the “[hopeful pessimism](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691265605/hopeful-pessimism?srsltid=AfmBOorvmhBI8nTUbmj89A7591sZpY4FeZIjGqxyaeBTI2Ykya-SnjGX)” of [Mara van der Lugt](http://iai.tv/articles/in-defence-of-pessimism-auid-1996). In the case of the environment, there are also recent defences of kinds of [eco-pessimism](https://philpapers.org/rec/NGUPFC) that, for its champions, does not collapse into resignation or inaction. As Roger Scruton in one of his many books insisted, there are “[uses of pessimism](https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://www.roger-scruton.com/book-shop/culture-and-politics-list/the-uses-of-pessimism-2&ved=2ahUKEwjTrvnok7SNAxXdU0EAHZj6POkQFnoECEIQAQ&usg=AOvVaw005fw_4ZYWIZM1MWsw1YHb),” and we should take them seriously.

For all the differences among the contemporary champions of pessimism, they agree that we need to take a more thoughtful look at pessimism—one more attuned to its many forms and less dogmatic about its dangers.
