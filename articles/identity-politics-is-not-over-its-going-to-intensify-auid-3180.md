# Identity politics is not over, it's going to intensify

*The ancient roots of identity politics*

**By Jean-Paul Faguet** | *30th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,376 words*

![Identity politics is not over, it's going to intensify](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-end-of-identity-politics-part-2-copy.webp)

---

*Is the dominance of identity politics over? Yesterday, [we published an article which claimed that it is.](https://iai.tv/articles/identity-politics-is-over-heres-what-will-replace-it-auid-3179) But today pioneering political scientist Jean-Paul Faguet argues that we should expect identity politics to get even more intense and dangerous. Class-based politics is giving way to identitarian politics, and the rise of a tribal, nativist populism is only accelerating this shift. This should be no surprise, he argues: politics organised around class interests which cut across ethnic boundaries was always a historical anomaly. In the broad sweep of human history, identity politics has been the norm.*

**The end of left-right politics**

The era of left-right politics, which came to dominate political systems throughout much of the world, is arguably [drawing to close](http://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-left-and-right). Long-established parties with enviable records of electoral success and governing achievement, such as [Britain’s Labour and Conservative](https://www.ft.com/content/20598c6b-3884-4f31-a874-e5a3a827cc4d) parties, Colombia’s Conservatives and Liberals, France’s Socialists, Germany’s Social Democrats, as well as traditional parties in the Netherlands, India, Venezuela, Chile and Ireland are in steep decline or are already dead. In many countries, their voters are being siphoned off by new parties that appeal across class lines to people’s ethnic, religious, racial, and sexual identities.

[In a recent article](http://iai.tv/articles/the-age-of-left-right-politics-is-over-auid-2993?_auid=2020), I traced this decline to deep economic and social changes that have undermined not just these parties as organisations, but the underlying ideological basis of a politics that pits left-wing, pro-worker parties favoring a larger government against right-wing, pro-business parties that want less government. We now work in ways that undermine the coherence of a self-identified working class, and by extension the coherence of a self-identified capitalist class. Once this opposition goes, parties organised according to such a divide no longer make sense.

In many ways, these changes mark the ultimate triumph of the Thatcher-Reagan revolution that began in the late 1970s. Both politicians led charges against the status quo by placing the individual firmly in the center of a new ideological order. People were no longer members of larger collectives, where the group mattered more than individuals because of strong common interests that could more effectively be achieved by working together. Instead, the indivisible “me” as a bundle of preferences and appetites became paramount. The goal of every citizen became to maximise her satisfaction without reference to any larger group or class.

We’re all entrepreneurs now.

\_\_\_

At the same time, efficiency drives in one industry after another raised productivity by replacing workers with machines. In the 1950s, workers lived in the same neighborhoods, [wore similar clothing](https://socialhistoryblog.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/army-of-clerks_UoNebraska.jpg), spoke in similar accents, and ate similar foods. Their work consisted of [similar tasks on assembly lines and workshops](https://www.the-american-interest.com/2017/08/30/end-working-class/) in the industrial and industrializing world’s factories. It was natural to think that they had common interests, and that these interests were in opposition to those of the factory owners. By the 1990s, North American and European factories were filling up with [automated machines and then robots](https://www.roboticsandautomationmagazine.co.uk/features/is-this-the-most-highly-automated-factory-in-the-world.html), and the legions of unskilled workers were gone. Remaining workers were far fewer and more specialised. Their sense of common identity, interests, and purpose were undermined from below by the conditions of their work, and sledgehammered from above, first by right-wing governments and then by the “New Left.” Of course, people continued to work hard, and they still do today, but no longer as members of a “working class.” We’re all entrepreneurs now.

**Back to the future: the (inevitable?) resurgence of identity politics**

What were the politics of this transformation? And where does it sit in the broader sweep of history?

The liberalising reforms of the 1970s and ’80s first [reduced market barriers within countries and then across national borders](http://iai.tv/video/the-rise-and-fall-of-globalisation), leading to what economic historians call the “third wave of globalisation.” The effect was to greatly increase the intensity of market competition. Firms had to become far more efficient to survive. The disintegration of the working class as a distinctive group was a direct result of this process. Another was a comprehensive undermining of the middle-class lifestyles and security of blue-collar workers throughout the industrialized West. Before long, a single blue-collar salary could no longer sustain a family’s middle-class lifestyle. Women went to work; people took second and third jobs. [Those who had not gone to university saw their prospects collapse](http://iai.tv/articles/the-new-class-divide-auid-1825).

It's important to acknowledge that liberalization also produced earth-shaking benefits. China’s rise [pulled some 800 million people out of poverty](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/04/01/lifting-800-million-people-out-of-poverty-new-report-looks-at-lessons-from-china-s-experience#:~:text=BEIJING%2C%20April%201%2C%202022—,million%20over%20the%20same%20period.); South Korea and Taiwan were utterly transformed.

In the West, right-wing parties now faced an existential dilemma. How could they cobble together electoral majorities when their policies had immiserated large numbers of voters? Their solution was strategically brilliant: divide the electoral pie in a different way. Replace pro-business appeals to liberalization and the low-tax, low-service state in favor of cultural issues that identified them with “the nation” or the “true people”: [“Frenchness” in France](https://foreignpolicy.com/2017/02/16/marine-le-pen-is-donald-trump-without-the-crazy-front-national/), [nativism in Germany](https://theconversation.com/what-is-the-afd-germanys-far-right-party-explained-250218), and so on. Style yourself as defender of the true nation and denounce the Left as protectors of foreigners, deviants and “others,” and then trounce them at the polls. Astonishingly, center-left parties in many countries fell for this trap, replacing class with narrower appeals to multiple minorities in an attempt to build a lasting coalition of women, racial and religious minorities, LGBTQ+, and others. [Some predicted a shift to permanent Democratic domination](https://www.thenation.com/article/politics/judis-teixeira-class-democratic-majority/) in the US, and for a few years it seemed to be happening. But then the wheels fell off the cart.

**Revenge of the Paleolithic brain**

This is a sad and dangerous turn for our societies, but it is neither new nor surprising. The left-right politics that arose first in Europe in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries is, in the broader sweep of history, an aberration. Anatomically modern humans emerged in Africa some 200,000 years ago. During the vast majority of our history since then, identity, not economic class, has most likely been the defining characteristic of our societies, and hence our politics. At the dawn of written history 2,500 years ago, [Herodotus delighted in describing the dress, food and habits of the exotic peoples and kingdoms he chronicled](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2707/2707-h/2707-h.htm). Compared to our world today, [few humans lived in this ancient world](https://ourworldindata.org/population-growth-over-time). Many groups lived in isolation, and the social characteristics they developed were often strikingly different. Herodotus understood the societies he described as defined by these identities, and implies that they thought of themselves that way too: “We wear togas, they wear trousers; they worship many gods, we worship one.”

\_\_\_

The idea that social identity might be based on class interests that cross regional, ethnic, and even national boundaries is a deeply Marxist invention of the modern age.

\_\_\_

Much closer to our own age, [Max Weber characterised patrimonial societies](https://archive.org/details/weber_max_1864_1920_politics_as_a_vocation/page/n1/mode/2up) of the pre-modern era as defined not by class interests tied to productive structure, but rather by tribal and clan identities, or other sorts of in- and out-groups that jockeyed for power. Such groups were typically rooted in religion, ethnicity, or kinship, and relied heavily on leaders’ charisma for their coherence. They were not defined by their relationship to the means of production. Class-based ideology has dominated the politics of the last two centuries, but the broader sweep of history suggests that most people naturally think of themselves in terms of race, religion, language, and clan.

Indeed, the idea that social identity might be based on class interests that cross regional, ethnic, and even national boundaries is a deeply Marxist invention of the modern age. Such a possibility would never—probably *could* never—have occurred to the hunter-gatherers and agriculturalists of previous millennia. If our politics descends all the way into a struggle between identitarian tribes, it will be a shame and may become a tragedy. But it will not be an aberration. It will mark a return to our roots as humans.
