# The man who mistook reality for a fantasy

*The feeling the world is real evolved and can be lost*

**By Gabriele Ferretti** | *25th April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,271 words*

![The man who mistook reality for a fantasy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-man-who-mistook-reality-for-a-fantasy.webp)

*What if the world didn’t feel real to you – and what if it never had? Italian philosopher Gabriele Ferretti argues that our deepest beliefs about reality, knowledge and science depend not on logic or reason alone, but on a fragile, evolved mental state: the feeling that the world is real and independent of our minds. People with derealization disorder lose this sense altogether, and their condition reveals just how contingent our sense of reality really is. It’s time for an evolutionary psychiatry of philosophy and science, which confronts the psychological foundations of our most basic assumptions about the world.*

“[Now he begins to wonder if also the World, indeed the World, is a Thing that does not exist](https://www.adelphi.it/libro/9788845911521).” If I had to choose a phrase that sums up the entire work of the philosopher, this elegant sentence by the Italian writer Giorgio Manganelli would be my first choice.

Humans have an inborn philosophical attitude. They ask what exists and what does not exist, what is real and what is not. And these questions quickly lead to a further question, about how we can know what exists and what is real. Welcome to [epistemology](http://iai.tv/iai-academy/courses/info?course=knowledge-and-rationality), a field of study that is based on *epistéme*, which means “to stand up for oneself,” in the sense of “being grounded,” referring to a certain stability of our knowledge of the world outside.

\_\_\_

There is only one thing we take for granted more than the fact that the world outside is real. And that is that the world outside feels real.

\_\_\_

But where does this stability come from? Human beings, phenomenologists teach us, have a natural attitude to consider [external reality as an objective, mind-independent world](http://iai.tv/video/a-field-guide-to-reality) that exists in its own right. It is this alleged objective reality that we perceive, we know, and that we do philosophy, science and metaphysics about. If you’re currently holding a device which allows you to read this article, you’ll be aware of the massive impact that the material world seems to have on us. It feels so powerful that most of the time we take for granted that there is a reality that we discover through our senses, and in a deeper way through science and philosophy. We analyze this reality’s architecture through physics, we count its objects through ontology, and we explain its ultimate nature through metaphysics.

Furthermore, as biological, embodied subjects with brains, we also *feel* this reality as being an outside entity. Indeed, there is only one thing we take for granted more than the fact that the world outside *is* real. And that is that the world outside *feels* real. The alleged fact that the world outside *is* real and the fact that it *feels* real to us, human beings, are clearly two different facts. There could be a real, mind-independent world without any biological system that feels it exists. And there could be a feeling that something is out there without anything actually being out there (as in a hallucination).

For most of us, however, the sense of reality is so strong that it seems to come directly from reality itself: it apparently comes from our strong connection to the stability of the world outside. We take for granted that the world feels real precisely because, the natural attitude goes, it is real. And this would suggest that everything that *is* real *feels* real to us: reality feels real to creatures like us.

\_\_\_

In so-called Derealization Disorder, people no longer feel the reality and the mind-independence of the world outside (a kind of reverse hallucination, we may say).

\_\_\_

But what if I were to tell you that the world contains cognitive creatures who may lack the sense that this world is real? What are these creatures? Well, they are some of us: in some humans, the sense of an external reality is broken. In so-called Derealization Disorder, people no longer feel the reality and the mind-independence of the world outside (a kind of reverse hallucination, we may say).

What is it like to experience derealization? The American Psychiatric Association’s manual, [*DSM-5*](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm), describes it as involving “a feeling of unreality or detachment from, or unfamiliarity with, the world, be it individuals, inanimate objects, or all surroundings. The individual may feel as if he or she were in a fog, dream, or bubble, or as if there were a veil or a glass wall between the individual and world around.”

Direct reports capture the phenomenology of derealization: “[It becomes difficult to tell what is real and what is only my imagination playing tricks on me](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6624990/)” and “[I am not living in a world at all](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2182424/).” According to Shaun Gallagher, derealization “can be analyzed as a [disruption of transcendental intersubjectivity](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-self-and-its-disorders-9780198873068?cc=gb&lang=en&), to the point that real things may no longer feel real or familiar, or as fully objective as they should. A significant privation of intersubjectivity, accordingly, may lead to an erosion of the sense of reality” (I’ve offered an analysis of derealization in [Ferretti 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-024-04808-4)).

Most of us humans do not suffer from derealization, and so have the sense that this world outside is real. We can therefore remain philosophically relaxed: science, ontology, metaphysics and all these human enterprises are anchored to external reality. But biology teaches us that this is only one way things could have gone – and we can speculate about the evolutionary advantages of our condition. What if, as humans, we had not been given this sense that the world outside was real? What if we’d been hardwired to be derealized by default; so that this was not the exception, but the rule? What if we, as a species, had no sense of the stability of this world, so that it felt weak, unstable, distorted, disconnected?

Of course, this raises the question of whether such a species is not only evolutionarily, but also conceptually possible. Derealization involves experiencing the world as unreal. But to experience the world as unreal, don’t we first need to experience it as real? To be able to appreciate the experience of *un*reality, you first need to undergo (or at least to understand) the experience of reality. There is a passage, in derealization, from an experience of reality to a lack of this experience. Derealization is not congenital: derealized patients experience a transition, from reality to unreality. The derealized person therefore has an awareness that there exists a sense of reality even when it is lost – this is how they are aware that something has been lost.

But if all of us were born without having the experience of reality as real, what would give us the idea of reality? And without any idea of reality, how could we even be aware of lacking the experience of reality, of *detachment* from reality? In order to feel detached from reality, we first need to have an experience of connection to reality. So you may wonder about the coherence of the whole idea of derealized experience being the default, in a possible evolutionary outcome.

\_\_\_

This may sound paradoxical: the more that the world is felt to be stable and outside of our minds, the more we feel connected to it; whereas when a derealized person feels the world to be a projection of their own mind, they feel disconnected or detached from it.

\_\_\_

However, there is a coherent idea here. The sense of reality is a mental state. We know that we can have a human without this mental state: derealized subjects lack it. And we can imagine a humankind in general without this mental state from birth. Of course, if they were derealized from birth, then they would lack any notion of reality, and in turn would lack awareness that they lacked an experience of reality. But – and this is the key point – their lack of the experience of the world as mind-independent or external would dramatically impact on how they conceive of their science and metaphysics. Presumably, it would mean saying goodbye to the experientially-laden bias in favor of scientific and metaphysical realism. These subjects may not even be interested in science at all. There would be nothing that felt real, external, out there (and they wouldn’t even know that there could be such a “reality”!).

The phenomenon of derealization teaches us that the sense of *mind-independence*, the sense of *stability*, and the sense of *connection* are deeply related. The feeling of detachment from external reality comes from the world failing to manifest concreteness, or a stability that would lead us to appreciate that it is something not depending on our mind. Indeed, losing the sense of stability leads us to feel detached from the world in its independent existence, so that this very same world feels like a strange representation of the mind, rather than something stable, whose stillness – whose being out there – can be appreciated. This may sound paradoxical: the more that the world is felt to be stable and outside of our minds, the more we feel connected to it; whereas when a derealized person feels the world to be a projection of their own mind, they feel disconnected or detached from it.

Now, imagine a science, an ontology, a metaphysics, even an epistemology, generated by a derealized human society. It would no longer be our natural attitude to experience what we have before us as something to call real. Would we then be so eager to find the Shakespearean “stuff” of reality? Would we be, as Nabokov puts it in Lolita, so “adorably keen to explore rich reality”?

This thought experiment on derealization suggests a valuable thing. Our conception of the external world is massively determined by the way we are. In particular, there is no objectivity, and [no idea of objectivity](http://iai.tv/articles/reality-and-objective-truth-diverge-auid-2868), without a *sense* or *experience* or *feeling* of objectivity. Without this experience, we would probably not even have chosen the notion of *epistéme* to characterize stable knowledge of the external world, because the world of the derealized has nothing stable in it.

Think about it. Each philosophical position can be associated with a particular psychopathology: one can simply open the *DSM-5* to determine each of these associations. Do you think the world does not exist? In the philosophy class, you may be classified as an idealist. In the psychiatric domain, however, you may have a psychosis. We may be reminded here of Wittgenstein writing, in [*On Certainty*](https://philpapers.org/rec/WITOC): “I am sitting with a philosopher in the garden; he says again and again ‘I know that that’s a tree’, pointing to a tree that is near us. Someone else arrives and hears this, and I tell him: ‘This fellow isn’t insane. We are only doing philosophy’.”

Science, epistemology and metaphysics, as they actually exist in our world, tend to be made by what we conceive of as psychologically healthy subjects. But this is only one way things could have gone. If most of the population were derealized, that would be the status of a psychologically healthy subject. And science, epistemology and metaphysics would arguably be radically different.

Here is the bottom line. We need to cultivate an awareness of the psychiatric domain of the positions we take in science, epistemology and metaphysics. There is already a metaphysics of science, a sociology of science, a feminist perspective on science. And there is an epistemology of psychiatry as well. Now it’s time for an evolutionary psychiatry of science, epistemology and metaphysics. That is, we need a field of study capable of considering scientific, epistemological and metaphysical positions, with respect to our evolutionary history, and in relation to what is considered a “normal” or a “healthy” point of view on or way to experience the world.

\_\_\_

We tend to overestimate the possibility of a priori knowledge – knowledge not based on experience.

\_\_\_

The way we do metaphysics, epistemology and science depends on the way we (or rather, most of us) process external reality. Reality would be different for us if we were different. And so would metaphysics, science, epistemology. Ignoring this can lead to deep philosophical myopia.

What exactly would an evolutionary psychiatry of philosophy tell us about metaphysics, science and epistemology?

One crucial message it would offer is that we tend to overestimate the possibility of *[a priori](https://academic.oup.com/book/3347)* knowledge – knowledge not based on experience. The way we do metaphysics, science and epistemology is related to the way we experience the world. One might have thought that knowledge of certain very basic structural features of reality is *a priori*; but it turns out that our confidence in reality itself depends on our experience of the world *as* real, and this experience is itself contingent because it is a result of how we evolved as neurobiological systems. Experience has a transcendental dimension: it is based on our evolved biological structures subserving knowledge states. Our metaphysics, science and epistemology depend on our evolutionary hardwiring, as much as they rely on the way the world is made up.

We’re familiar with the idea that we only know what’s in front of us because we experience it as being in front of us: experience delivers us knowledge of the world. What this article has emphasized is that there is another layer to this experience, which we don’t usually consider. We don’t only experience a world in front of us; we also experience it *as* real – *as* external, mind-independent, stable and objective.

In this actual world, we (most of us) don’t “derealize.” Philosophers, scientists and poets are generally not suffering from derealization. And so our poetry, our engineering, our science, our metaphysics, our epistemology, our ontology are taken to be related to an external, mind-independent world. But we should not ignore the evolutionary standpoint from which we appreciate “*our*” reality; we should not ignore how contingent our sense of reality itself is.
