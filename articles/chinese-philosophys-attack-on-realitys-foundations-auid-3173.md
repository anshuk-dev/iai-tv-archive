# Chinese philosophy's attack on reality's foundations

*The kaleidoscopic metaphysics of Chinese Huayan philosophy*

**By Nicholaos Jones** | *20th May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,094 words*

![Chinese philosophy's attack on reality's foundations](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/chinese-philosophys-attack.webp)

---

*From Plato to particle physics, the search for reality’s ultimate building blocks has long been seen in the West as promising to reveal life’s deepest truths. But this approach has run into all sorts of trouble. Chinese Huayan philosophers see this search for one fixed fundamental ground of reality as a mistake. Philosopher of science Nicholaos Jones here uses the Huayan idea of “partial truth” to argue that there are multiple ways to conceive of reality’s foundations, each of which illuminates a different aspect of a complete truth which no single mind can ever fully grasp. Instead, by learning to move freely between these different partial truths, we can take pleasure in their kaleidoscopic variety.*

**1. The structure of reality and the meaning of life**

Plato imagined ordinary objects as [combinations of air, earth, fire and water](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0180%3Atext%3DTim.%3Asection%3D53c), and he imagined these four elements as combinations of right-angled triangles. Colossians 1:16–17 declares that [all things are created through, depend upon, and aim toward God](https://biblehub.com/colossians/1-16.htm). Spinoza argued that everything other than God is a [mode of God](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spinoza-modal/#Mode). Sabine Hossenfelder, in [*Existential Physics*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/616868/existential-physics-by-sabine-hossenfelder/), maintains that “one of the best established facts about nature” is that “things are made of smaller things, and if you know what the small things do, then you can tell what the large things do.” All of these proposals concern reality’s [fundamental structure](https://doi.org/10.5840/tpm201674103)—relations whereby some items of reality are more fundamental than others. For Plato, triangles are more fundamental than everything else. For Spinoza and the author of Colossians, God is more fundamental. For Hossenfelder, small things—the [elementary particles of physics](http://iai.tv/articles/we-need-new-physics-not-new-particles-auid-2611)—are more fundamental than larger ones.

People care to know about reality’s fundamental structure, in part, because they desire some sense of where and how they fit into the grand scheme of things. A life made through and for God, for example, has a different sort of meaning than a life made through particle interactions and for nothing in particular. It’s no accident, I think, that just as Spinoza’s investigation of reality’s fundamental structure culminates by demonstrating the superiority and meaningfulness of living in accordance with reason, Hossenfelder’s concludes with an existentialist recommendation to find your own meaning.

One tempting thought about reality’s fundamental structure is that it’s *fixed* or *determinate*, so that if one item of reality is more fundamental than another, then any proposal to the contrary—any proposal which reverses the direction of fundamentality—must be incorrect. Denying this seems to entail that reality is somehow inconsistent, structured so that something is simultaneously more and less fundamental than something else. Denying it also seems to [defeat the desire to find life’s meaning](http://iai.tv/video/nihilism-and-the-meaning-of-life-nolen-gertz). After all, if lifeless particles are more fundamental than anything else, and yet at the same time Colossians is correct that God is more fundamental than elementary particles, it’s unclear whether our place in the grand scheme of things is due to divine creation or mindless physics.

**2. Buddhism from India to China, and the idea that reality’s structure is not fixed**

Despite its allure, there’s some reason to resist the thought that reality’s fundamental structure is fixed. The reason derives from the Chinese Buddhist monk [Fazang](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buddhism-huayan/#Faza643712) (643–712), who strove to reconcile diverse philosophical elaborations of Buddhist teachings without dismissing alternative elaborations as heretical. His strategy for reconciliation involved identifying some partial truth in each philosophical tradition, and then explaining their departures from a more complete truth as their skillful ways of making Buddhist teachings accessible and comprehensible for different audiences. This may seem like just a strategy for interpretating texts and teachings. But Fazang’s metaphysics—in particular his account of reality’s structure—derives directly from this hermeneutic strategy. He identified items in reality which are fixed across different perspectives, and then argued that relations of fundamentality among these items must be variable or indeterminate.

Fazang’s hermeneutic strategy can be seen in his reconciliation of apparently contradictory elaborations of the Buddha’s doctrine of dependent arising (something closely related to the [doctrine of no-self](http://iai.tv/articles/why-there-is-no-self-a-buddhist-perspective-for-the-west-auid-1044)). Many early Buddhist texts present the Buddha as teaching that all things arise in dependence upon other things. [Sarvāstivāda](https://oxfordre.com/religion/display/10.1093/acrefore/9780199340378.001.0001/acrefore-9780199340378-e-682) traditions of Buddhism offer a twofold elaboration: first, that composite wholes are nothing more than their constituent parts and thereby depend upon those parts for their existence; and second, that while there are partless items of reality—[*dharmas*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abhidharma/#AbhExeDhaDha)—with [natures](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abhidharma/#IntNatBetCatOnt) that do not depend upon other things, the present activity of each *dharma* depends upon other *dharmas* in the way that the growth of seeds depends upon moist soil. But Yogācāra traditions of Buddhism offer an alternative elaboration. For [some Yogācārins](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/yogacara/#CittDoctPrioMind), all things, whether composite or not (except, perhaps, for mind), depend for their existence upon the conceptualizing mind (in Sanskrit, [*ālaya-vijñāna*](https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/display/document/obo-9780195393521/obo-9780195393521-0003.xml)) and are thereby empty of any independent existence.

Sarvāstivādins and Yogācārins therefore seem to have competing proposals about reality’s fundamental structure—the former proposing that *dharmas* are more fundamental than all else, the latter proposing that the conceptualizing mind is more fundamental than *dharmas*. But Fazang sees harmony rather than conflict. For Fazang, Sarvāstivādins are correct to reduce wholes to their parts, and conceptualizing *dharmas* as independent existents is merely a skillful accommodation for an audience which is attached to a notion of reality as being independent of minds. At the same time, Yogācārins are correct to hold that all things are empty of independent existence, and the idea that they are dependent on a conceptualizing mind is merely a skillful accommodation for a different audience, which is attached to the idea that reality is dependent on minds, but is ignorant of the pure and inexpressible nature of mind. Hence, by Fazang’s lights, there is no genuine conflict between Sarvāstivādin and Yogācārin elaborations of the Buddha’s teaching about dependent arising, and there is no basis for rejecting either tradition as heretical.

Metaphysical theories are partial truths mixed with skillful means for presenting these partial truths to audiences who have different attachments.

\_\_\_

Fazang’s point is not that Sarvāstivāda proposals about reality’s fundamental structure are true for Sarvāstivādins, while Yogācāra proposals are true for Yogācārins. He does not relativize truths about fundamental structure to different audiences. Instead, both express non-relative partial truths. That is, for Fazang, Sarvāstivādin and Yogācārin metaphysical theories are partial truths mixed with skillful means for presenting these partial truths to audiences who have different attachments—one to the idea that reality is mind-independent, the other to the idea that reality is mind-dependent. Neither fully discerns reality’s fundamental structure. The reason, for Fazang, is that correct discernment requires the absence of all [attachments](https://shambhalatimes.org/2024/09/17/non-attachement-in-buddhism-exploring-the-buddhist-teachings-on-attachment/), and so the attachments that drive Sarvāstivāda and Yogācāra metaphysics mean that Sarvāstivādins and Yogācārins fail to fully discern reality’s fundamental structure.

The relation between attachment and discernment, and the principle that attachment impedes correct discernment, is central to Fazang’s approach to metaphysical inquiry. Attachments are not mere beliefs. For example, bodhisattvas—enlightened persons who help others achieve enlightenment—lack attachments, but they rely upon beliefs about others to tailor their advice and assistance in ways that help others overcome their own attachments. For Fazang, and for many other Buddhists, attachments are beliefs that attend to some aspect of reality while ignoring some other aspect. They are beliefs that, while partially true, imagine reality to be some way that it is not. A standard example is attachment to permanence. When a lump of clay changes into a pot, the clay seems to persist through the change—and this persistence explains why the cause of the pot is the lump of clay rather than, say, an acorn. But for those who, like Fazang, endorse the Buddhist teaching that all things are impermanent, believing that clay persists through the change from lump to pot involves attachment to permeance. The belief is partially true. The lump of clay changes. But the belief, by virtue of ignoring the impermanence of all things, imagines that there is something about the lump, some persisting substance, that is immune to change. Because attachment-laden beliefs imagine reality to be some way it is not, attachments impede correct discernment.

For Fazang, because correct discernment requires the absence of all attachments, the only way to correctly discern reality’s fundamental structure is to avoid being attached to any notion of reality. And the only way to avoid being attached to any notion of reality’s structure is to allow that its fundamental structure varies with perspective and is indeterminate rather than fixed and determinate. This is because to conceptualize fundamental structure as fixed across perspectives is to be attached to a certain idea of its structure.

\_\_\_

The key to being unattached is to avoid fully committing oneself to any one particular description of reality.

\_\_\_

Why think that conceptualizing fundamental structure as fixed across perspectives is a source of attachment? One reason has to do with a motivation lying behind Fazang’s hermeneutic strategy for reconciling apparent conflict. Chinese Buddhists inherit much of the foundation for their metaphysical theorizing from Indian traditions of Buddhism. But they receive this inheritance through piecemeal translation of Indian scriptures and commentaries. Because the translators do not always clearly convey that different texts come from competing traditions, the inheritance does not always seem to fit together well. But the Chinese monks are committed to the scriptures and commentaries being true—all forming one thread. This is why they interpret different texts as partial truths intended for different audiences, with different attachments. And they view resistance to this interpretive strategy as a mark of attachment: the basis for rejecting some of the texts and teachings as false is accepting some alternative as *completely* true, and this kind of confrontation-inducing full acceptance has its basis in attachment to some subset of the inherited texts and teachings. So the key to being unattached is to avoid fully committing oneself to any one particular description of reality.

Fazang applies similar reasoning to the metaphysics of fundamental structure. There are many proposals about reality’s fundamental structure, and these proposals do not always seem to fit well together. Fazang is disinclined to prioritize exactly one proposal as fully correct. It is possible to interpret different proposals about fundamental structure as partial truths for [different explanatory purposes](https://iai.tv/articles/there-is-no-foundation-to-reality-auid-2938). Just as interpreting different texts and teachings as partial truths leads Chinese monks to the view that no text or teaching is completely true, Fazang infers that no proposal about fundamental structure is completely true—and that the quest to articulate some one true and complete description of reality’s fundamental structure has its basis in attachment. It follows that correctly discerning reality’s fundamental structure requires conceptualizing it as variable across explanatory perspectives. This, at any rate, is roughly the conclusion Fazang reaches in his effort to discern reality’s fundamental structure without attachment.

**3. Beyond attachment**

Contemporary metaphysicians tend to speak of *the* fundamental structure of reality, presuming that, if there is a fundamental structure, it is fixed across perspectives. They then ask questions like whether or not [reality has a foundation](http://iai.tv/articles/reality-has-no-ultimate-building-blocks-tuomas-tahko-auid-2465), whether or not some items of reality [are more fundamental than all others](http://iai.tv/articles/reality-has-no-fundamental-level-auid-2715). But this issue is downstream from the issue of whether fundamental structure is fixed. If it’s *not*, then even if, say, elementary particles are more fundamental than anything else from one perspective, proposals to the contrary—whereby, for example, [the *cosmos* is more fundamental than its particles](http://iai.tv/articles/russells-mistake-that-changed-philosophy-auid-2830)—can be correct from alternative perspectives.

Of course, if contrary proposals about fundamental structure can be correct, the point of inquiring about fundamental structure—and the force of abandoning one proposal in favor of another—is not clear. Contemporary metaphysicians typically motivate their inquiry in a desire to answer compelling explanation-seeking questions, and they typically frame the force of abandoning one proposal in favor of another as part of a general quest for truth. This is plausible if fundamental structure is mind-independent, less so if it’s projected onto reality by us.

In the latter case, thinking about fundamental structure as variable across perspectives, or even just thinking about Fazang’s approach to fundamental structure, offers a way to answer certain questions about reality without succumbing to attachments that inhibit correct discernment. If Fazang’s view about fundamental structure is correct, endorsing his view also facilitates the relinquishing of attachment. His view thereby yields a kind of hyper-existentialist recommendation for the quest to figure out how we fit into the grand scheme of things. Don’t just find your own meaning. Find many meanings. Figure out their virtues and limitations so you can shift among them in different explanatory contexts. Then embrace people with different views about reality’s structure as guides to alternative meanings, and let go of the impulse to confront them as mistaken.
