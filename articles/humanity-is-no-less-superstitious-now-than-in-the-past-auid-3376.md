# Humanity is no less superstitious now than in the past

*The Big Bang theory, broken mirrors, and other superstitions*

**By Rami Gabriel** | *2nd October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,581 words*

![Humanity is no less superstitious now than in the past](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Humanity-is-no-less-superstitious-now-than-in-the-past.webp)

---

*We tend to believe that the human race has become increasingly rational over time; that the superstitions of the past – such as the Ancient Greek superstition that important decisions should be made based on the direction birds fly in the sky or alternatively by reading the entrails of a sacred bull – are no longer believed in. But Rami Gabriel, author of [A Suspicious Science: The Uses of Psychology](https://www.audiobooks.com/audiobook/suspicious-science-the-uses-of-psychology/679988), argues we have modern superstitions of our own. We all have beliefs that we hold on insufficient evidence, that other or future cultures will almost certainly call superstitious. Belief in theories such as the Big Bang theory may even be among them.*

Belief is our best tool to assuage doubt. It provides a seemingly solid foundation for our actions. Now, *what* we believe *in* could be anything; from free trade to aliens, from the causes of Alzheimer’s disease to the inherent grace (or evil) of human nature. In this we are an incredibly flexible species. Nevertheless, in the grand economy of possible beliefs, we consider some to be more rational, say the belief in Newton’s laws, than others. When a belief is outlandish, that is to say when it is not in accord with the consensus view or is only perceived as rational for a small group of people that does not include us, we call it a superstition.

As we live through these turbulent times, it has become apparent that many of the beliefs we held to be rational have been rendered obsolete. Even those eminently rational repositories of facts called scientific disciplines are questioning basic premises once considered beyond doubt. Take [the Big Bang](https://iai.tv/video/beyond-the-big-bang-bjorn-ekeberg), of [which a new book by Niayesh Afshordi and Phil Halper](https://iai.tv/articles/the-big-bang-cant-escape-its-religious-beginnings-auid-3171) claims, “When a theory becomes so widely accepted that it is immune to question, we’ve moved from science supported by evidence to belief upheld by faith.” Yes, indeed, it has recently come to light that there is no way to explain or even find evidence to support the idea that *the universe “began” as a singularity.* Does this shift the theory of the Big Bang from the abode of rational belief to the realm of superstition?

Let’s consider the nature of beliefs we hold about matters that are outside of our realm of expertise. If you are not a physicist, then what exactly does an abiding, though often vague and peremptory belief in the Big Bang consist of? Well, at the most basic level, belief in scientific principles that we comprehend lightly functions as a form of meaning-making. [The Big Bang offers us a narrative](https://iai.tv/articles/the-big-bang-is-not-the-only-game-in-town-auid-3052) to frame answers to some mysteries of the universe; it is the best theory the experts have to account for the origin of the universe. The majority of people will not grapple with the question of its veracity and what, if anything, might suffice as an alternative to replace it.

\_\_\_

It is because our beliefs are grounded in the worlds we have constructed and that we inhabit, whereas the beliefs of others are grounded in worlds to which we have little to no access.

\_\_\_

When is belief in a theory that far outstrips our capacities to understand, say about the possibility that cosmic inflation is the origin of the universe, go beyond science? In short, what is it that makes a belief seem to us like more of a superstition than a fact?

Things like time, space, and complexity elude us, and thus we create symbolic orders to capture them. All beliefs, all facts in which we believe, are attempts to humanize phenomena that transcend our comprehension; they make nature *ours*. That was the great insight of structural anthropologists from Franz Boas to Marshall Sahlins. But what makes the order that I grew up with, say the Big Bang, rather than the cycle of creation and destruction of Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva, a belief rather than a superstition? According to [David Hume](https://iai.tv/articles/kant-vs-hume-can-we-access-reality-auid-3125), superstitious behavior is brought about by fear, weakness, ignorance, and melancholy. But that can’t be the whole story, it doesn’t account for why we take the ancient Romans or Siberian shamans to be superstitious. The way we distinguish between the validity of beliefs is not only psychological, but crucially dependent on cultural norms.

In the words of Rom Harré, belief is a *grounded disposition* confined to people with certain institutions and practices that support and codify that belief. Our beliefs are interlaced with the institutions that we are educated into and through. To grow up from infancy is, as Clifford Geertz and Jerome Bruner would have it, to be inaugurated into a manner of constructing the world that you will live in. This follows from the apparently natural human need to seek, make, and maintain meaning.

That is the key to understanding why natives perceive cultures other than their own to be built on superstitions and vagaries. It is because our beliefs are grounded in the worlds we have constructed and that we inhabit, whereas the beliefs of others are grounded in worlds to which we have little to no access. This is most dramatically demonstrated when we look at the past.

\_\_\_

Yes, if history is any indicator, our own most cherished and deeply felt beliefs will one day be considered superstitious.

\_\_\_

Herodotus of Halicarnassus, in his Histories of 430 B. C., provides a delightful compendium of observations, explanations, and geographies of cultures adjacent to Ancient Greece. Like Marco Polo’s travels in the 13th Century, it is through the description of other cultures that readers experience a sense of awe at the vast diversity of modes of life. While some descriptions reveal admirable individuals and societies, the overwhelming majority of scenes to which the reader is exposed evince a sense of alienation, of coming upon a group of strangers who lived and died in a world vastly unlike their own. The fact that we are reading about them makes it possible to feel that our perspective empowers us to legitimately assess the validity of the way they lived. Nowhere is this more evident than in our appraisal of the beliefs held in *other* cultures. They strike us as alternatively strange, obscure, pitiable, execrable, ridiculous, and vertiginous. In a word, the ancients strike us as superstitious.

Anthropology here is a helpful tool to consider our predicament of being convinced by the validity of our own beliefs – which will surely in the future be adjudged by those to come as woefully inadequate. Yes, if history is any indicator, our own most cherished and deeply felt beliefs will one day be considered superstitious. But right now, rationally held beliefs, in things like the Big Bang, are common sense. Common sense, of course is constituted by the collective systems that we inhabit. It has been said that because man is a social being, his institutions have to be imbued with psychological significance. Through the engendering of meaning and salience, we externalize aspects of ourselves into the world so that the world can be known, controlled, and codified as a part of ourselves. We humanize nature by finding words, systems, models, commandments, edicts, and laws that can encompass it while simultaneously providing tools for us to make cognitive pronouncements.

As a way of taking stock of the ideas I have presented, consider that what makes a particular belief a superstition or a fact depends on where you are standing relative to it. In 425 BC Athens, men made important decisions by consulting the direction that birds flew in the sky or alternatively by reading the entrails of a sacred bull. In the 14th century, Ibn Battuta recounts the immense, god-like, control that rulers such as Muhammad bin Tughluq were privileged to hold. In 2020, for some months many people were convinced a pangolin was the source of a disease that threatened to scourge the world of our species. And now, this year, some physicists suggest [we reconsider the theory of the Big Bang](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MdIV57k2ILM), that in fact it may have been a superstition that obscured the true origin of the universe.

\_\_\_

The strangeness of superstition is the portal through which we see our own uncanniness, which, rather than negating our endeavors, confirms us in the legacy of man, the believing animal.

\_\_\_

So how do we deal with the churning caused by the situated nature of all subjectivity and knowledge? One way is to adopt ethnocentrism, the position that our own beliefs are primary and that all other beliefs are parochial, misleading, or flat out wrong. We know what that looks like; the violent adoption of cultural, racial, national, and religious supremacy, and the thousand ills this cruel propensity has spawned in colonial and fascist administrations.

What I would like to suggest here is that rather than denigrating a system of belief by conceiving of it as alien and thus superstitious, we can consider how the range of possible beliefs offers the opportunity to appreciate the diversity of thought and life. The anthropologist Clifford Geertz conceives of observing other cultures as a prospect from which we can *make ourselves visible* by representing ourselves and others as similarly thrown into a world of irremovable strangeness. The strangeness of superstition is the portal through which we see our own uncanniness, which, rather than negating our endeavors, confirms us in the legacy of man, the believing animal.

As in Herodotus, we are enriched by stretching our imaginations to empathize and contemplate the diversity of beliefs, not as superstitions, but as opportunities to appreciate our multiplicity. These are the many splendors of [the miracle of mind](https://iai.tv/video/the-miracle-of-mind-consciousness), the Big Bang of belief.
