# How to maintain your hope in humanity

*I'm not angry at you humanity, I'm just disappointed*

**By Ian James Kidd** | *22nd January 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,803 words*

![How to maintain your hope in humanity](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/dont-give-up-on-humanity-3.webp)

---

*Many are critical of humanity's failings, and some even argue it would be better if we did not exist. Philosopher of misanthropy Ian James Kidd offers an exposition of stances in philosophy that offer sharp critiques of humanity’s moral failings. In a time of uncertainty, environmental harm, animal cruelty, and social injustice, Kidd explores various ways of dealing with humanity’s malevolence without losing hope.*

**Condemnation.**

Humankind is, according to many critics, in a dreadful moral condition. Environmentalists connect our greed, short-termism, and rapaciousness to the ‘climate crisis’, despoilation of environments and the destructiveness that motivates worried talk of ‘the end of nature’. Animal activists decry the ‘crime of stupefying proportions’ perpetrate against animals. Factory farms, laboratories, hunting, and other human practices kill or displace billions of animals. Cruelty, indifference, cold willingness to exploit creatures ‘at our mercy’, and the reductive perception of animals as resources – as ‘meat on legs’, say – are just some of the vices manifest in the practices and institutions of ‘carnist’ societies.

Social critics attack the injustice, inequality, and corruption of political and economic arrangements. An inspection of the social world reveals malice, short-termism and stupidity sustaining systems that brutalise and immiserate millions of people daily. Existentialists such as Sartre, de Beauvoir, and Marcel lament the inauthenticity and ‘bad faith’ of those of us – most of us – who surrender to the imperatives of mass society. Most of us are guilty of an array of existential failings – evasions of responsibility, self-serving narratives, conceits, and what Iris Murdoch called the ‘self-aggrandising and consoling wishes and dreams’ we create to distract us from reality.

\_\_\_

Arrogance and dogmatism, indifference to truth as an ideal, and intellectual laziness – and also ‘vices of the heart’ such as cold-heartedness, emotional rigidity ... such vices seem to be permanent features of human life.

\_\_\_

For those without such specific concerns, one can also point to the many ‘ordinary vices’ – as Michel de Montaigne called them – displayed in our everyday talk and behaviour. Many are familiar moral failings – such as the contemptuousness, envy, jealousy, hypocrisy, and selfishness that distort our relations with others. Or the self-righteousness and sense of self-superiority that flows from moral zealotry. There are also ‘vices of the mind’ – such as arrogance and dogmatism, indifference to truth as an ideal, and intellectual laziness – and also ‘vices of the heart’ such as cold-heartedness, emotional rigidity, and failures of compassionate attention to other people and creatures. Such vices seem to be permanent features of human life. Two and a half millennia ago, the Buddha described many vices and ‘defilements’ – greed, self-delusion, covetousness, moral laziness, lasciviousness, to name but a few. Reading his lists, it is depressing to realise none of them has disappeared from human life. In fact, most of them seem to be flourishing.

**Being a misanthrope.**

If you share this sense of humankind as suffused with vices and failings, then you are well on the way to becoming a *misanthrope*. A dictionary will tell you that a misanthrope is ‘a hater of humankind’, someone who feels contempt, disgust or hatred for human beings and the human species. The title character of Moliere’s play *La misanthrope* declares that he ‘hates all men’ for the evil they do or the evils they condone. [Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/misanthropy/?rdt=59759)’s threads for misanthrope are full of statements of hatefulness or scorn for human beings. Other misanthropes impugn us as a ‘cancer’ or ‘plague’. [David Benatar](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/better-never-to-have-been-9780199549269) – who defends the ‘anti-natalist’ idea that we should cease procreating to bring a non-violent end to humankind – justifies this in part by our being ‘the most destructive and harmful species on earth’.

\_\_\_

Philosophical misanthropy is therefore a verdict, a judgment informed by experience, reflection, thought and deliberation.

\_\_\_

If misanthropy means hatred and disgust leading to extreme rhetoric or policies, then you may despair of the idea of becoming a misanthrope. Fortunately this is *not* a good account of misanthropy. First, hatred and its related emotions don’t spring from nowhere. They will be related to our experiences and reactions to situations – anger *at* someone letting us down, disgust *at* an awful betrayal and so on. In the case of misanthropy, the ‘hot’ emotions, such as hatred and contempt, are reactions to the profusion of vices and failings one sees in everyday behaviour and social arrangements and institutions. As philosophers argue, misanthropy is a critical judgment, a verdict, on the human condition, as it has come to be. In his book *Animals and Misanthropy*, [David E. Cooper](https://www.routledge.com/Animals-and-Misanthropy/Cooper/p/book/9781138295940?srsltid=AfmBOoqX93fseIvCes1pa7DKmlH53KnU1DNppNXlXhddZFa93J8psOna) describes several clusters of failings then documents in depressing detail how they are displayed in our ways of treating and thinking about animals. Like any judgment a misanthropic one might be expressed in emotions, but they are two different things. Misanthropes, indeed, do report anger, disappointment, regret, sadness, and other emotions—there are hateful misanthropes, for sure, but also quiescent misanthropes, like Cooper.

**Failings and judgment.**

Philosophical misanthropy is therefore a verdict, a judgment informed by experience, reflection, thought and deliberation. It can express itself a range of moods or emotions. Hatred is optional and many misanthropes will resist it. Immanuel Kant had misanthropic attitudes but his moral commitments to respect for human beings protected him against hatred of them. In fact, the target of the misanthropic judgment is not individual humans: it is something more abstract and collective – humanity, human civilisation, human ‘forms of life’. For sure, the world contains lots of cruel and greedy people, but they are not the main target.

Eco-misanthropes locate greed, exploitativeness and destructiveness that are integral to our ‘carnist’ food cultures and consumerist ways of life. Feminist philosophers such as [Kate Manne](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/313936/down-girl-by-manne-kate/9780141990729) distinguish the sexist attitude and behaviours of individuals and the misogyny of social institutions and systems. Of course, misanthropes can condemn specific individuals – some of whom are striking symbols of our collective failings. More happily, other individuals are loved and admired by the misanthrope as exemplars of virtues so absent or lacking in the wider human world. Jonathan Swift, for instance, could ‘heartily love’ some individuals, while condemning ‘that animal called man’. If I condemn the callousness and selfishness of our world, that explains why I admire those who are caring and selfless.

\_\_\_

The failings of humankind are not superficial, like surface-level stains that could be removed easily. They are inseparable from what we have come to be.

\_\_\_

I’ve mentioned different vices and failings so far, and three comments on them are needed. First, there is no definitive list of the vices and failings of humankind – I have a list somewhere of about five hundred. Greed, cruelty, and dogmatism appear in the lists of most of the misanthropes past and present. Others include specific vices reflecting their values and concerns – impiety and false righteousness, racial discrimination, insensitivity to beauty, philistinism, vulgarity, mindlessness, lack of respect for tradition … It is instructive to compare and contrast the charge lists of different misanthropes to see which varieties of human badness we are missing. The second comment is that the misanthrope sees our vices as entrenched – deeply built into the structure of human life.

The failings of humankind are not superficial, like surface-level stains that could be removed easily. They are inseparable from what we have come to be: imagine how utterly different our world would be without, say, warlikeness or abuses of power or shoulder-shrugging indifference to the vulnerable. The third point is related – our vices and failings are ubiquitous, infecting almost all areas of human life. Some misanthropes seek places of refuge – environments relatively uncontaminated by acquisitiveness, nastiness, and self-absorption. The talk of greed and other vices as ‘diseases’ or a ‘raging fire’ (the Buddha’s favourite image) vividly communicate a sense of how many vices are both destructive and prone to spread unless checked.

**Being a misanthrope.**

Suppose you are becoming a misanthrope. What, then? How do you live as a misanthrope? Or – as one of my students put it – ‘how do you *do* misanthropy?’ The question is a good one because misanthropy is an existential doctrine. It has real implications for the ways we think and feel – our social relationships, ways of understanding the world, and it can disturb our moral and political convictions. Becoming misanthropic also requires us to change our stance towards life. One can’t go on as one did before. The Buddha’s ‘Noble Truths’, for instance, are fully understood only when one feels, thinks, and sees the world in new ways. *Conversion* will for most people be a good description of the process of becoming a misanthrope.

Looking at the historical record, there are several *misanthropic stances* – distinguishable ways of living out a misanthropic vision of the human world. I already mentioned one – the violence-disposed sort which Kant labelled ‘the Enemy of Mankind’. They call for, or may perform, violence to disrupt or destroy the forms of life that manifest our vices. Think of the eco-misanthropes who call for an ‘unmaking of civilization’ or the nihilist hope that a comet or pandemic will destroy humankind. A second misanthropic stance, also named by Kant, are the ‘Fugitives from Mankind’. Fearful of the corrupting risks of continuing immersion in the human world, they escape – to isolated communities, monasteries, or life ‘off the grid’, like Scotland’s famous ‘Hermit of Trieg’. Other Fugitives, unable to fully commit to escape, rest content with periods of escape.

If escape seems too arduous or egoistic, there are varieties of Activist misanthropes. Confronted with the entrenched vices of our form of life, their response will be to roll up their sleeves and attempt the hard work of collective moral transformation. Activist strategies to achieve substantive, lasting improvement are diverse – moral teaching, religious preaching, economic reform, social engineering, judicious application of science and technology. Their ranks include ‘techno-optimists’ and ‘philanthroptimists’ – even if these tend toward radical ends, like transhumanists ambitions to ‘upgrade’ us and usher into a world of viceless ‘post-humans’.

If radical reform seems too much or too doubtful, there are also kinds of misanthropic Quietism. Without hopes for serious reform, and rejecting violence and the isolation of escape, the Quietists seek to accommodate, adapt, and cope with the imperfect reality of humanity. Modest moral efforts at a local level, cultivating virtue, and avoiding what Zhuangzi called ‘entanglements’ in the more corrupting areas of life – these are aspects of misanthropic Quietism. It has ancient precedent – such as Buddhism and Epicureanism – even if it is out of key with the ambitious activist ethos of mainstream moral discourse today.

There are, then, many ways to be a misanthrope, only some of them requiring hateful violence. There is also ample motivation for critical misanthropic verdicts on our moral condition and performance—whether our focus is our mistreatment of animals, the natural world, or other humans. A misanthrope does not welcome the verdict but accept it and find ways to incorporate it into their life.
