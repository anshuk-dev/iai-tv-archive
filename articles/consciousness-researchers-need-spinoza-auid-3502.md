# Consciousness researchers need Spinoza

*Consciousness everywhere all at once*

**By Jordi Galiano Landeira** | *20th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,656 words*

![Consciousness researchers need Spinoza](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Spinoza-3.webp)

*The mind-matter problem still haunts us to this day. The question of how and why consciousness and the physical world interact and influence one another remains a mystery. In response to this problem, the philosopher Spinoza argued that nature is constituted by something more fundamental than either the mental or the physical: something that contains both. Philosopher **Jordi Galiano-Landeira** here argues that Spinoza’s philosophy is panpsychist – matter contains mind – and that integrated-information theory can help solve some of the remaining problems with panpsychism.*

In 2023, the Oscar-winning film *Everything Everywhere All At Once* became a worldwide sensation. Its surreal humour and roller-coaster pace, from slow and mundane at the beginning to frantic chaos, complemented an original sci-fi storyline. The protagonist, Evelyn Quan Wang, transforms from a woman trapped in experiencing a monotonous, bureaucratically suffocating life to one who experiences the vast [multiverse of possibilities](https://iai.tv/articles/the-mistake-at-the-heart-of-the-multiverse-auid-3014) she could have lived.

I remember watching this film on a sweltering summer night by the Mediterranean coast, feeling both amused and overwhelmed by the sheer intensity of events unfolding “everything, everywhere, all at once.” This overwhelm was neither entirely good nor bad. The protagonist seemed to feel the same: full-blown existential overload. *Spoiler alert:* by the end of the film, she realizes that, despite her ability to inhabit infinite possibilities (with an infinitude of skills and superpowers), the most meaningful choice was to stay in one and [fully *experience* it](https://iai.tv/video/the-dance-of-life).

This brings us to our topic of discussion. Do you recall the scene where Evelyn finds herself in a universe where she is a rock? Did she not still seem to feel something in that state? Well, imagine a world where every single thing, from the tiniest speck of dust to the vast expanse of multiverses, exhibits some degree of consciousness, some form of subjective experience. While this might sound like a fantasy, it is actually a theory: [panpsychism](https://iai.tv/articles/what-physicists-get-wrong-about-consciousness-auid-1954).

At first glance, panpsychism may seem like a stretch. Why do we need to posit that a rock is conscious? However, panpsychism tries to solve one of the most perplexing problems in neuroscience, psychology, and philosophy of mind: Why are we conscious, and how does subjective experience arise? This is what David Chalmers famously called the “hard problem of consciousness” in 1995. Years later, Chalmers himself suggested that solving it might require a radically unorthodox approach, even pointing towards panpsychism.

If consciousness is a fundamental property of reality, then it has always existed, overcoming the problem of its origin. However, we do not know yet why and how we are conscious. A specific variant of panpsychism, named [micropsychism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/panpsychism/#MicrVersCosm), proposes that complex forms of consciousness, such as those experienced by humans, are constituted by micro-elements of consciousness. The idea is that these micro-elements combine to form more complex structures, leading to macroconsciousness. This would explain how and why different entities, say, humans and rocks, possess different levels of consciousness (though, according to the film, perhaps not).  If this were the case, have we solved the hard problem? Not quite.

\_\_\_

Unfortunately, we cannot call for an unspecified combination of unknown microelements of consciousness and expect that everybody will be happy. Indeed, the main problem of this proposal is the “[combination problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/panpsychism/#CombProb),” originally posed by William James a century before the hard problem of consciousness came up. The challenge is simple: How on Earth or, probably better, Universe, the combination of microelements creates a unified, coherent macroconscious composition. What we need is a mechanistic proposal, something that can both identify subjective experience and measure its combination.

This is where [Integrated Information Theory](https://iep.utm.edu/integrated-information-theory-of-consciousness/) (IIT) comes into play. [Proposed by Giulio Tononi in 2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2202-5-42), IIT aims to unveil both qualitative and quantitative dimensions of consciousness. IIT posits that consciousness is the capacity of a system to “integrate information,” also known as Φ (phi). Before diving deeper into how IIT could provide a mechanistic framework for panpsychism, we must first grasp the concepts of “information” and “integration” in this consciousness-related context, as they are cornerstones in IIT.

The term “information” refers to a reduction in uncertainty within a system. This seems quite abstract, but it can be understood through an example. Imagine living in two different countries with clearly distinct weather: Spain and the Netherlands. In Spain, the weather is consistently predictable, mostly sunny. If a weather forecast predicts sunshine for tomorrow, this *information* will not significantly reduce our uncertainty because we already expected a sunny, warm day to spend visiting the Costa Brava. In [mathematician and forefather of information theory](https://www.itsoc.org/about/shannon) Claude Shannon’s terms, this means the information content in this scenario is low since the probability of sunshine was already high.

Now, consider the Netherlands, where the weather is quite unpredictable, with an equal chance of being sunny, rainy, cloudy, or *everything*. If a weather forecast accurately predicts sunshine for tomorrow, not only does this bring joy to the people, but this information will also substantially reduce our uncertainty. In this case, the information content is high because the probability of sunshine was low, meaning the reduction in uncertainty was more significant. As you can foresee, and will be further discussed below, the information content is context- or system-dependent, as each scenario has a different information content but depends on the probability distribution of the weather events.

Returning to the conscious experience, and borrowing from the film (spoiler alert again!), when Evelyn gains access to all possible versions of herself, she becomes lost in an overwhelming flood of possibilities. Eventually, she chooses the most meaningful one: staying with her daughter. The analogy here is that when she realizes she could have lived in an infinite number of lives, the act of choosing one makes that experience more meaningful. In IIT terms, this corresponds to a terrific reduction of uncertainty, enhancing the conscious experience.

At this point, you might be wondering that, for this to happen, we need all the information coming over together. And this is the key for the concept of “integration,” which refers to the way different elements of a system interact to form a unified whole. Consciousness, according to IIT, arises when information cannot be broken down into independent subsystems without losing its essence. This is intuitive when reflecting on our experiences. For instance, consider a summer day on a Mediterranean beach. You cannot fully experience it by splitting it into distinct components like the heat of the sun, the chilly sea breeze, the sand’s BBQ-like burning touch, or the laughter of children running around and playing as if there was no tomorrow. Conscious experience is holistic and needs to be *integrated*.

However, we still need to find a way to naturalize this information integration in the physical world to make the whole story matter. Where can we find this information that is being integrated in the natural physical world? IIT, besides its deep and conceptual mathematical formalization, also offers a theoretical framework built on axioms and corresponding postulates. Echoing Descartes’ “*Cogito, ergo sum,*” IIT starts affirming that conscious experience is an immediate and irrefutable given. From this, IIT argues that:

(1) Conscious experience exists for itself, similar to Sartre’s concept of being-for-itself, and, as such, the conscious system must have cause-effect power.

(2) Conscious experience is specific and irreducible, meaning that an experience is always about something in particular, and it cannot be broken down into independent parts without losing its meaning. For instance, you are reading this work and not watching *Everything Everywhere All at Once*. In fact, you might decide to (re)watch it later, but at this moment your conscious experience is specifically about these words, not that movie.

(3) Conscious experience is spatio-temporally situated. For instance, my experience of watching the film was even more hectic because it was shaped by the circumstances: an open-air cinema within the walls of a medieval castle on a warm summer Mediterranean night.

Even though we have abstracted away, and we have explained what IIT is in general terms, Tononi and colleagues applied IIT to consciousness features and brain anatomy. IIT explains why the cerebral cortex is important for consciousness, while the cerebellum is not. IIT predicts reduced information integration in states of diminished consciousness, confirmed in dreamless sleep, seizures, and in disorders of consciousness. IIT even offers an evolutionary perspective, suggesting that organisms with higher levels of information integration had an adaptive advantage.

Thus far, we have developed a panpsychist framework that defines conscious experiences as a fundamental feature of reality. In addition, the micropsychist variant posits that complex conscious experiences are a composite of micro-elements of consciousness. We have seen how IIT provides a computational and neuroscientific framework for these ideas. The starting point for IIT is conscious experience itself as an immediate, irrefutable, and irreducible given. IIT postulates that this conscious experience can be defined as information integration in physical causal-effect structures. However, something is still missing. Why on Earth, or Universe, or *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, could we identify this conscious experience with a physical causal-effect structure? What we need now is a metaphysical framework that ontologically links both mental and physical dimensions but that keeps a panpsychist framework at its foundations.

Spinoza believed that Nature, as the fundamental substance, exhibits infinite “attributes” which express its essence. However, as humans, we can perceive only two: thought (mental dimension) and extension (physical dimension).

\_\_\_

This is a thorny question and, to address it, we need to travel back in time (if only *Everything Everywhere All at Once* could grant us this superpower) to the Netherlands, four centuries ago. It was the Dutch Golden Age, and [Benedictus de Spinoza](https://iai.tv/articles/spinoza-reborn-yitzhak-melamed-auid-2476) (1632-1677) was living between the commercial hecticness of a globalizing Amsterdam and the political blossoming of The Hague.

Spinoza is broadly known for his works on ethics and political freedom, which probably stemmed from his theological and metaphysical conflicts with the Jewish community at the time. Spinoza defended a pantheistic theological metaphysics which posited that there is only one fundamental substance, which he identified as, quoting his famous words, “*Deus sive Natura,”* i.e., God or Nature. This substance, which we will call from now on God or Nature indistinctively, is “in itself” or, in other words, self-caused. According to Spinoza, Nature is self-contained, understood as it is *everything* and can be found *everywhere, all at once*.

But how can this substance be both mental and physical without any type of emergence or either property being epiphenomenal? Spinoza believed that Nature, as the fundamental substance, exhibits infinite “attributes” which express its essence. However, as humans, we can perceive only two: thought (mental dimension) and extension (physical dimension).  These two attributes are then expressed through modes, which are particular manifestations of the fundamental substance and adhere to the ontology of their respective attributes. For instance, our body is a modal expression of the extension attribute, while our mind is a modal expression of the thought attribute. The fact that both modes arise from the same underlying substance rules out epiphenomenalism.

\_\_\_

This duality is crucial for understanding the relationship between conscious experience and the causal-effect structure in IIT.

\_\_\_

This leads to Spinoza’s parallelism doctrine: the mental and physical realms are causally isomorphic because they both derive from the same fundamental substance. This doctrine bears a striking resemblance to panpsychism, suggesting that all things possess some degree of mentality, albeit in proportion to their physical complexity. Spinoza related complexity to power in both realms. For instance, the higher the complexity of a physical entity, the higher its power of acting. Its associated mental entity would also have higher levels of perception. Conscious experience, as a specific mode of the mental realm, is likewise ascribed to complexity. Specifically, Spinoza associated power and complexity with consciousness.

Do you remember the rock in *Everything Everywhere All at Once*? Spinoza would not agree that the rock is as conscious as a human being, as it seemed to be in the film. However, Spinoza would admit some minimal degree of mentality, though very low, since its physical structure is not particularly complex compared to human beings and other living organisms.

While Spinoza argued for a gradation of complexity, he did not provide any approximation of how to calculate this complexity. We only know that the higher the complexity, the higher the mentality aspect associated with a physical entity. However, we all might agree that this complexity statement aligns perfectly with the information integration measure: the higher the information integration measure, the higher the levels of conscious experience. Spinoza also argued that complexity corresponds to causal power, which is precisely what information integration is all about. In this sense, IIT offers a framework that might help address some of the key challenges in Spinoza’s panpsychism. What, then, can Spinoza’s panpsychism offer back to IIT?

As we previously discussed, IIT lacks a metaphysical explanation connecting both conscious experiences, understood as causal-effect structures, with physical entities. Spinoza’s metaphysics provides this connection through his parallelism doctrine and a fundamental substance from which those modes are expressed. Nevertheless, we still need a way to implement this connection within IIT itself. We must identify a concept related to the causal-effect structure that has two distinct yet interconnected dimensions.

To borrow a familiar phrase, *information is power*. The concept of information, which is a cornerstone for IIT, exhibits two interesting dimensions that are relevant to our endeavour. We have already explored the information-theoretic perspective developed by Claude Shannon, where information is defined as a reduction of uncertainty in a system. Another relevant dimension, particularly interesting for physical entities, is the thermodynamic perspective, in which information is synonymous with difference, order, or pattern. As you may have guessed, these two dimensions are linked through the concept of entropy, which measures disorder or chaos within the system. In a highly chaotic system, no information can be conveyed in a message (i.e., in the information-theoretic sense), nor can any structural pattern emerge (i.e., in the thermodynamic sense).

Additionally, entropy is directly related to a system’s capacity to perform work. The higher the entropy, the lower the working capacity of the system. Paraphrasing [Gregory Bateson’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gregory-Bateson) famous quote that describes information as “a difference that makes a difference,” we can see that information is connected to work through entropy. And this capacity to make a difference is precisely what defines the causal-effect structure implied in IIT. In this way, we have shown that two distinct dimensions of information, which could be *attributed* to two differently expressed modes of the fundamental substance, are linked, similar to Spinoza’s attributes of thought and extension.

To wrap things up, we have shown that panpsychism claims mentality and, by extension, consciousness, is a fundamental aspect of reality. According to micropsychist variants, macroconscious experiences of human beings are constituted through the combination of micro-elements of consciousness. IIT offered a panpsychist-friendly framework with a mathematical formulation that explains how this macroconsciousness might be composed through information integration.

Spinoza comes in handy here, offering a panpsychist and pantheistic metaphysics in which there exists only one fundamental substance, perceived through two attributes: thought and extension. This duality is crucial for understanding the relationship between conscious experience and the causal-effect structure in IIT. The link between them might be provided by two dimensions of information: thermodynamics and information theory. Both dimensions describe causal work through a reduction of chaos, uncertainty, and entropy. Consequently, this analysis suggests that if the two attributes, thought and extension, are forms of information integration from two distinct dimensions, then the fundamental substance can be an almighty notion of information.

Finally, returning to our starting point in *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, we see that the fundamental aspect of the protagonist’s reality was information. She could have been anyone or anything else, but to be someone, she had to exist as one integrated entity, not a Mediterranean Sea of possibilities. Otherwise, she would not have been able to be “a difference that makes a difference,” which, ultimately, was her final goal for both her life and her loved ones.
