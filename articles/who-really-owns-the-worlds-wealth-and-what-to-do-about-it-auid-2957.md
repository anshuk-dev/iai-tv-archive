# Who really owns the world, and what to do about it

*The three companies that own everything*

**By Albina Gibadullina** | *27th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,151 words*

![Who really owns the world, and what to do about it](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/Who-controls-the-worlds-worth.webp)

---

*Asset management firms invest in everything from the stock market to housing, and as a result they now oversee over 26% of global private wealth. The 'Big Three' firms now manage over $23 trillion in assets - a number close to the GDP of the US. But, argues A.G. (writing under a pseudonym while she investigates the firms), these companies' size and influence encourages anti-competitive behaviour and prioritises short-term profits over broader economic health. This has led to wage stagnation and increased wealth concentration among the rich. We need alternative approaches. A democratically controlled public asset management firm could help us to achieve more equitable and sustainable prosperity.*

Our lives have become increasingly intertwined with global financial markets. If you have a retirement plan or a personal investment account, chances are your savings are being managed by asset management companies. By managing investments on our behalf, asset management firms not only command the flow of global capital, but they also exert control over the companies where we work, own the houses and apartments where we live, administer infrastructure services on which we all rely, and influence our countries’ monetary and climate change policies. Despite promising to make investing more accessible, this new financial regime has failed to bring about a more equitable and democratic ownership of capital, with an ever smaller group owning increasingly more of our societies. As asset management firms continue to prioritise short-term capital growth above all else, we are left to deal with the fallout from their investment behaviour, including stagnating wages, a cost of living crisis, crumbling infrastructure, and a burning planet.

While the level of asset managers’ involvement varies greatly depending on asset type and the fund’s investment approach, asset managers have acquired an unparalleled ability to directly impact the lives of millions of people, all while operating largely out of the public eye with little regulatory oversight. The asset management industry oversees [$119 trillion worth of assets globally](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/ai-next-wave-of-transformation), accounting for [26% of global private wealth](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/family-office-uhnw/reports/global-wealth-report-2023/exploring.html), spanning investments in the equity of publicly traded and privately held firms, corporate and sovereign bonds, real estate, infrastructure, and commodities. The United States alone accounts for over [half of these holdings](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/industries/financial-services/asset-management/publications/asset-and-wealth-management-revolution-2023.html).

The asset management industry is highly concentrated. The three largest firms (BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street), frequently referred to as the ‘[Big Three](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-and-politics/article/hidden-power-of-the-big-three-passive-index-funds-reconcentration-of-corporate-ownership-and-new-financial-risk/30AD689509AAD62F5B677E916C28C4B6)’, own [17% of the US stock market](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0308518X231195890) and manage assets worth [$23 trillion](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2024-05-02/big-three-index-funds-need-to-be-passive-not-political) (almost the size of the GDP of the United States). [The Big Three are largely passive investors, with a significant share of their investments held in index funds, which track various stock market indices such as the S&P 500](http://articles/how-the-free-market-is-failing-jose-miguel-ahumada-auid-2679). Because of their passive investment approach, they have become [universal owners](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03085147.2020.1781417#d1e303), holding investments in almost all US publicly traded firms and being the largest shareholder in [88% of the S&P 500 firms](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-and-politics/article/hidden-power-of-the-big-three-passive-index-funds-reconcentration-of-corporate-ownership-and-new-financial-risk/30AD689509AAD62F5B677E916C28C4B6). Outside of the United States, they hold investments in [20% of non-US listed firms and own 4% of non-US equity](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0308518X231195890).

The ascent of asset management firms as the largest shareholders of publicly traded companies is a relatively new phenomenon. In the post-World War II era, 95% of US corporate equities were directly owned by households (see Figure 1). With the rise of private pension funds throughout the 1970s and the 1980s, the share of stocks directly held by US households began to rapidly drop.

The [pension fund capitalism](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/pension-fund-capitalism-9780199240487) of the 1980s, however, was soon replaced by the [asset manager capitalism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/american-political-economy/asset-manager-capitalism-as-a-corporate-governance-regime/3AD6C80F112EBDCA77DE228E408F3F49) of the 2000s, following changes to the Employment Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, which mandated for pension funds to [follow the prescriptions of modern portfolio theory](https://www.jstor.org/stable/26266307). As a result of these regulatory changes, pension funds were encouraged to outsource investment decisions to external asset managers, creating new layers of financial intermediation. As of 2020, 27% of the US corporate equities market was directly owned by various mutual funds and only 10% was directly held by pension funds.

The Big Three were able to achieve such unprecedented dominance in the US investment landscape largely because of their passive investment approach. The [economies of scale](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016718519302520) present in digital platforms enable the Big Three to offer much lower fees compared to their competitors. Figure 2 visualizes the average expense ratios of US investment funds since 1995, showing that the Big Three annually charge 0.11% as a share of assets under management, while other asset managers charge 0.19% for their index funds and 0.80% for their actively managed funds.

In 1990, the top 0.1% of Americans owned 13.5% of US corporate equities and mutual fund shares. Three decades later in 2020, the top 0.1% owned 23%.

Given that [index funds are able to offer similar rates of returns compared to active funds but charge much lower fees](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.27.2.97), a significant share of capital has moved from actively managed to passively managed funds during the past 20 years. There has also been a significant decrease in expense ratios for both actively managed funds and index funds since the 1990s due to the technological advancements that further reduced operating costs and competitive pressures in the industry.

Advocates of the [passive investing revolution](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/622819/trillions-by-robin-wigglesworth/) have argued that index funds with their low expense ratios have cut down the exorbitant compensation of various financial intermediaries by allowing the everyday saver to keep most of the capital gains accrued. At the same time, technological developments, such as the [creation of ETFs](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13563467.2016.1094045) (Exchange-Traded Fund), have made it easier for the average person to invest. According to survey data from the Federal Reserve, 58% of US families owned stocks (directly or indirectly) in 2022, which is a notable increase from just 32% in 1989.  Increases in stock market participation occurred across all wealth percentiles (see Figure 3). Nonetheless, Figure 3 also shows that huge disparities continue to exist between households with different levels of wealth. For instance, 96% of Americans in the top 10% of wealth distribution own stocks compared to only 24% of households in the bottom 25%. The participation gap between the rich and the poor has thus remained approximately the same.

Despite what has been promised by the proponents of financial liberalization, a greater participation in the stock market amongst ordinary Americans did not result in a more equal distribution of resources. In fact, as investment participation rates increased, the ownership of US stocks has grown more unequal over time (see Figure 4). In 1990, the top 0.1% of Americans owned 13.5% of US corporate equities and mutual fund shares. Three decades later in 2020, the top 0.1% owned 23%. Altogether, between 1990 and 2020, the top 1% increased its ownership share of stocks and mutual fund shares from 41% to 51%. [The increase in the ownership of capital at the very top came at the expense of the bottom 90% of US households](http://iai.tv/articles/all-debt-is-not-created-equal-auid-2799), who owned only 12% of US stocks in 2020, all while the bottom 50% of Americans owned a mere 0.8%.

Asset manager capitalism does not appear to get us closer to fulfilling Margaret Thatcher’s promise of shareholder democracy. This is because the rise of asset management firms has had quite a contradictory impact on income and wealth-based inequality. On the one hand, asset management firms offer many low-cost investment options, which could enable everyday households to benefit from the growth in the stock market and other asset classes. On the other hand, however, [the rise of asset managers can be partly attributed to the dissolution of defined benefit pensions,](http://iai.tv/articles/how-to-avoid-working-forever-auid-2900) which offloaded the financial pressure to save onto workers.

\_\_\_

Emerging evidence from the US airline and banking industries, for instance, suggests that common ownership can lead to anti-competitive behavior, including increases in airline ticket prices and banking fees.

\_\_\_

At the same time, asset management firms appear to [favour short-termist corporate practices](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03085147.2020.1781417#d1e135), such as stock buybacks, which have been linked to [wage decreases](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1024529420934641). The rise of index funds has also led to significant [common ownership](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3768263), a phenomenon where competing companies are being [owned by the same set of shareholders](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-129/horizontal-shareholding/). Emerging evidence from the US airline and banking industries, for instance, suggests that common ownership can lead to anti-competitive behavior, including increases in [airline ticket prices](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12698) and [banking fees](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2710252). The increasing ownership by asset managers of infrastructure in sectors such as housing and energy has also increases instances of [predatory pricing](https://www.versobooks.com/en-gb/products/2985-our-lives-in-their-portfolios-why-asset-managers-own-the-world). Thus, asset manager capitalism, while increasing public access to better savings, might also be contributing to wage stagnation and inflation.

At the crux of this contradiction lies the [asset management business model](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/american-political-economy/asset-manager-capitalism-as-a-corporate-governance-regime/3AD6C80F112EBDCA77DE228E408F3F49). With their main source of revenue being management fees, asset managers earn more income when the value of “assets under management” (AUM) grows. There are two main ways to increase a firm’s AUM: getting more people to commit their savings with you or increasing the value of one’s existing assets. Thus, lowering their fees when compared to competitors is a way for investment firms to attract more clients. The second approach entails many tried and true practices of shareholder value maximization, like downsizing, wage cuts, and price hikes, all things which exacerbate income and wealth-based inequality. This contradiction is also felt by the [asset management industry itself](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/articles/more-boomers-prepared-retirement-but-gaps-persist.html) since supporting practices prioritizing capital growth above all else at an aggregate level reduces people’s savings and in turn shrinks the potential asset management client base.

The last four decades in particular have been characterised by the truly astounding disparity between stock market and wage growth in the United States (see Figure 5). Accounting for inflation, between 1980 and 2020 the S&P 500 index has grown by a factor of 23.7. In the same period, average hourly wages have grown by a mere 10%. The stagnant wages and the explosive growth in the US stock market can be understood as different sides of the post-1980s financialization turn that privileged shareholder returns above all else.

\_\_\_

Thus, within the context of stagnating wages and a booming stock market, it is not much of a surprise that inequality in the ownership of capital has soared.

\_\_\_

The rise of institutional investors with their [downsize or distribute](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/030851400360541) mantra is a significant part of this story, as large investment funds were able to pressure firms to keep wage costs down and engage in various shareholder value maximization practices like stock buybacks. Thus, within the context of stagnating wages and a booming stock market, it is not much of a surprise that inequality in the ownership of capital has soared. Because of wage stagnation and rising costs of living, many households simply do not have enough funds remaining to make substantial enough investments to benefit from this financialized era.

How do we move past the age of asset manager capitalism? It seems that structural transformations in the global investment landscape are much needed to achieve greater economic prosperity for all. As pension funds are strongly incentivized to outsource investing decisions to external firms, establishing a [democratically controlled public asset manager](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00346764.2023.2237485) would provide a viable public alternative for pension funds in need of asset management services. A publicly controlled asset manager could create an investment portfolio and govern companies with the broader societal interests in mind rather than with the goal of maximizing its own bottom-line. For example, it could invest a greater share of its assets into socially valuable projects (such as investments in green/renewable energy) and express its support for pro-labor policies. Regulatory changes in how corporations are being governed, such as [providing significant voting power to workers](https://www.versobooks.com/en-ca/products/2855-owning-the-future) rather than just shareholders, is also likely to lead to substantial changes in what corporations end up prioritizing. When it comes to the provision of publicly vital services in sectors such as energy, housing, education, and health care, [outright public ownership of these assets](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/finance-and-society/article/taking-our-lives-out-of-their-portfolios/EA0418F3C011734A5EC2DED8C63D4EF5) could potentially provide the necessary stewardship in line with the broader public interests.
