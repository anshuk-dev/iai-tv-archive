# AI is incapable of telling the truth

*Heidegger, Foucault and why AI needs a body*

**By Bun-Sun Kim** | *9th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,060 words*

![AI is incapable of telling the truth](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/ai-is-incapable-of-telling-the-truth3.webp)

---

*We worry that AI will spread misinformation, but the real problem runs deeper: AI is incapable of telling the truth at all. Philosophers **Bun-Sun Kim** and **Hongjoon Jo** draw on Foucault and Heidegger to argue that humans speak truthfully because our finite, mortal existence is at stake in every word we say. AI, lacking a body, anxiety, or a conscience, risks nothing — it just recombines the internet's idle talk into statistically plausible text, with no self to reveal. Outsourcing our communication to AI doesn't just degrade information; it traps us in an endless loop of crowd-sourced mimicry, and threatens our capacity for genuine thought.*

ChatGPT can answer complex questions and even seem to hold conversations. But can it tell the truth?

In an era where AI can answer virtually any human question, we must examine whether AI language can truly contain truth. Since the Dartmouth Conference of 1956, we’ve witnessed dramatic technological evolution—from the AI Winter of the 1970s and 80s to today’s [sophisticated language models like ChatGPT](https://iai.tv/articles/llms-show-language-does-not-describe-reality-auid-3578) that generate remarkably human-like text. As we increasingly delegate communication to artificial, rather than human, entities, a fundamental question emerges: Can AI’s artificial language capture the essence of truth conveyed by human discourse?

This article argues that, despite AI’s impressive capabilities, artificial language fundamentally cannot contain truth in the way that human language does. Drawing on the philosophical insights of Michel Foucault and Martin Heidegger, we examine the intrinsic limitations of AI discourse. Foucault reveals how language serves as a vessel for complex meanings, beyond mere information transfer, while Heidegger’s analysis of authentic discourse exposes AI’s inability to stake its existence on its words. [In the post-truth era](https://iai.tv/articles/objectivity-and-truth-auid-548), recognizing these limitations becomes crucial as we navigate between human and artificial communication.

**The** **f****olds of truth** **t****hrough Foucault’s lens**

*The identity of things, the fact that they can resemble others and be drawn to them, though without being swallowed up or losing their singularity—this is what is assured by the constant counterbalancing of sympathy and antipathy.*

Michel Foucault, *The Order of Things*

Truth is formed through the process of the speaking subject caring for oneself. He maintains that truth does not originate from truth-value, but rather from the meaning one confers upon oneself in relation to oneself. For instance, consider the concept of pain. For example, a doctor might define pain through a standardized description of pain. But we don’t necessarily think that’s the truth about the pain that I experience. We define the pain we think of in our own language, but we leave room for the assumption that the pain I have experienced may or may not be the same as that of others.

So, when we ask AI the same question, can it also leave room in its [language for sympathy](https://iai.tv/articles/language-and-reality-auid-650), just as we have prepared? I believe it’s worth discussing this issue before relying on AI for the answer. To approach this question, let us turn to Foucault’s perspective.

Foucault analyzes the use of language in Western culture and traces how its modalities have transformed across historical epochs. The linguistic process that gives rise to empathy, according to this view, does not merely serve to designate a referent at a simple level; rather, it operates to convey meaning at a more intricate and relational level.

The four modes of expressing resemblance in language possess distinct characteristics. Foucault analyzes these modes as the four conditions that structure the semantic network of resemblance: *convenientia,* *aemulatio,* analogy, and *sympathia*, or the language of sympathy*.* From his perspective, the very technology of human language ultimately aims at the perfection of the language of sympathy.

It’s important to note that the language of sympathy isn’t a language that provides a straightforward, single answer. The four linguistic techniques unfold using symbols. These symbols, combined with linguistic play, repeatedly embody and reveal multi-layered meanings. This kind of linguistic play is also a human affect to express his thoughts and to express the truth of his actual experiences. Through language, we encounter others and thereby expand the horizon of our own lifeworld. Ultimately, the human endeavor to invent new languages and generate new expressions reflects an effort to contain the truth that constitutes both the self and its way of life.

AI cannot speak with its own existence in mind, because there is no inherent being to reveal when it speaks.

\_\_\_

Human language constitutes a chamber of coexistence, an open space that both awaits and invites the presence of others. To enable such encounters, we articulate ourselves through diverse symbols and metaphors, seeking to communicate the truth inherent in what we wish to express. In this process, we also cultivate self-care, preparing ourselves for the encounter with others by attempting to render our inner selves. Discourse attains meaning when it becomes the most capacious vessel—one that connotes both quantity and quality through its multiple folds (*plis*), layered and implicit beneath the surface. This, ultimately, is the goal of language design. “Language has no other locus, no other value, than in representation; in the hollow it has been able to form*.*”

Let us return to our problems here in the first place and apply these problems of words and objects to the problems of the AI era. The [language of AI](https://iai.tv/articles/llms-show-language-does-not-describe-reality-auid-3578) evolves by expanding and refining its linguistic capacity through processes of learning. To complete AI language with the same rules as human language, it follows the method of inputting logic language into AI.

AI language is primarily designed for the transmission of knowledge. Yet, can it also convey the self-perception of the world that humans require to live meaningfully? Even if AI surpasses algorithmic reasoning and develops into a form of strong or even superintelligence capable of autonomous learning, will it continue to strive toward sharing sympathy, the fundamental purpose of language itself?

When humans expand their world through language, they do not merely expand their own thoughts. They expand language by considering its relationships with other beings and with the environment in which they coexist. This expansion of language, in turn, is tantamount to the expansion of the human world itself.

If AI language must contain truth, it’s essential to grasp the true meaning of truthfulness, one that resides within the empty yet sympathetic spaces created in the manner of human existence. As truth is concealed within the folds of life, it can be disclosed only through the language of sympathy.

**The Rise of Truth and the Limitations of AI** **t****hrough Heidegger’s Lens**

[Heidegger’s phenomenology](https://iai.tv/articles/heidegger-poetry-and-the-cure-for-technology-auid-3439) further highlights the limitations of AI. The assertion that “artificial languages cannot contain truth” stems from his phenomenological interpretation of human existence. For Heidegger, being human (*Dasein*) means a “speaking being.” It is precisely from this definition that a direct comparison with AI can begin, since AI is also a “speaking being.” However, unlike AI’s, the speech of Dasein is not merely a means of conveying information; it is the authentic discourse through which humans exist and create their world.

In order to make this comparison, we must examine the four essential elements of authentic discourse, as described by Heidegger. First, the object that the word signifies, i.e., its meaning or implication. Secondly, the word itself—the sound or sign that we utter. Thirdly, discourse involves communication and sharing between beings. Fourth—and this is the core—authentic discourse expresses the being of the speaker (Dasein) itself, their unique way of existing in the world. When we speak authentically, we are not merely conveying data; we are revealing ourselves and simultaneously illuminating the world we inhabit.

However, Heidegger contrasts authentic speech with what he terms [“idle talk”](https://iai.tv/articles/what-death-tells-us-about-life-auid-263) (*Gerede*), which is a non-authentic form of speech. Idle talk signifies only the sound of words and the communication between speakers; it lacks both the meaning of the words and the speaker’s being. For instance, in idle talk, we convey what the “they” (*das Man*; the anonymous crowd) says, repeating their opinions and catchphrases. Phenomenologically, it is a fact that, in everyday life, we primarily live as simple conveyors. The problem is that idle talk obscures the truth, leading us to live by concealing the world and ourselves behind the veil of familiar conventions.

However, we humans can pierce through idle talk and progress towards [authentic speech](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-heidegger-and-the-language-of-silence-auid-3361). This does not originate from the calculative logic of reason, however, but rather from what Heidegger calls the (fundamental) mood, “Anxiety” (*Angst*). This is an instinctive, profound confrontation with our own finite existence—a visceral recognition that nobody can live our lives for us. In this moment of “anxiety,” we hear the “call of conscience.” Heidegger's conscience is not a moral voice, but a silent call from within ourselves demanding we take responsibility for our own being rather than being swept along by the crowd.

When we respond to this call with “resolution,” speech is transformed; the idle talk of the “they” is stripped away. We no longer circulate the empty words of the “they,” but begin to speak from the depths of our own being. In doing so, we reveal the world as *Aletheia* (unconcealment). This authentic discourse pierces convention and names things truthfully. This is as different as conveying the facts of someone else’s death is from confronting your own. Unlike idle talk, authentic speech expresses the entirety of the speaker’s finite existence.

This very shift towards the authentic discourse about human existence constitutes an insurmountable barrier for AI. This is not merely a technological barrier, but an existential limitation of AI. AI cannot experience anxiety. It possesses neither a mortal body nor a finite existence to lose. Consequently, lacking an individual self to appeal to, AI cannot hear the call of conscience. Therefore, it is unable to choose decisiveness. It merely processes inputs as it has been trained to do. AI cannot speak with its own existence in mind, because there is no inherent being to reveal when it speaks.

Therefore, whatever AI produces will always be confined to idle talk. No matter how sophisticated the advanced language models developed may be, they remain merely elaborate systems that circulate and recombine the words of the “they”—i.e., the training data—to generate the most statistically plausible variations. This increasingly precise statistical plausibility will ultimately lead to a dictatorship of the “they.” In other words, the true tragedy lies in the fact that AI’s “empty talk” will come to dominate us. With AI’s assistance, the idle talk that Dasein must break through will become ever more entrenched, ultimately preventing Dasein from [expressing itself authentically](https://iai.tv/articles/escaping-from-distraction-simon-glendinning-auid-2743).

Heidegger later describes language as the “house of being” where being is revealed and preserved. Yet this house cannot be built by algorithms that merely convey sounds or signs. It can only be built on the foundation of the resolute act of finite beings who stake their own irreplaceable being in speech. Moreover, without this existential foundation, even an artificial language that conveys sound with technical proficiency alone would ultimately collapse.

**Conclusion**

The philosophical analysis of Foucault and Heidegger reveals fundamental limitations in AI’s capacity to embrace or convey truth. According to Foucault, AI language is a compressed, text-based discourse that lacks the essential *plis*—the emotional, subjective and embodied dimension that gives human language its genuine meaning. AI only processes patterns and lacks the “sympathy” required for genuine expression. Heidegger’s critique more sharply delves into the difference between the two. AI is forever trapped in “nonsense,” circulating empty words without the capacity for authentic discourse. Without a mortal body capable of experiencing [“anxiety,”](https://iai.tv/articles/existentialism-and-the-future-of-humanity-auid-1753) a “conscience” to prompt self-reflection, or the capacity for decisive judgement, AI cannot stake its existence on words. It possesses neither a genuine self to express nor a world to reveal through language. Even if “strong AI”| emerges and embodies itself in an artificial physical body, as long as this body is a mere combination of software and hardware, it cannot possess human emotions such as sympathy and anxiety, which fundamentally stem from the unity of human body and mind.

Therefore, as we increasingly delegate communication to AI systems, we risk impoverishing the world of language. However, this risk does not lie in AI becoming excessively human-like, but rather humans becoming so immersed in artificial forms of language that they lose sight of why authentic discourse is irreplaceable. Therefore, we must develop frameworks that preserve the value of natural language and utilize it effectively within the inherent limitations of AI language. We must always remember that truth is revealed only through the courageous utterances of finite, anxious, and conscientious beings—through embodied language, which is the language of emotion and subjectivity.
