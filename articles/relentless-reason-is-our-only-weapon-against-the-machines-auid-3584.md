# Relentless reason is our only weapon against the machines

*We must never stop being critical and creative*

**By Vangelis Giannakakis** | *26th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,734 words*

![Relentless reason is our only weapon against the machines](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/relentless-reason-is-our-only-weapon-against-the-machines.webp)

---

*The tech economy is booming, and we’re sleepwalking into servitude. Algorithms capture and fracture our attention, AI is forced upon us, and capital becomes ever more concentrated. Some respond by worshipping technological progress as our salvation. Others retreat into fantasies of a simpler past. Both responses, argues philosopher **Vangelis Giannakakis**, have already surrendered, because both have abandoned reason’s power to be simultaneously critical and creative. To resist tech domination, we must, as Adorno said, be “absolutely modern,” wielding a restless reason that refuses to settle, that never stops questioning, and that relentlessly bends the world towards something better.*

**The modern world drains experience of meaning**

We are living through a period in which subjective human experience feels increasingly imperiled. Social media fragments attention into restless cycles of distraction and outrage; streaming platforms and generative AI organize culture around what is predictable, marketable, and algorithmically successful. More and more domains of life are governed by metrics, analytics, and optimization.

Of course, optimization as such is not inherently malign. Medicine, engineering, and public infrastructure depend upon it. The danger arises when optimization ceases to function as a practical tool and instead becomes a governing ideal: when efficiency, scalability, and predictability become the primary standards by which culture, thought, and even human value are assessed. Experiences requiring contemplation, ambiguity, inwardness, or instinctive judgement begin to appear inefficient, opaque, or superfluous.

In such a world, the critical theory of Theodor Adorno acquires renewed force. Writing about mass media in the twentieth century, Adorno argued that industrial society increasingly transformed culture into a system of standardization and passive consumption. The technologies have changed; the underlying logic has only deepened.

Late capitalism does not abolish experience outright. Rather, as Adorno foresaw, it standardizes it, disciplines it, and drains it of meaning: “It is as if the … hardened plaster-cast of events takes the place of events themselves,” and people “are reduced to [walk-on parts in a monster documentary film](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/adorno/1951/mm/ch01.htm) which has no spectators, since the least of them has his bit to do on screen.”

Faced with this landscape, we tend to gravitate towards two extremes. Some embrace acceleration as destiny. Innovation becomes synonymous with progress, and progress with salvation. Others retreat into imagined authenticity, seeking refuge in a reconstructed past or a supposedly natural state of things. Both responses evade the harder labor of reflection. Each offers a convenient escape, and both ultimately regress.

Adorno proposes another orientation. He often cites Arthur Rimbaud’s injunction: *il faut* *ê**tre absolument moderne*. One ought to be absolutely modern. The phrasing matters. It does not say that one must absolutely be modern because modernity is inherently good. Rather, if one is to be modern at all, one must be so absolutely, without nostalgia but also without the illusion that progress is inevitable.

Here lies the paradox. Modernity, particularly late modernity—*our* modernity—has become an impersonal apparatus of instrumental reason: we view “[anything which does not conform to the standard of calculability and utility … with suspicion](https://dn710607.ca.archive.org/0/items/pdfy-TJ7HxrAly-MtUP4B/Dialectic%2520of%2520Enlightenment%2520-%2520Theodor%2520W.%2520Adorno,%2520Max%2520Horkheimer.pdf).” Yet Adorno, who prophesied our situation, does not counsel escape. Instead, he insists that we must inhabit the present fully, critically. This is the riddle. How can a thinker who dismantled the myth of progress so thoroughly still urge us to be absolutely modern?

**Reclaiming reason from the illusion of objectivity**

The answer lies in his understanding of reason. The Enlightenment gave us the idea that reason provides us with timeless truths, and that by the light of reason progress is inevitable. But for Adorno, this was always a castle in the sky. Reason is not a rigid arbiter of timeless truths, and nor should it be used as a justificatory veneer for whatever happens to prevail. Instead, it is a reflective, restless, self-critical capacity. Its task is not to secure a final, absolutely objective picture of reality, but to remain attentive to the ways in which every picture is historically conditioned, partial, and liable to conceal forms of domination or exclusion.

That is why reason must remain “restless”: once it claims complete objectivity or final truth, it ceases to be critical and instead hardens into ideology. Reason, on this view, is better understood pragmatically, as an ongoing process of testing and revising our interpretations of the world in light of changing historical and social conditions. We judge ideas not by whether they correspond to some timeless standpoint outside history, but by how adequately they illuminate experience, respond to suffering and contradiction, and enable forms of life that are less coercive and more humane. Because the world itself changes, reason can never arrive at a final resting place; its critical work is necessarily unfinished.

Adorno suggests that what we take to be objective is really subjective. The “non-controversial aspect of things, their unquestioned impression, the façade made up of classified data,” all seem objective to us, but this is an illusion: university rankings reduce education to numerical scores; hiring algorithms present biased decisions as neutral data processing; social media “engagement” metrics treat clicks and watch time as objective indicators of value; GDP figures suggest a country is flourishing even while many of its people feel economically insecure and socially isolated. On the other hand, whenever we still manage to embrace subjective experience, we penetrate this façade by casting off “[all readymade judgements and … the majority consensus](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/adorno/1951/mm/ch01.htm)” and instead, indeed somewhat paradoxically, grant priority to the truly objective side of things. Humble subjective experience enables us to see through the fake, hubristic objectivity of the façade. To put it in Adorno’s words, the sort of experience “that approaches its objects openly, rigorously, and on the basis of [progressive knowledge](https://platypus1917.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/adorno_onsubjectandobject.pdf), is also free toward its objects in the sense that it refuses to have rules prescribed to it by organized knowledge. It turns the quintessence of the experience accumulated in it to the objects, rends the veil with which society conceals them, and perceives them anew.

To reject absolute objectivity today means rejecting authoritative, even authoritarian, subjectivity. In simpler terms: resisting know-it-all certainty. Paradoxically enough, the best—if not the *only*—way to do that today is through more subjectivity, not less: more breach of the façade, more relatedness to the object, more emphatic experience. To be sure, subjective experience, even a humble one, does not grant access to timeless truths or guarantee moral consensus. People can experience the same event and arrive at radically opposed conclusions, as contemporary political conflicts aptly demonstrate. Anger, grief, fear, or outrage do not by themselves produce objective truth. What subjective experience can do, however, is interrupt the false appearance of neutrality projected by dominant systems of knowledge. The horror elicited by war, the feeling of suffocation produced by severe inequality, or the anxiety generated by ecological crisis do not function here as proofs of an objective demonstration. Rather, they register tensions, contradictions, and forms of suffering that established social, political, cultural and technological frameworks often normalize or render invisible. Their importance lies not in delivering certainty, but in preventing existing arrangements from presenting themselves as complete, natural, or beyond dispute.

In that sense, subjectivity is not opposed to critique but stands as its condition. It keeps thought responsive to experiences that cannot be fully absorbed into statistics, administrative categories, or ideological narratives. The point is therefore not to replace one absolute truth with another, but to preserve the possibility of critical reflection against systems that claim to have already explained everything.

**Tech “progress” has become a regression**

The contemporary digital landscape intensifies all these tensions. Technocratic systems promise limitless creativity even as they operate through statistical pattern recognition. They recombine existing material and refine standardization. In this sense, they are not only the culmination of the good old culture industry, but a synthesis of retro and futuro utopia that preaches solutionism that conserves the status quo.

Where formulaic cinema, radio, television, the press or socio-political discourses once produced predictable narratives, machine learning models now generate text, images, music, indeed entire experiential environments disconnected from actual reality. These simulate novelty while remaining anchored to aggregated precedent. The new kitsch presents itself as cutting-edge. Increasingly, the feeling emerges that we inhabit a world resembling a collision between *Groundhog Day* and *The Matrix*: an endless repetition of the same old to the benefit of impersonal, modernistic—but not modern—tech-orders. It is not for nothing that Yanis Varoufakis refers to the current state of affairs as “[techno-feudalism](https://iai.tv/video/the-rise-of-technofeudalism).” With AI and the tech revolutions we’re living through, humanity should have been catapulted 1,000 years into the future, yet it seems stuck 1,000 years in the past.

This is not to deny the technical sophistication of these systems. The question concerns rather the kind of experience they produce. When culture becomes seamless output shaped by algorithmic feedback loops and profit margins, the space for independent, critical and actually progressive reflection and action contracts. Creativity risks collapsing into sheer optimization. Expression becomes marketable content. Action turns into self-serving, calculated interaction. And culture turns into [pseudo-culture](https://www.are.na/block/2008333).

The totally administered world that Adorno identified has acquired a more advanced interface, sure, but the same old conformism persists behind it. What becomes of the sort of experience that resists existing frameworks of thought and practice? Of all those experiences that challenge assumptions, present enigmas, and demand decisions? Is there still room for something like that in our modernity? Risk-assessment algorithms say “no.”

**Escapism which achieves no real escape**

At the same time, the mythology of the slow life promises redemption through unplugging, homesteading, traditional values, or a return to simpler times. Certain populist rhetorics frame digital culture as decadence and modern pluralism as decay. The cure, they insist, lies in restoration. Others propose a solar-punk horizon: a techno-ecological identification with what Freud once called the “oceanic feeling,” the harmonious equilibrium between humanity and nature, humanity and itself, between self and universe.

Both tendencies are artificial regressions. Reactionary traditionalism does not transcend modern systems. It reproduces them in disguise. The desire for purity frequently relies upon the very technologies it condemns, as with the “trad wives” who promote their idylls with daily Instagram posts, hyper-masculine “primitive” lifestyles built around caveman diets, biohacking routines, artisanal hunting culture, and ancestral fitness regimens endlessly optimized through podcasts, wearable trackers, and algorithmically driven supplement markets. Our reality remains much less *[Dear Alice](https://youtu.be/z-Ng5ZvrDm4)* than *[Beneath a Steel Sky](https://revolution.co.uk/games_catalog/beneath-a-steel-sky/)*. Moreover, these gestures often render individuals more predictable. Declaring oneself anti-modern in a stylized fashion simply supplies another category for algorithmic classification. Rebellion becomes a lifestyle segment. No wonder traditionalism and rustic eco-technologism increasingly converge: anti-vaxxers, alternative healers, medieval fairs, conspiracy theorists, futurists and techno-prophets splice together into one infinite TikTok scroll.

This dynamic appears most clearly in contemporary right-wing populism. In a 1967 [lecture on right-wing radicalism](https://www.politybooks.com/bookdetail?book_slug=aspects-of-the-new-right-wing-extremism--9781509541447), Adorno argued that such movements are not relics of pre-modern society but products of late capitalist democracy itself. Trumpism illustrates this well. Donald Trump’s political brand is not a simple return to the 1950s. It is a hyper-modern creation of the culture industry, mobilizing social media, data analytics, and digital spectacle to promote fantasies of national restoration and rebellion against an irrational system whose quintessential representative he is.

Similarly, ecological discourse has often been absorbed into green capitalism, which repackages the same old structures under a renewed moralistic façade. Profits, exploitation, gentrification, and even natural despoliation persist. Meanwhile, the affluent purchase ethical consumption with a clear conscience in glossily designed, yuppified, delivery-friendly, car-free districts, while the “meek” produce the relevant goods and services under precarious conditions in prison factories, “bullshit job” cubicles, and flammable workshops. Worse still, the two extremes increasingly close in on each other behind the veil of the ever more ubiquitous and one-dimensional interface screen and polished, depthless habits.

The rhetoric appears anti-modern or ultra-modern. The methods are entirely conventional. Outrage spreads algorithmically. Pseudo-individuality flourishes through personalized feeds. Revolution is an influencer’s daily bread.

This is technological populism: a movement that masters advanced tools while preaching pastoral nostalgia. Confronting it with outdated platitudes merely repeats its logic. Resistance must be at least as technologically and conceptually sophisticated as the forces it opposes. In *Negative Dialectics*, Adorno criticizes identity thinking: the assumption that a concept exhausts the reality it names. When politics reduces itself to slogans such as “The People,” or when technology is equated uncritically with “Progress,” identity thinking is at work.

Total modernity represents the culmination of identity thinking: a social order in which [algorithm and ideology leave no remainder](https://x.com/PalantirTech/status/2045574398573453312). In such a society, experience dissolves into data points and metrics. The non-identical aspects of life, those elements resistant to classification, are suppressed. Conscious experience, which is larger and richer than quantitative data can ever capture, is utterly repressed.

**Restless reason and mimesis as means of resistance**

Adorno replaces identity thinking with the principle of *mimesis*. The concept is often misunderstood. Mimesis does not mean mere imitation. It refers to the capacity to approach objects through attunement rather than domination, to know them through proximity. Within a world governed by techno-instrumental reason, such responsiveness appears weak, indeed primitive.

Yet Adorno insists that mimesis can assume highly sophisticated forms. Two domains of human experience remain especially capable of sustaining it: art and philosophy. No wonder that the greatest beneficiaries and thereby defenders of the present state of affairs, like Elon Musk and his acolytes, [allow no place for them in their dystopian present](https://doi.org/10.1080/01425692.2025.2609177).

To resist AI, one must understand its material. To resist digital populism, one must grasp its logic. The task is not sentimental humanism, a vague appeal to warmth against cold machinery. Rather, it is to inhabit the advanced materials of the present so thoroughly that their contradictions become visible.

One speaks the language of the algorithm in order to express what the algorithm cannot register: the irreducibility of experience.

This approach rejects easy synthesis. It does not promise harmonious reconciliation between humanity and technology, nor nostalgic restoration of tradition. Instead, it sustains tension. Digital infrastructures, urban systems, and cultural narratives are not external threats but environments we already inhabit. The task is to navigate them critically, revealing their limits while exploiting their capacities, [to push the materials of the present to their breaking point](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/guy-debord-gil-j-wolman-a-user-s-guide-to-detournement).

In practical terms, this might mean using social media platforms not simply for passive consumption or personal branding, but to reveal the ways they commodify attention and shape political discourse. It might mean usurping corporate technologies for forms of collaboration or public knowledge that exceed the commercial aims for which they were designed. It could mean designing cities around collective wellbeing and accessibility rather than purely around efficiency or profit, thereby testing the limits of existing economic assumptions. The “breaking point” is therefore intellectual and political rather than apocalyptic: the point at which dominant systems become unable to conceal their own contingency, contradictions, and failures, opening space for alternative ways of living and organizing society—for a “new sensibility,” to use Herbert Marcuse’s well-known term.

To do this, we must cultivate a form of reason that is restless, self-questioning, and attentive to the non-identical. In its restlessness and self-questioning, it never settles on something it calls objective truth, but always pushes on critically. We must affirm the new that exceeds regulated appearances and established knowledge. In the convergence of reflection and mimetic responsiveness lies the possibility of an absolute modernity.

Renewed critical reason offers only a fragile defense. It promises no final victory over the machine. Yet it may open new dimensions of thought and action within the modern world. Such modernity rejects both resignation and naive optimism. It recognizes crisis without surrendering to despair. It embraces innovation without equating it automatically with progress. It embodies what Slavoj Žižek calls the “courage of hopelessness.”

Experience is not merely data input or virtual profiles, likes, and subscribers. It is the texture of attention, the intensity of relation, the depth of reflection. It is the insight gained through failure and the joy of understanding while preserving nuance.

To be absolutely modern is therefore a demanding mandate, an absolute, negative one. It requires inhabiting our time without illusion, mastering its tools without becoming their servants, resisting both mythic past and technocratic destiny. In doing so, thought may recover its generative force. And perhaps, within that recovery, the integrity of human experience can still be defended. As some old graffiti once put it: we must be realists and demand the impossible.
