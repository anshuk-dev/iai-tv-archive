# Heidegger, poetry, and the cure for technology

*Finding stillness in a world of turmoil*

**By Mathilde Nielsen** | *8th December 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,724 words*

![Heidegger, poetry, and the cure for technology](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/FINAL-Heidegger.webp)

*Contemporary life is dominated by technology that makes it difficult to be present with one another, to take time to make decisions, to concentrate fully on others. Philosopher **Mathilde Nielsen** argues that we can look to Heidegger to make sense of this predicament. His turn to poetry in his later years allowed him to find a way to resist technology's dominance in his day. We can do the same. The simple act of reading poetry can expand our ethical horizons by requiring us to focus and care for something outside ourselves. Heidegger's idea of "dwelling" in poetry can teach us how to live more ethically.*

In his later years, the German philosopher Martin Heidegger (1889-1976) turned to poetry as the best way to do philosophy, as he found in poetry what he was struggling to find elsewhere: a place where he could think deeply and meaningfully. He called this place “the clearing,” as in the clearing of a forest, and held that once one was there, one could begin to think about and apprehend one’s presence — “the clearing is the place of stillness from which alone the possibility of the belonging together of Being and thinking, that is, presence and apprehending, can arise…”

Written in 1969, I find these words astonishingly apt for some Western contemporary societies—especially considering how those societies have been influenced by technological developments, making the clearing more difficult to find. In some respects, technological developments have turned societies into a thick forest of products, images, notifications, dings and dongs. Technological developments have accelerated our everyday lives; turned activities into tasks to be solved, boxes to be ticked; reduced phenomena and creatures to stocks of resources. To see this, think only of how human beings are being reduced to the product they can present to their employer by the end of their day – or how sentient beings have been reduced to red blobs of goo packaged, priced, and tailored to nearly every segmented consumer.

In this article, I’d like to focus on how some contemporary technological developments, or rather, how an underlying technological *framework*, has made it increasingly difficult to think and act ethically in today’s Western societies. In 1953 Heidegger referred to this framework as *Gestell*, or “enframing.”

Enframing is his term for the distorted worldview that turns human beings into “human resources”, patients into “supply” for clinics, rivers into “power stations,” reducing phenomena and creatures to stocks of resources. Enframing touches upon what Weber meant when he talked about [“the iron cage,”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weber/) or what Adorno and Horkheimer were referring to with their [“instrumental reason”;](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/horkheimer/) yet enframing remains distinctly its own in part due to its links to poetry—and poetry’s links to the clearing.

As a site of apprehension, the clearing is obviously valuable for many reasons and purposes. In this article, however, I’ll mention just one: ethics.

One crude understanding of ethics goes that ethics is the theoretical and practical attempt to allow beings—oneself and others—to thrive. I write “thrive” and not “function” since I believe, with Heidegger, that human beings are not mere cogs in a machine but a specimen of being that is capable of looking beyond its given place, questioning this place, dreaming of different places, and coming to terms with what comes after its time—that is, with its death. Human beings are, in short, for Heidegger, “an entity for which, in its Being, that Being is an issue.”

Okay. But what has this got to do with poetry and ethics?

To allow oneself and other beings to thrive, that is to act ethically, one must view and address other human beings *as* human beings and not as cogs in a machine. Heidegger’s argument is that where the enframing and technological developments can turn human beings into mere cogs, poetry can counter this distortion. Thus, if you live in a modern society where technological developments constantly work to reduce human (and arguably non-human) beings to cogs or resources, Heidegger proposes poetry as an especially pertinent step towards the clearing.

That sounds great—so of course, it’s not the whole story.

Poetry can counter the enframing by bringing its readers and makers closer to the clearing. How so? First by offering a place of stillness and quietude. When you think about it, a white page with just a verse or a single line on it is a stark contrast to the visual and textual overload that other sites of your day may offer, be it the tube, the office, the street, or your social media feed. The page, in all its simplicity, is an invitation to the clearing. Second, in virtue of its whiteness, its blankness, the page can be considered a “dummy” for the nothingness which Heidegger—and Kierkegaard before him—thought a part of the warp and woof of existence. Existence consists in some tangibility but also in a great deal of intangibility, of nothingness, not much unlike the nothingness offered by the paper’s white. Poetry thus offers a gentle way for us to familiarize ourselves with it.

Yet, for poetry to be able to counter the enframing, Heidegger held that the reader—and maker—of poetry must also make an effort. Simply skimming the page won’t do; Heidegger dubs this effort “dwelling.” Naturally, part of this effort is innate in the mere activity of reading, contrasted to the minimal effort required for gliding, swiping, scrolling through a feed. Whereas a poem—any kind of text—requires reading, focusing, and the picking apart of some code, short videos like the ones we see on social media pour their “message” into their viewers.

Here, I believe that language contains an important clue. The word “text” comes from the Latin *textus*, a cloth comprised of interlaced threads. A cloth or a code can be unravelled, if one makes the effort and gets to grips with it. By contrast, the word “content,” the standard phrase used to refer to the, well, *content* on social media, implies a “filling,” something that does not require an effort on the part of its recipient.

Now, as with most of Heidegger’s notions, scholars have proposed a variety of interpretations of what dwelling means and what it is good for, if anything at all. Some argue it is a tool for architects, others that dwelling is a remedy for artists. In this piece, I’d like to suggest that dwelling is not limited to any profession. Rather, it is a fundamental human capacity; more specifically, to dwell is to pay attention to *x* in a way that does not interfere with *x*, but which makes the dweller aware of the dweller’s *own* fundamental kind of being, that is, a *caring* kind of being.

\_\_\_

Dwelling is an *ethical* activity and as such, it can resist enframing, the view that reduces the world to a stock of resources.

\_\_\_

That is enigmatic and calls for an example. Consider the following one, given by the Danish poet Inger Christensen (1935-2009):

*We do it as  
well as we can.  
We shower,  
do the cleaning,  
sweep the spider from its web and,  
when the rain stops  
we go out onto the terrace  
and listen  
to the river.  
On the road  
a woman passes  
and just there  
to the left  
of the  
blooming onions  
a bird  
sits.  
And all night  
under the light of the  
stars  
we study the map*

In this fragment from the poem *Letter in April*, the “we” of the poem pays attention to the house, the spider, the rain, the river, without interfering with any of them. The spider is swept from its net, and its net isn’t torn asunder. Similarly, the house is kept clean and is not left to decay. The same goes for the body of the “we”: “we shower.” Not even the rain is interfered with. Instead, the “we”—the you and I, humankind—wait for the rain to stop and only then does it—do we—enter the terrace. Finally, the “we” of the poem becomes aware that this caring attentiveness that it pays to the house, the spider, the rain, the river is specific to its own kind of being, that is, to human beings: It is “we” who do it all as well as we can, whilst the bird just sits there.

Pulling all this together, my suggestion is that dwelling is an *ethical* activity and as such, it can resist enframing, the view that reduces the world to a stock of resources. Dwelling, as the effort to pay this kind of caring attention, is, in a deep sense, to give ethics a chance. How? Because dwelling always resists the view of human beings as uncaring, uncared-for cogs. Dwelling always insists upon the view—as in Christensen’s poem—of human beings as the caring kind of being, the only view that could make ethics (here understood broadly as the attempt to make (human) beings *thrive*, not “run” or “produce”) successful.

Here enter the controversies.

In 2014, the German philosopher Peter Thrawny began publishing Heidegger’s “black notebooks.” The notebooks contain the most despicable slurs and beliefs aimed at Jewish people, and they have divided Heideggerians on the question of how and even whether to read Heidegger.

In a nutshell, if Heidegger held that Jews are somehow “disconnected” from the being of the world and therefore unable to think about and apprehend their presence, did Heidegger then believe that Jews were barred from entering the clearing? And if he did think this, can we then think it differently? Can we think about the clearing as a site of apprehension open to *all* human beings, an enabler of our desired ethics, or must we, bearing the notebooks in mind, discard and condemn the clearing as an anti-Semitic concept?

Scholars disagree and I will not pretend to be able to offer a satisfying argument for whether one can or cannot think about the clearing without endorsing the notebooks’ beliefs. What I am able to say is that I believe we do ourselves a disfavour if we ignore Heidegger’s work. It simply has too much to offer us as in the case of our lives within the enframing. When technological developments seem to extend the enframing and technology’s logic of instrumentalization around our entire existence, Heidegger reminds us that poetry continues to offer itself, quietly and insistently, as a route to a place of stillness—and perhaps even a place for ethics to bud.
