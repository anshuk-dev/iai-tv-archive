# Nietzsche was no simple relativist

*Rethinking the nature of truth*

**By Kathleen Higgins** | *17th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,122 words*

![Nietzsche was no simple relativist](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/nietzsche-was-not-a-relativist.webp)

---

*Friedrich Nietzsche is often seen as the prophet of anarchic relativism. His notorious claim that "there are no facts, only interpretations" appears to license a world of competing narratives and alternative facts. But did Nietzsche think that anything goes? In this article, **Kathleen Higgins** revisits his perspectivalism and his debt to Immanuel Kant, arguing that Nietzsche did not abandon truth but reconceived it. By recognizing the inescapable role of human perspective, he calls not for relativism, but a deeper and fuller objectivity which tests our interpretations against evidence and against one another.*

We often hear that we live in a [post-truth era](https://iai.tv/articles/in-a-post-truth-world-truth-seeking-is-more-important-than-ever-auid-3382). As opposed to a time when disagreements mostly concerned what to do in light of the facts, now people disagree about the facts themselves. And it is hard to deny that polarization is widespread in many places, with little evidence that those with conflicting views are attempting to understand their opponents’ positions. Many find it all too easy [to consider their political rivals insensitive to reality](https://iai.tv/articles/democracy-and-the-polarization-trap-auid-2035) and willing to spread lies to achieve their objectives. From there, it is an easy step to abandon efforts to negotiate on policy or even to have civil conversations with those of a different opinion.

Friedrich Nietzsche is often mentioned in connection with current political problems, whether with reference to his theory of will to power, his denial of traditional theories of truth, his critique of democracy, his elitism, or some combination of the above. His writings present challenges for interpreters, not least because they are unsystematic and filled with assertions followed by reconsiderations. But Nietzsche’s gift for catchy one-liners leads many to think his views can be encapsulated in a few statements, and often disturbing ones at that.

Thus, several of his quotable statements sound as though he thinks facts are unimportant. Most of these, we should note, are comments written in notebooks, not published claims. But we do find a number of notebook comments to the effect that there are no facts, but only interpretations. Does this mean that he thinks we only have “alternative facts,” vying interpretive claims that are equally justified? Hardly.

\_\_\_

We cannot, as Nietzsche puts it, subtract the human contribution from the facts as we know them. We cannot directly compare our statements with external reality to see if they correspond.

\_\_\_

What can we even make of the claim that we only have interpretations? What could be interpreted, if not the “facts” as one encounters them? One of Nietzsche’s notebook comments even says as much: “There are no ‘facts-in-themselves,’ for a sense must always be projected onto them before there can be ‘facts.’”

In referring to “facts-in-themselves,” Nietzsche reveals himself to be an intellectual heir of eighteenth-century philosopher Immanuel Kant. Kant maintained that human beings do not have direct knowledge of reality as it is in itself. We don’t know the “thing-in-itself.” Instead, our mental faculties integrate and structure the information that we take in through our senses, and what we “know” of reality is always formatted by our faculties. Given that human beings all have the same mental faculties, according to Kant, the formatting our minds supply to what the senses perceive is standard across the species. But whatever “truths” we know are only humanly formatted truths. We cannot, as Nietzsche puts it, subtract the human contribution from the facts as we know them. We cannot directly compare our statements with external reality to see if they correspond.

But Nietzsche goes further, since he is interested in more perspectival human contributions to what we know. He thinks that the facts we know are always facts with spin. That is, what we consciously take to be factual is not only shaped by the mental faculties we all share. It is interpreted in accordance with our needs and desires, and some of these are unconscious.

\_\_\_

We are always observing from a perspective, and perspectives are only partial.

\_\_\_

Many facts regarding external reality are not matters over which people tend to disagree. Under normal circumstances, letting go of a lifted fruit will result in its falling to the ground, and this fact is recognizable by any person on the planet past an early stage of infancy. This fact is usually neutral in terms of our motivations, though sometimes it may be usefully factored into our plans for fulfilling our needs and desires, as when we shake a tree with ripe cherries to get the fruit.

In other cases, our differing perspectives have an impact on what we observe, with the result that we have different impressions of the facts. We may have different conceptions of the shape of an object depending on where we stand in relation to it. The color of a car may seem incontrovertible, but in a very dark parking lot, a burgundy car may appear dark gray. We can reconcile differences in observations of such sorts by taking account of where different observers were located and the conditions of observation. But even these reconcilable differences should remind us that we are always observing from a perspective, and perspectives are only partial.

Nietzsche is especially interested in the less obvious ways we come to disagree about facts. Habits affect what we observe, and we are often not very attentive. Nietzsche complains that many people think they have gained knowledge when they have merely analyzed something striking in familiar terms. Those who share societal habits may agree because an interpretation accords with what seems familiar, though the phenomenon encountered may have been obscured in the process.

We also notice things we have labels for, and Nietzsche has problems with the tendency to be content with what things are called, as opposed to what they are. Reputations can be faulty, but people usually rely on them. Indeed, as Nietzsche notes, new coinages themselves can lead to the impression of there being “new things.” We might notice this in connection with buzzwords and trendy slogans. Without the expression, how many “perfect storms” would have occurred? And yet, “perfect storms” started to appear in diverse domains once the expression gained popularity.

Perhaps more problematically, motivations affect our impression of the facts. Nietzsche contends that our drives affect our observations, and our conscious interpretations follow the dictates of our unconscious drives, or more precisely, the drive that is currently dominant among them. We always interpret what we observe in accordance with our desires, even when we attempt to be unbiased. We are eager to organize our observations into patterns, preferably familiar patterns, and so we unwittingly oversimplify. We typically interpret new evidence in a way that corroborates our existing beliefs, a tendency now called “confirmation bias.”

Indeed, we notice what is relevant to our aims, and interpret in light of them. Nietzsche thinks that our fundamental motivation is what he calls “will to power.” His sense of power is not primarily about ruling over others or striving for overt dominance. Instead, it is, broadly, [the will to operate freely in one’s environment](https://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-and-the-myth-of-free-will-auid-2535) as opposed to being controlled by it. Regardless of whether we seem to be bent on gaining power, Nietzsche contends that will to power subtly motivates our behavior. Creative activities and various ways that we try to make our mark on the world may not be overt power plays, but they manifest will to power, which drives all our efforts to have an impact and achieve something in our lives.

Will to power also affects the way each of us sees the world. What we notice in the environment depends on what we take to be relevant to our aims, including the broad aim of being in a position to further what we consider to be our own interests. As a consequence, we often disagree because our respective goals prime us to have different appraisals of the situation. Even if plausible generalizations can be made about human aims in general, we vary considerably in the way we weight our priorities. What each of us sees as salient aspects of our world are seen through the filter of our goals and the general will to power that lies behind them.

\_\_\_

We will never have objectivity full-stop, but we can become increasingly objective by pooling perspectives and testing our own views against the evidence.

\_\_\_

Thus, according to Nietzsche, we should not expect agreement about the salient features of the situation or how to interpret them. But even if we will never get convergence on the salient features of the environment or on how we should organize our priorities, this is not a reason to abandon concern with truth. Instead, we should recognize that our own and others’ motives affect the way things appear to us, and we should recognize that views of the world are in competition. In other words, our perspective on the world will inevitably compete with perspectives that others have, and it behooves us to acknowledge this and to realize why this is inevitable.

This is not, by Nietzsche’s lights, a reason to endorse a “post-truth condition,” understood as thinking that facts do not matter or do not matter much. Instead, it is a reason to be skeptical of both our own and others’ claims, and to continue to reexamine them. Nietzsche thinks we should doubt anyone (ourselves included) who claims to know “the Truth” in an absolute sense, and recognize that one’s own observations are inevitably partial. But this does not mean that we should throw out the idea of objectivity. It only means that none of us has a completely objective view, and that we can improve our observations by comparing notes and continually reassessing our own impressions. Objectivity is not an all-or-nothing matter; it is a matter of more or less. As Nietzsche puts it in *On the Genealogy of Morals*: “There is *only* a perspective seeing, *only* a perspective ‘knowing’; and the *more* affects we allow to speak about one thing, the *more* eyes, different eyes, we can use to observe one thing, the more complete will our ‘concept’ of this thing, our ‘objectivity’ be.”

“Objectivity,” according to Nietzsche, involves drawing the obvious conclusion from a recognition that our individual perspectives constrain what we know. We will never have objectivity full-stop, but we can become increasingly objective by pooling perspectives and testing our own views against the evidence. We are epistemologically most responsible when we acknowledge the limitations of our own observations of things—the facts as we interpret them—and attempt to reduce these limitations by comparing notes with other people and with our own observations on different occasions. To improve our knowledge, we need intellectual humility. This is hardly to relinquish concern for truth, but we have to recognize that “truth” is an ongoing project, not a secure possession.

We should also recognize that just as our own observations can be biased, so can those of others. We should be on guard against willful efforts to replace our best approximation to the truth with “alternative facts,” whether these are pandered as outright lies or unconsciously motivated. Nietzsche points out that any claim that is presented as true involves interpretation. Not only should this prompt us to be [intellectually humble](https://iai.tv/articles/nietzsche-ignorance-sets-us-free-auid-2445)—it should also prompt us to question the authority of anyone else who is not. Nietzsche is well aware that getting others to accept your interpretation of things is a means of gaining power over them. He is hardly horrified by the fact that people try to sway others to their views (or their purported views). But he thinks we should arm ourselves against blind acceptance of anyone else’s statements.

Nietzsche is far from defending a radical relativism in which “alternative facts” should be accepted in opposition to evidence. But neither does he think that competition among different perspectives is something to fear. When multiple perspectives are actually based on the evidence, they can add dimension and objectivity to our accounts. That some perspectives contradict the evidence is no cause for surprise, but exactly what one should expect, given the motives we all have to see our situation as favorable to our goals and to benefit from others’ agreement. But that is all the more reason to examine matters more closely.

A more objective account of things can benefit the pursuit of almost anyone’s agenda, for it provides information about what one has to work with. Thus, we all have a stake in trying to know the facts as well as we can, however much we disagree about what to make of them. But we should not take anyone’s interpretation of [the facts](https://iai.tv/video/a-matter-of-facts) as authoritative. We may not have access to any facts that are not interpreted on some level, but we have reason to distrust purported facts when they seem ideally suited for lending support to a given agenda. That may be just what they are, and, according to Nietzsche, there are good reasons for that.
