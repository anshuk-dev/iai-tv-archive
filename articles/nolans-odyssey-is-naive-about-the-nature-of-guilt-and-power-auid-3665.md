# Nolan's Odyssey is naive about the nature of guilt and power

*Odysseus knows nothing of our contemporary moral code*

**By Kathryn Lawson** | *28th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,602 words*

![Nolan's Odyssey is naive about the nature of guilt and power](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/nolans-odyssey-is-naive-about-the-nature-of-guilt-and-power.webp)

*Emily Wilson has critiqued Christopher Nolan's Odyssey for being shallow. Philosopher of ethics **Kathryn Lawson** goes further. Using Hannah Arendt, Emmanuel Levinas and Simone Weil — three thinkers who turned to Homer to make sense of atrocity after the World Wars — Lawson argues that by giving Odysseus a contemporary conscience, Nolan made a dangerous mistake. Homer's Odysseus feels no guilt for his violence which serves as a warning that power rarely recognises its own cruelty and needs something external to stop it. By erasing that for a guilt-ridden hero, Nolan cuts the story’s ethical depth and leaves us with the fantasy that the powerful will change themselves once they understand what they've done. Reality, Lawson argues, suggests otherwise.*

In this summer’s blockbuster movie adaptation of Homer’s epic poem, *The Odyssey,* the key theme is a variation of the New Testament Hebrews 13:2, perhaps best known to literary types as the welcoming words written on the wall of the famous Parisian bookstore Shakespeare and Company:

“Be not inhospitable to strangers, lest they be angels in disguise.”

Writer/director Christopher Nolan’s main theme in the film blends this law of hospitality with the Golden Rule: “Do unto others as you would have done to you,” another biblical invocation, this time in the New Testament’s Matthew7:12 and Luke 6:13. Together, these imperatives form the film’s “Law of Zeus”. Perhaps surprising for a summer blockbuster, the movie is predominantly a meditation on the ethical demands of the stranger, with a similar lesson to Nolan’s 2023 summer blockbuster *Oppenheimer*.

Film critics aside, the movie has had mixed reviews from philosophers and classicists alike. Classicist Emily Wilson [found it to be shamefully written](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v48/n14/emily-wilson/an-uncomplicated-man), although entertaining in a plebeian kind of way, while philosopher Slavoj Žižek argued that it was not the monster blockbuster he expected, [but rather a modest chamber drama](https://slavoj.substack.com/p/odyssey-finally-a-good-chamber-drama). But love it or hate it, it is undeniable that the main theme is a timely reflection upon our age of steadily rising social and political polarization and xenophobia. Here I will not offer a strict “review” of the film but rather invite a conversation between it and the twentieth-century philosophers [Hannah Arendt](https://archive.org/details/hannah-arendt-between-past-and-future), [Emmanuel Levinas](https://archive.org/details/totalityinfinity0000levi/mode/2up), and [Simone Weil](https://archive.org/details/iliadorpoemoffor0000weil), all of whom turned to Homer to consider this very theme during their own dark times. In this conversation between ages, I will consider the quietly hopeful arc of the film and why it ultimately fails to access the true crux of the ethical demand of the stranger.

**Homeric ethics and the necessity of violence**

[For Arendt and Weil](https://fordhampress.com/the-origin-of-the-political-hb-9780823276264.html), Homer’s poem [*The Odyssey*](https://www.emilyrcwilson.com/the-odyssey) and its predecessor, *The Iliad*, are so miraculous because they narrate the war from the perspective of both the conquerors and the conquered. The implication of this narrative choice is that the poet(s) are able to recognize the humanity of those “enemies” in the city they have destroyed. The poem is able to contemplate a responsibility for atrocities without succumbing to the staggering abyss that may follow recognizing one’s own heinous crimes.

In her review of the film, Wilson notes that Nolan’s rendition of the *Odyssey* mistakes ancient for contemporary notions of morality. While the film is not necessarily wrong in its use of contemporary issues, this blending of moral codes is precisely where it loses philosophical depth in the question of the ethical obligation to the other. Arendt distinguishes that for ancient Greece, [violence is justified](https://iai.tv/video/violence-vengeance-and-virtue) in the pre-political world because it is an act through which one can overcome necessity and find true freedom through politics. In this way, the violence against another who lives within a political state is not necessarily justified, but violence outside of that state is often necessary to maintain one’s own freedom. Violence itself is not morally wrong in Homer, and great action does not uphold ethical human dignity as in our contemporary world, but rather aims to rise above the mortal bondage of human life: to have the story of one’s greatness told by the poets from within the political realm.

This distinction of pre-political versus political violence in ancient Greek morality is exemplified by the blinding of Polyphemus, the Cyclops. In Homer’s poem, the race of Cyclopes is depicted as precisely this pre-political being. They lack customs, agriculture, and common laws. As such, Odysseus and his men are arguably [justified in their violent actions](https://iai.tv/video/the-philosophy-of-dehumanisation-with-david-livingstone-smith) against Polyphemus. Nolan’s film emphasizes this aspect further by removing the neighboring Cyclopes and Odysseus’ infamous deceit by claiming his name is “No-man,” thus causing the neighboring Cyclopes to ignore Polyphemus’ screams when he exclaims that “no man” is hurting him.

This excision strips Nolan’s film of an important layer in the text in which these claims of a pre-political Cyclopes come into question: If someone’s community and laws are different from one’s own, is it justifiable to take their food and even to blind them? Monstrous in his inability to practice politics and community, the figure of Polyphemus draws out the ultimate indiscretion of Odysseus through which the hero and his men are cursed by Poseidon. Seen only as a monstrous “it” in the film, Polyphemus is a god in disguise, or at least the son of a god, and thus he represents a missed opportunity to truly consider the film’s main theme of radical alterity.

**Levinas on the other and Odysseus’ violence**

In Levinas’ post-World War II philosophy that struggles to make sense of the horrors of the Holocaust in particular and war in general, he asserts that we find God in other human beings, namely, in the widow, the orphan, and the stranger, or those humans who are dangerously vulnerable and othered. As such, his philosophy deals closely with the central theme of Nolan’s film.

But for Levinas, Odysseus, or the Latinized Ulysses in his reading, is the zenith of one-pointed thinking. He is the true Cyclops of the poem in that he sees the world in a radical sameness.  Odysseus attempts to master everything he sees. He totalizes the world to fit his own perspective, and the end of each journey is a return to his own island, his wife, his rooted olive tree bed. He is absolutely allowed the spoils of war that Nolan, as well as Pasolini in his 2024 adaptation, [*The Return*,](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt19861162/) read as war’s transgressions. In Pasolini’s version of the epic, Juliette Binoche’s Penelope asks a devastated Ralph Fiennes if he, her husband, has [murdered or raped at war](https://iai.tv/video/the-forgotten-crimes-of-war-with-christina-lamb), both silently aware that he undoubtedly has.

Nolan’s Odysseus, on the other hand, seems to have sidestepped much of the murder and rape, and emerges from the horse instantly devastated by his brilliant, terrible idea. Rather than Odysseus, he is more of a Robert Oppenheimer figure, devastated by his wile and the actions it has caused others to perform rather than his own actions in Troy. While the viewer could picture Ralph Fiennes’s Odysseus raping and pillaging alongside his fellow Achaeans, caught up in the anger and desperation of 10 long years of war, Matt Damon’s Odysseus is a good man, immediately remorseful, and uncomplicated in his modern Western ethical responsibilities. But Pasolini too veers from the Levinasian reading because Fiennes is wracked with guilt and deep humiliation for his actions. To Levinas, as to Homer’s poem, Odysseus returns home with no such baggage. He is not ashamed to rape and pillage on his way home after the war if it is done in the proper time and place.

After the killing of the suitors, Odysseus pragmatically calls for the slave girls who have been sleeping with the suitors to clean the palace and then to be murdered. He commands:

Hack at them with long swords, eradicate

all life from them (Book 22, 443-444).

This incident, shocking to our modern sensibilities, is central to Margaret Atwood’s [*The Penelopiad*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/6127/the-penelopiad-by-margaret-atwood/9780676974256). It reveals Odysseus to be the same cocksure No-man who stabbed the Cyclops Polyphemus. The girls were chattel; the Cyclops, a monster. There is something painfully poignant in recognizing the same myopic logic in the “hero” as the poem’s end nears, and this is erased from Nolan’s film.

It is here that we can clarify two key ways that the film misses out on the ethical richness it promises: 1) Matt Damon’s Odysseus is a man of morals, but he largely rejects the gods, and 2) he is self-reflective and aware of his own evils. These two aspects sharply distinguish the film’s engagement with alterity from Levinas, Weil, and to some extent Arendt, and leave the viewer at an ethical impasse.

**An atheistic Odysseus**

Nolan’s movie misses the crucial intervention of the gods to halt the cycle of violence. In the poem, after killing the suitors, their families take up arms, and Odysseus prepares with Telemachus to continue the battle. It is only the intervention of Athena that quells his rage and allows for peace:

“Ithacans!

Stop this destructive war; shed no more blood,

and go your separate ways, at once!” (Book 24, 531-533)

It is not surprising that this aspect of divine intervention is left out of the film, which mostly removes the gods in favour of human psychology. Wilson is correct in her estimation that Nolan wants to “have his cake and eat it” by rejecting the gods in favor of human psychology while embracing them as a tool for action and special effects. The sirens’ song and the lotus flowers act as opioids or alcohol, drugs to numb the hero’s war-riddled senses. Circe reveals the soldiers for what they are in her literal and short-lived transformation of their bodies. Athena appears as a young Trojan priestess rather than a powerful warrior god. Portrayed by Zendaya, Athena adroitly depicts the Levinasian notion of God as the other. But in Nolan’s film, the gods are the stranger that never really reveals herself or himself as a God. Athena’s mournful, haunting presence is more ghostly than divine.

In this rejection of the gods, the film loses a magnificent layer of the poem: that mystery which is beyond human control. That which is not psychological or human-made, but an immortal unknown. Relinquishing the place as the center of all knowing and allowing for the existence of something radically unknown is what Weil refers to as the miracle of ethical encounters, and it requires a leap beyond the self into God.

This God may be the God of the philosophers, namely that which is beyond our own limited comprehension, as in Arendt, who is not a religious thinker yet thinks in a religious structure; the God/ Good of Christianity and Plato as in Weil; the God of the Hebrew bible as in Levinas; or the Greek gods as in Homer. Whatever name one chooses to give the mystery, there is a requirement in each of these modes of thinking to move beyond the self-same I in a way that the film overly psychologizes and thus ultimately rejects.

**Returning Home to the Same**

For Levinas, the true journey of self-reflection begins with the I that “springs forth without returning, finds itself the self of an other…” This requires encountering the radical other without attempting to subsume them into one’s own worldview: the stranger, the slave-girl, the Cyclops must be experienced in their own right. In response and responsibility to the other, one changes. By the poem’s conclusion, Odysseus has not undergone this change. Not only does he continue the cycle of violence, unlike the film, but he spends only one night with Penelope before he leaves again. The seer Tiresias prophesizes that Odysseus must carry a ship’s oar on his back and journey so far beyond the sea that someone confuses his oar for a winnowing fan.

Only then can Odysseus make amends to the God of the sea for his transgression against Polyphemus and return home at last. The reader can assume or perhaps hope that on *this* journey, Odysseus will recognize the other and return home not to himself but to a world transformed by the other. But this transformation is not a given. The reader can hope Odysseus will change but they cannot know for certain. This is in stark contrast to the eminently self-reflective Odysseus of the film.

**The Self-Reflective Odysseus and Weil’s Force**

From Nolan’s film, we have the Odysseus we deserve. As a society, we are stuck in an incredulity that the perpetrators of war and violence could possibly prioritize their own greatness and power over the lives of other human beings. Surely, if these powerful men could understand their violence, they would change. And yet, they do not. And this is ultimately why the film falls short in its ethical imperative: it requires the violent men in power to realize their own culpability.

In [her essay on Homer’s *Iliad*](https://cambridgeliteraryreview.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/weilclr13.pdf)*,* Weil argues that power operates as a kind of force, a force which she claims to be the true hero of the *Iliad*. This force intoxicates those with power and incites them to take more and more. In Weil’s estimation, this can be overcome in the moment when we could hurt another and choose to do otherwise. Weil claims that this difficult choice is a miracle. This miracle is evident in the *Odyssey* when the poem concludes with Athena’s intervention to stop the battle. By giving himself over to the gods, Odysseus performs the miracle that ends his killing spree. [This reading of force in Homer](https://undpress.nd.edu/9780268026042/simone-weil-and-the-specter-of-self-perpetuating-force/) was particularly important for Weil in coming to terms with the Nazi-puppeteered Vichy government in France, and, in translation, Mary McCarthy saw this as essential for Americans grappling with their culpability in the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

From a contemporary moral framework, Nolan’s Odysseus is a figure more like. He is more pious and dutiful than wily and impish. He is long-suffering and a martyr to his people, thinking for the community rather than his own greatness. As a stunned Matt Damon observes the destruction of Troy, I kept waiting for the famous scene of the pious Aeneas to hurry by: his old father on his back and his young son holding his hand, a refugee escaping to one day found the city of Rome. The vision that Damon brings is powerful, but it is more suited to Vergil’s hero.

And so, the film’s reflection on contemporary ethical problems falls short because the shift in the hero’s persona leaves an abyss in understanding the Odysseus of Homer and thus the powerful men violently destroying the world today. The movement from Odysseus to Aeneas impoverishes the ethical reflection of the film. The only hope one can have from the film is that the men in power will recognize the alterity of others and reflect upon their ethical culpability in response. Tragically, such a desire is far more fanciful than any god or goddess. From the standpoint of Arendt, Weil, and Levinas, the hype of the film is not necessarily overblown in its special effects, or acting, or even its writing, as Wilson argues, but rather in turning to what Žižek refers to as a chamber drama without leaving space for the difficult miracle and the terrible truth that men like Odysseus do not seem to find their atrocities to be so very atrocious. The fairytale ending of the film rings shallow, and its intended hope for the future hinges on the fantasy that those wielding force will recognize its harm without a miracle.
