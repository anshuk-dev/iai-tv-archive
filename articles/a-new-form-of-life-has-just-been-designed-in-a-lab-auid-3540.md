# A new form of life has just been designed in a lab

*Nature is no longer the only source of life*

**By Adrian Woolfson** | *7th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,733 words*

![A new form of life has just been designed in a lab](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/AdobeStock-1866075475-copy.webp)

*For most of human history, the origin of life was a religious question. Darwin made it a scientific one. But now scientists have created an artificial species designed entirely by artificial intelligence, inaugurating a new age in which life is a product of human engineering. **Adrian Woolfson** argues that generative biology—where genomes are written rather than discovered—will fundamentally change our lives, transforming medicine, agriculture, and even the nature of evolution itself.*

On Friday, December 18, 1903, the *London Herald* reported that a flying machine had taken to the air the previous day. The Wright brothers, Orville and Wilbur, had made the first controlled and sustained engine-powered flight in their aircraft, the Wright Flyer.

The flight at Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, lasted just twelve seconds, and the aircraft traveled only 120 feet. Yet in that brief interval, the brothers had demonstrated that heavier-than-air flight was possible. In doing so—and in a triumph of the human imagination—they inaugurated the age of aviation.

Twenty-eight years later, Wiley Post and Harold Gatty circumnavigated the globe in around eight days. The subsequent invention of jet engines made commercial flight routine, facilitating intercontinental connectivity, globalization, and mass air travel. Aviation also laid the foundations for the eventual conquest of space.

Nearly a century and a quarter after the first airplane tentatively lifted off from the ground, in an event that passed largely unreported, biology has experienced its own Wright brothers moment. A new and rudimentary form of life has gently bumped off the runway, offering humankind a glimpse of the [vast biological possibilities](https://iai.tv/video/biology-beyond-genes-denis-noble) that exist beyond nature.

At the [Arc Institute](https://arcinstitute.org/) at Stanford, California, on September 17, 2025, Brian Hie and his colleagues—including Samuel King—announced that they had created the first synthetic species designed entirely using artificial intelligence. For the first time in life’s approximately four-billion-year history, evolution by natural selection – previously the only mechanism capable of designing species—had acquired a competitor: [life designed by a computer](https://futurism.com/health-medicine/ai-designed-virus-printed).

We have, as a result, been propelled into a post-Darwinian world that we currently only partially understand and are poorly equipped to navigate. If carefully explored with diligence and caution, it promises substantial benefits and the potential to preserve natural species and habitats. But if mismanaged, it could cause serious harm.

This unprecedented moment represents a phase transition not just for humankind but for the entire history of life on Earth. Hie and his colleagues have devised an entirely new mechanism for generating living things. In doing so, they inaugurated the age of AI-driven genome design. Life will never be the same again. Rather than passively cataloguing species, humankind can now play an intentional role in life’s creation.

Biology is transitioning from a descriptive science into a creative, generative one. Genomes have ceased to be historical records alone and have become a writable language, with humans— assisted by AI—acquiring a new authorial role. While species were once exclusively facts about the world, some are now becoming decisions about the world.

Unlike evolution by natural selection, which lacks foresight and intentionality, this artificial mode of species generation is deliberate, purposeful, and directed. It constructs biological entities according to pre-specified aims.

Rather than being tested solely in the real world, artificially generated species can first be simulated on computers. Following virtual evaluation through artificial selection, preferred versions can be realized in the physical world through human-mediated construction.

While natural selection tinkers with inherited designs—shaped over immense stretches of geological time—the new generative approach allows the reimagination of biological possibility largely independent of evolutionary history. The biological code can be written on a blank slate, much as a programmer scripts a novel piece of software.

Life, in short, is becoming programmable. This represents a significant advance over [gene editing](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-01516-w), which is like red-lining a document rather than authoring a new one from scratch. Authorship frees the AI designer to explore design possibilities that lie beyond nature’s imagination.

The viral entity that Hie and his colleagues created lacked the complexity of a zebra, and could not buzz, whir, or hover like a dragonfly. Nor could it hop like a kangaroo. Strictly speaking, it was not even alive: viruses require bacterial host cells in order to replicate. Yet viruses occupy the hinterlands of living systems, and their genetic information—like that of all organisms—is contained within a genome. In that sense, they maintain continuity with living things.

If AI can design a viral genome, then designing the genome of an ant, a penguin—or even a human—becomes principally a matter of scale. As the French molecular biologist [Jacques Monod](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jacques-Monod) famously stated, what is true for a bacterium is also “true for the elephant.”

The virus designed by Hie’s team may reasonably be regarded as the Wright Flyer of an emerging discipline: generative biology. Structurally simple and not quite alive, it nevertheless points toward the future design of more complex genomes—just as the Wright Flyer foreshadowed jet engines and spaceflight.

The AI used by Hie to design this viral species belongs to a class of systems known as [genome language models](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168952524002956). These employ machine-learning algorithms similar to those used by chatbots such as ChatGPT. The models analyze genomes as though they were written in a language composed of four letters, combinable in countless ways. The apparent simplicity of the genetic alphabet contrasts with human languages, which contain dozens of letters.

Mathematics proved ideal for deciphering physics, allowing the workings of the universe to be captured in elegant expressions. Biology, by contrast, has resisted such reduction, yielding no equivalents to Newton’s laws, Kepler’s laws, or Carnot’s law.

AI, however, offers a new path. By trawling through the vast databases of DNA sequences and detecting patterns invisible to humans, it can begin to extract the grammatical rules of genomes. Each detectable pattern functions like a grammatical rule. Taken together, they constitute the grammar of a biological language.

Hie’s early insights into viral grammar resemble the tentative scribblings of a preschool child or a simple children’s story. Yet our ability to decipher biological grammar is advancing rapidly. We are approaching the point where we may author the genomes of entirely new bacterial species—unequivocably alive, unlike viruses.

To become Dostoevskys or Joyces of biological authorship—to write the genomes of zebrafish, penguins, humans, or entirely novel organisms that have never existed—our understanding of biological grammar must expand considerably.

Biological systems do not resemble human-made machines in a straightforward way. In engineered devices, components perform discrete, largely independent functions. In living systems, properties emerge from interactions among vast numbers of molecular parts.

Despite this complexity, the computational metaphor remains compelling. It hints at the possibility that we may one day master [the language of life](https://iai.tv/video/the-origin-of-life) and transform biology into a predictive engineering discipline.

Designing genomes, however, is only part of the challenge. The designs must also be constructed. That requires genome “printers”—technologies capable of converting virtual sequences into chemical DNA. Such technologies are [advancing rapidly](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-023-01964-9), and it should not be long before we can design and synthesize genomes of human size and complexity swiftly, accurately, and affordably. At that point, the production of living things will become industrialized. Life will have been turned into technology.

These innovations promise to transform healthcare and to become part of our infrastructure, much as steel did in the nineteenth century after [Bessemer’s 1856 invention](https://www.rotabroach.co.uk/blog/how-the-bessemer-process-changed-steel/). By reducing production costs nearly tenfold, steel became a global commodity, deployed in bridges, railways, and skyscrapers, with far-reaching consequences.

Earth’s evolutionary history has explored only an infinitesimal fraction of biological possibility. Just about anything imaginable could be built from biological components. The vast expanse of DNA sequence space contains genomes with transformative potential. Unlike destructive chemical manufacturing, biologically derived materials are sustainable.

Biological materials will be used for almost anything: biocomputing, information storage, bioenergy, biomaterials, agriculture, environmental engineering, biosensing, and healthcare. Food production remains an area of key interest. While gene editing can make small tweaks, the ability to reprogram genomes could drive a new “green revolution”—necessary to address the increasing world population.

The genomes of cash crops such as maize, potatoes, wheat, and rice could be rewritten for drought, heat resistance, pest resistance, nitrogen fixation, and higher yield. This could reduce the need to clear wilderness, helping to prevent species extinction.

Humanity now produces more data every two years than in all previous history combined. Storing information in DNA to archive the world’s information could profoundly benefit civilization, as DNA is one of the most efficient and durable known information-storage mediums. [A single gram of DNA could store petabytes of data](https://www.science.org/content/article/dna-could-store-all-worlds-data-one-room), compactly, stably, and sustainably. It could serve as back-up for human knowledge against potential destruction.

Currently, d**ata centers account for around** [one-tenth](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary) **of global electricity demand growth**. DNA information storage, requiring no ongoing energy expenditure, could reduce environmental destruction and offer profound civilizational benefits.

As our understanding of the human genome’s generative grammar grows, we may reverse engineer the grammatical mistakes that contribute to human diseases and decode the mechanisms of human ageing. [Generative biology](https://iai.tv/video/creating-life-in-a-lab) thus holds the prospect of extending healthy longevity in ways previously unimaginable.

Yet, like fire, gunpowder, or nuclear power, this technology carries [dual-use risks](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-isnt-a-dual-use-technology-it-is-inherently-violent-auid-3515). Safety is paramount to prevent the deliberate design of biohazards, or inadvertent ecosystem collapse. The potential to modify aspects of human nature similarly demands appropriate guardrails.

Humanity must align on a global basis to determine how generative biology can be used safely, responsibly, transparently, equitably, and ethically, for the benefit of society. A guidebook—a manifesto for the future of life—is urgently needed.

When Orville and Wilbur Wright first reported their achievement, it was not obvious that they had created the foundation for a revolution that would reshape humankind. Their [telegram](https://www.loc.gov/item/mcc.061/) to their father Milton Wright on December 17 read simply: “Success four flights Thursday morning all against twenty-one mile per hour wind started from level with engine power alone average speed through air thirty-one miles longest 57 seconds.”

Yet in those few seconds lay the beginning of modern aviation. The construction of Hie’s simple virus—the world’s first computer-designed biological entity—marks the beginning of a new generative biology revolution. It will change the world in ways that we are only just beginning to comprehend.
