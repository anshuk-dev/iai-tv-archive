# Your brain does not perceive reality, it predicts it

*Humans run inference just like AI*

**By Thomas Parr** | *6th July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,355 words*

![Your brain does not perceive reality, it predicts it](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/your-brain-does-not-perceive-reality-it-predicts-it.webp)

---

*We assume perception is a window onto reality as it is. But Oxford neuroscientist **Thomas Parr**, a close collaborator of one of the most highly cited living scientists, Karl Friston, argues that this assumption has the brain backwards. Under the Free Energy Principle, perception and action are the same process: both work to close the gap between what we predict and what we encounter, either by updating our beliefs or by changing the world to fit them. Curiosity, Parr argues, is as fundamental to behaviour as reward — we act not just to get what we want, but to know our world better.*

While reading this article, you are engaging in active inference. With each movement of your eyes, you select the sensory data that best resolves uncertainty about what will be said next. Those visual impressions are synthesized with your brain’s [internal world model](https://iai.tv/articles/anil-seth-the-hallucination-of-consciousness-auid-2525) to form letters, words, sentences, and ultimately meaning. As you hear a sound in the background and turn your head to localize where it has come from, you act to provide your internal model with the information you need to update your percept. In short, perception and action are part of the same process.

The graphic below helps to make the active nature of perception intuitively clear. If you fixate on the cross in the center of the picture, and try not to move your eyes, you will perceive the colours in the periphery fading away. As soon as you allow your eyes to move freely again, the colours re-emerge. This underlines that our world appears the way it does because of the way we choose (consciously or unconsciously) to sample it. Of course, the way we choose to sample the world also depends upon the way in which we perceive it. This reciprocity between action and perception is [active inference](https://direct.mit.edu/books/oa-monograph/5299/Active-InferenceThe-Free-Energy-Principle-in-Mind).

Active inference is (almost) synonymous with the Free Energy Principle—a theoretical principle [developed by neurobiologist Karl Friston](https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/~karl/The%20free-energy%20principle%20-%20a%20rough%20guide%20to%20the%20brain.pdf) to explain how our brains work. It has roots in the work ofnineteenth-century polymath Hermann von Helmholtz, draws from advances made in the twentieth century by physicists including Richard Feynman and Nikolay Bogolyubov, and builds upon the same mathematics used at the turn of the century by pioneers of modern artificial intelligence. While highly influential in psychology and neuroscience, the principle has taken on a much broader role in the life sciences. It is difficult to think of a theoretical programme in biology, since Darwin, of similar scope and ambition.

This level of ambition excites both enthusiasm and scepticism, and rightly so. The Free Energy Principle has been celebrated for its simplicity and criticized for its complexity. Advocates point to the unifying power of the framework, facilitating a common language to solve apparently disparate problems. Critics argue that the principle is ultimately unfalsifiable and therefore scientifically futile.

**Simple or complicated?**

At its core, the Free Energy Principle is remarkably simple. It proposes that we can understand the brain, or other living systems, in terms of an imperative to behave to [keep their variational free energy to a minimum](https://iai.tv/video/how-brains-construct-reality-with-karl-friston). Free energy is a concept, originally from physics, which turned out to be closely related to quantities in probability theory and statistics. In a biological setting, it can be understood as a measure of how surprising it would be for a particular kind of creature to encounter a particular sort of sensation. For instance, while it might be unsurprising for a fish to have the sensation of being completely submerged underwater, it would be very surprising to find a giraffe experiencing the same sensation. There is something uniquely relational about this, in that surprise depends upon the relationship between a creature and its world.

Another interpretation of free energy is as a measure of how well (or, more accurately, how poorly) the internal model our brains hold about our world accounts for—or predicts—our sensations. Internal, or generative, models of this sort have gained much publicity in recent years with the advent of generative artificial intelligence. They are central to the Free Energy Principle and Active Inference. This perspective is useful in that it lets us think about free energy as a measure of prediction error. How much does the world deviate from our predictions? It also immediately tells us how we might go about reducing our prediction error.

If the world is not as we expect, we can do two things. We can change our expectations—our implicit beliefs about how things are—or we can change the world. We call the former “perception” and the latter “action,” but both serve the same purpose in suppressing prediction errors. This is the power and simplicity of active inference. We can understand everything that a sentient system does in terms of a single unifying imperative: it must minimize the free energy in its relationship to its world.

So why is the Free Energy Principle thought to be so complicated? Partly, it is due to an inversion of previous thinking in cognitive science. An analogy from physics is the generalization of Newton’s equations of motion by Hamilton and others. In place of writing down a set of dynamical equations that represent—for instance—the movement of a planet based upon the gravitational force applied to it, we can understand planetary motion in terms of deeper symmetries. In place of asking which dynamical equations a given system obeys, we start from the conservation of energy and asks which dynamics must be in play for this conservation to occur.

Hamiltonian and Lagrangian approaches to the same mechanics start from the more fundamental energetic quantities that are conserved in our universe and determine the path a dynamical system will follow to ensure that this is so. This generalization afforded further advances by applying the same energetic quantities in different domains—including the scales at which quantum mechanics operates—in which they are still valid but in which Newton’s equations are not.

The Free Energy Principle does something similar for systems (like us) that are both coupled to their environment and insulated from it. It offers a similar energetic quantity—one predicated upon the relationship between us and our world—from which we can derive the dynamics of the (possibly unconscious) beliefs we hold about the causes of our sensations. There is a powerful (non-Cartesian) duality to this. In studying neural matter, we can understand the equations governing the activity of networks of neurons from the perspective of the scientist measuring them *and* from the perspective of the nervous system itself measuring an external environment.

In taking the brain’s perspective, we invert late twentieth to early twenty-first century thinking in neuroscience, in which one builds complex percepts from simple building blocks. Previously, we could understand visual perception by noting that neurons in our primary visual cortex are sensitive to the orientation of short line segments. Combining the responses of lots of these neurons, the secondary visual cortices then include neurons that are sensitive to longer lines. These can be combined further to give shape to sensitive neurons—a process that continues, one step at a time, until reaching the (in)famous [“Jennifer Aniston” cells](https://www.newscientist.com/article/dn7567-why-your-brain-has-a-jennifer-aniston-cell/).

While there is some mileage in the approach of building solutions to problems in this way, [the Free Energy Principle turns this around](https://iai.tv/articles/reality-is-a-creation-of-consciousness-auid-3365) and starts from an understanding of the problem—the structure of the generative model—and looks for a solution that emerges from this. From here, we can reframe visual perception as the problem of explaining the causes of patterns of activity across our retina. The connections along our visual pathway are therefore understandable in terms of the communication of predictions and errors between causes at different spatial scales. This makes sense of the fact that these connections are bidirectional, with information communicated in both directions, which we might not predict from earlier feedforward accounts.

**Principles, theories, and hypotheses**

Objections that the principle is not directly amenable to experimental verification or falsification should be taken seriously, as they are entirely correct. However, they also depend upon a category error. This is clearest when we attempt to apply the same critique to other ideas in science. There is no experiment that could disprove the principle of natural selection. Similarly, there is no finding that would raise objections to Hamilton’s principle of least action. Both are essentially tautologies. Evolution by natural selection is the process by which genetic material, in a species capable of reproduction, becomes represented with greater frequency from one generation to the next when it increases the chances of individuals with this material reproducing. This is entirely circular. Similarly, if we understand “action” in the physics sense, as a measure of the implausibility of a trajectory, Hamilton’s principle just says that the most likely path is that which is least implausible. Again, this is clearly definitional.

This is not a criticism of either natural selection or Hamilton’s principle. It would be hard to do without either of them in modern science. This is because each is a principle and only becomes a workable theory once an extra ingredient is introduced. In the case of natural selection, when we specify the form of reproduction, the representation of genetic material, and the environmental conditions in which competition amongst individuals occurs, we can develop a theory with meaningful hypotheses concerning specific evolutionary trajectories and seek evidence in the form of fossil records and molecular genetic analysis. If we are prepared to commit to the form of the Lagrangian potential function, the principle of least action immediately brings us to predictions of measurable paths.

The Free Energy Principle is the same. On its own, it does nothing. Equipped with a hypothesis about the form of the generative model, it allows us to make wide-ranging predictions about everything from behavior and perceptual experience through to neuroanatomy and electrophysiology. In short, it is not an answer, but a way of posing questions, which themselves are answerable to empirical data. In other words, when we observe a behavior that needs explanation, we must ask what form the generative model takes for that behavior to reduce free energy. As a physician, I find this element particularly appealing. This is because, while we might traditionally ask “why does the behavior I observe in a patient appear maladaptive?”, we instead ask the more person-centered question: “what would I have to believe for this behavior to be optimal?”

**Looking ahead**

While we have touched upon the idea of a generative model, and its influence on behavior, in an abstract sense, to gain a more complete understanding of the ideas underneath the Free Energy Principle and active inference, there is an additional element we must consider. For our internal models to guide our behavior, and to fully explain our sensory data, they need to account for the impact we ourselves have on our world and our vantage point in it. This is particularly important in the context of planning ahead and deciding what to do in the future. Active inference treats planning as the problem of modelling our future selves. The plausibility of alternative futures must therefore be an element of our internal models.

So, what makes a future version of ourselves more plausible—thereby driving behavior to achieve it—than an alternative? A simple answer to this is that we expect ourselves to behave in such a way as to become more proximal to a goal state. This is the idea that implicitly underwrites ideas like reinforcement learning, itself inheriting from Pavlovian conditioning experiments (i.e., repeated pairing the sound of a bell and food leads animals to salivate when they hear the bell). In a sense, this sort of goal is definitional of reward, in that rewarding stimuli are things we act to obtain.

However, this is only part of the story. We can also be driven towards certain kinds of future based upon intrinsic motivating factors. Chief amongst these is curiosity. As creatures with beliefs, one of the aspects of our future selves that we can model is the change in our beliefs over time. In sampling different kinds of information, some futures will involve us becoming more confident about our world relative to others. Some appeal to the metaphor that our brains are scientists, taking actions that play the role of experiments, testing our internal hypotheses.

Active inference combines these imperatives—those driven by something we want in the external world, and those driven by wanting to improve our internal model of that world—giving equal weight to the two. It goes beyond reward-driven behavior in that it treats information as intrinsically valuable. Perhaps for this reason, there has been a recent expansion of active inference into the (similarly initialled) artificial intelligence space. In both corporate and academic settings, there is ongoing work into the use of brain-inspired engineering, derived from the Free Energy Principle, to develop more intelligent machines.

While I have focused on the fundamental theory of active inference in this article, it is worth highlighting that there is a wealth of [theoretical](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27870614/), [empirical](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5884328/), and [philosophical](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-predictive-mind-9780199682737?cc=gb&lang=en&) research on this topic. It has been transformative in many areas of neuroscience. A particular interest of mine is in its [clinical](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/26/10/833) applications, in understanding the inferential pathologies that underwrite neurological syndromes. This sort of approach is very active in the field of Computational Psychiatry. This would require (at least) another article to unpack. However, I hope the mechanics of beliefs outlined here will spark the creativity in readers to explore even more novel and exciting ideas.

By looking ahead, we have returned to where we began this article. Action depends upon perception, and perception on action. With each word you read, your brain selects between future versions of yourself that do or do not know what the next word will be (preferring the former). The errors induced by the difference between now and your anticipated future are resolved by changing your world through rotating your eyes to fixate on the next word. In doing so, you resolve uncertainty—through resolving prediction errors along your brain’s visual cortices—and [improve your world model](iai-academy/courses/info?course=predicting-ourselves), ready to decide upon your next future self.
