# Farage deputy Richard Tice: "Some of our policies... are a form of socialism"

*Reform UK deputy leader tells all in exclusive interview*

**By Richard Tice** | *4th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,365 words*

*Right-wing populism is surging across the West, from Trump’s election to Reform UK’s rise, to the spread of nationalism in Europe. In this challenging interview, Reform’s former leader Richard Tice, now deputy to Nigel Farage, is pressed on the philosophical foundations of the movement, challenged to locate it on the political spectrum, and asked whether the historical parallels with national socialism are dangerous. Interviewed by philosopher Hilary Lawson.*

**Hilary Lawson: Where do you think Reform fits on the political spectrum from right to left? Your critics often describe you as** *“***far right.**” **Would you agree with that description?**

Richard Tice: I think [the old labels of “left” and “right” are a bit tired](http://iai.tv/articles/the-age-of-left-right-politics-is-over-auid-2993), in the same way I think people are tired of the traditional nature of politics. Historically, we’ve had a two-party system largely dictated by the first-past-the-post voting system. But if you look at what’s going on at the moment, all of a sudden you’ve got the two main parties struggling in their vote share compared to historical trends. We’ve emerged seemingly out of nowhere in the last two or three years, and now we’re leading in the national polls. The Liberal Democrats’ vote share has increased. The Greens are doing well. You've actually got five parties that people are looking at and talking about.

**Reform is in favour of the nationalization of a number of essential services: electricity, water, and steel. This doesn’t tie in with your traditional libertarian views about encouraging capitalism. How do you make sense of the two together?**

Through that fundamental philosophy of making people’s lives better.

It’s really important to touch on the public ownership point, a term I will use over *“*nationalization.” I come from a business background, where I’ve done lots of collaborative partnerships and joint ventures. Take the example of water, which is not a competitive market—you can only buy it from one place. Maybe you could have 50% public ownership, 50% owned by long-term, patient capital. But the critical thing is that it is managed by some of the best people in the private sector. That is a model that we haven’t really had in the UK. But I know it can work.

\_\_\_

We’re just completely overregulated, and it dampens down the application of good, old-fashioned common sense.

\_\_\_

**But traditional liberal economics promotes international competition to increase efficiency.**

And it's absolutely failed. The people have been shafted, no ifs, no buts. If you fully privatize water companies without a proper regulatory framework, run by competent regulators—if you allow Chinese billionaires to own all our electricity transmission network and our gas networks—you're going to end up in a bad place.

**So is liberal economics wrong**?

It clearly hasn’t worked here. If you look at all our bills, they’ve gone through the roof. Water companies have ripped out a whole load of dividends, a whole load of high shareholder interest on shareholder loans. And look at the state of our water companies. Look at the state of our rivers. It hasn't worked.

I believe in free markets and competition, but it’s got to be smartly regulated. Not overregulated—smartly regulated. You need to make work pay and risk-taking pay. But don’t let monopolies, like utilities and steel, rip the customer off.

**It sounds as if you’re in favour of free markets, so long as it’s not billionaire Chinese capitalists?**

If it’s a free market, where you can have competition. You can’t have competition on the electricity infrastructure under London, or on the gas transmission network. That’s not competition—that's one pipe. If you allow that to be owned by a greedy privateer that leverages it with huge amounts of bank debt, strips out as much money as they can, and doesn’t properly maintain it, we’re all the poorer for it. That’s what's happened with Thames Water.

**What do you mean by “****smart”****?**

Smart is where you incentivize risk. You have a sensible regulatory framework. You’ve got to have rules, of course, but not so that it burdens people to the point where they give up on creating businesses or employing others. And that’s unfortunately where we are at the moment. We’re just completely overregulated, and it dampens down the application of good, old-fashioned common sense.

**Do you think that means we need a substantial state, not a small one?**

I am in favour of a small, smart state. A state that makes work pay. Too many people are now trapped on benefits because it doesn’t pay to work 40 hours a week. That’s a failure of the state to properly regulate and organize the way that people remunerate and the way the tax system works.

**Do you think that organizations like the BBC have been fair to Reform and your policies?**

I think now, given the success that we’re having, they’re rapidly catching up. Would certain people at the BBC rather we didn't exist? Of course. And that’s fine. I think a couple of years ago, no, they probably weren’t fair. We’ve just worked hard and ignored it. Occasionally, we made a bit of noise, but at the end of the day, it’s about your performance.

\_\_\_

But all of a sudden, over the last 15 to 20 years, we’re not allowed to be patriotic. We’re not allowed to fly our own flag. As for the media, because the mainstream media have got it wrong, competitive elements have emerged

\_\_\_

Where are we on that journey? I’ll probably get criticized for this, but we’re like a 21-year-old. We've come through the teenage years, the rapid growing up. We’re in early adulthood as a political party. Of course, we have been around the block—I’ve run big, successful businesses. We’ve got some good ideas. We think that we can do better than the two failed parties that have made things worse. But equally, I will accept the response that 21-year-olds like to think they know everything, but they don’t. The challenge now is to actually get stuff done and start delivering.

**Populist parties across Europe often get less mainstream coverage, partly because some see them as promoting dangerous aggressive nationalism. Do you think that’s why there’s resistance to Reform?**

I don’t accept the characterization of aggressive [nationalism](http://iai.tv/articles/nationalism-and-the-left-roy-casagranda-auid-2408). I think being patriotic about the country that one is born in and grows up in is a fine thing, whichever nation across the world you're born in. But all of a sudden, over the last 15 to 20 years, we’re not allowed to be patriotic. We’re not allowed to fly our own flag. As for the media, because the mainstream media have got it wrong, competitive elements have emerged. So you’ve had the emergence of GB News, Talk TV, Talk Radio. All of a sudden, they’re growing and lots of people are watching. Then, of course, you’ve got podcasts, you’ve got blogs, you’ve got all sorts of other things, and I think that competitive element is great.

**There’s no question you’ve shifted the political gravity, on immigration especially. But your critics are still nervous because of historical fears attached to** [**populist nationalism**](http://iai.tv/articles/against-the-end-of-history-alexis-papazogolou-auid-2729)**.  Do you recognize that there are concerns there? How do you avoid your policies bleeding in that direction?**

I know that my policies will make ordinary folk better off and make the place work better. Fundamentally, that is the role of any government. And bluntly, if you don’t make people's lives better off, don't be surprised if they vote for someone else. And with the greatest of respect to the establishment, they are 0.5% of the population. And if we're talking the language and concerns of people out and about—that the establishment never gets to talk to—then maybe we’re more in touch with ordinary folk than they are. And we understand what’s really going on. We understand what works and what doesn’t work.

\_\_\_

It's interesting you use the term “national socialist.” Some of our policies, in terms of public ownership, are one form of socialism. I've got people on the right of politics criticizing me, thinking that I'm becoming a lefty.

\_\_\_

**To his credit, Nigel Farage sought to get rid of the sort of followers who express explicitly express racist positions from the party. So it’s not as if you're not aware of the risk here. What do you do about that risk?**

We’re all aware of it. Racism in any form is completely unacceptable. But in any large entity, you end up with someone who says or thinks or does something daft. Whether it’s the Green Party having issues with aggression against the Jewish community, or the anti-Semitism that was rife in the Labour Party **–** these are all horrific experiences. Every party has to deal with them. We’ve had a few instances, and of course, we deal with them. There are some people that I made clear, and Nigel’s made clear, who we want nothing to do with. That’s who we are, and we put a very clear marker down about it. But equally, we should be criticized. Every political party should be criticized. That is the role in a democracy. And God, wouldn’t life be boring if we all agreed with everything and there was only one party?

**Nevertheless, your critics are nervous. Is there a sense in which you think appealing to nationalism has its risks?**

The word populism is a label—it’s a bit naughty. But, actually, what does it mean? It means you’re popular. It means lots of people like what you stand for and what you talk about. So, I'm not afraid of the word populism. If the establishment doesn’t like that and tries to label it and smear it, that’s their problem.

**Some critics raise concerns about your policies, suggesting a risk that the party could resemble a national socialist movement. How do you ensure this combination of nationalism and elements of socialism doesn’t get out of hand?**

I don't think there is a risk at all, because we know who we are, and we've got a successful track record. People who think or act inappropriately, if it's proven, are out.

It's interesting you use the term “national socialist.” Some of our policies, in terms of public ownership, are one form of socialism. I've got people on the right of politics criticizing me, thinking that I'm becoming a lefty. I tell them that, in this particular instance, it doesn’t work, and that's what we're trying to do. I'm just saying what works, applying some common sense, and taking a bit of the best of everything.

**Another concern is about the party becoming overly dominant around a single leader**. [**Rupert Lowe**](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn4qzj22d8xo) **has made those criticisms of Nigel Farage. Is this a risk?**

Let’s pick up that word, risk, because you’ve mentioned it a few times. The biggest risk in life is never taking one. You don’t make progress if you sit in your bedroom and don’t take some risks and create some value. I’ve known and successfully worked with Nigel for, give or take, ten years in a variety of different entities and campaigns. I’m a football fan. I’m a happy Liverpool fan at the moment. And what you need is a team of people who act as a team. Eleven individuals won’t win the Premier League. Eleven people who act as a team under a good, strong manager can win things, whether it’s the matches, the Premier League, or elections. So I think you need a strong leader. Weak leadership never works. We all know that in any walk of life. A bad boss can ruin a good company in two years, but a good boss can turn it around in two to three years. That’s where I see the political scene at the moment. The fact that we achieved what we did—we’ve grown to 400 branches, we stood more candidates than any other party—that’s a combination of hard work and some great people under Nigel, and under the chairman Zia Yusuf.

\_\_\_

If the Labour government starts to deliver real economic growth, cuts the cost of living and immigration, and stops the boats, then they’ll win again. But I don’t think they’ll do that, and that’s why we exist.

\_\_\_

**Rupert Lowe’s criticism was that there isn’t room for democracy within that framework. That Nigel is too dominant, and there isn’t a genuine variety of views.**

You have your debates, your discussions, your disagreements, your brainstorming behind closed doors. Whether it’s in a business community, whether it’s in a football team, or in a political party. But then, when you’re out there on the pitch in front of everybody, you act as a team. Ultimately, someone has to make a decision, and that’s the boss. You listen to the advice, then the boss has to make a decision. We all put forward ideas to Nigel, and he’ll agree with some, but he won’t agree with others. That’s the nature of it. With Rupert, I’ve said before it was a shame, but sometimes things happen, and we parted company. Yes, it’s sort of ugly—it’s in the public domain. But at the end of the day, the local elections prove that, frankly, the electorate is not happy with the two main parties. They’re delighted at a new competitor emerging. They’re saying, “Rupert who?” We’ve got momentum. Momentum builds momentum. Success breeds success. But now we’ve got to perform. We’ll get lots right, and we’ll make some mistakes. As long as we’re honest about the mistakes, don’t try to cover them up, learn from them—I think people will recognize that.

**Are you going to be in government in four years?**

[We've got a cracking chance](https://news.sky.com/story/latest-polling-says-if-an-election-was-held-tomorrow-reform-uk-would-win-a-majority-13386754). You have to be an optimist in politics. If the Labour government starts to deliver real economic growth, cuts the cost of living and immigration, and stops the boats, then they’ll win again. But I don’t think they’ll do that, and that’s why we exist.

**And if you’re in government**—**Chancellor of the Exchequer?**

That’s not for me to decide—that’s for the leader to decide. I’d be delighted to be in government and to try to help out. I’ve got some strong views about how things should be done. My background is in the world of business, and I understand the energy market. So those are the natural places to use what I think are our skills and talents. Others might say: forget it.
