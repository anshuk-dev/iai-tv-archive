# The self does not exist without other people

*Tolerance is a truce, not peace*

**By Hamedeh Rastaei** | *20th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,247 words*

![The self does not exist without other people](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-self-does-not-exist-without-the-other6.webp)

---

*In a world riven by ethnic and religious conflict, we’re often urged to tolerate difference, as though tolerance alone could sustain peace. But Iranian philosopher **Hamedeh Rastaei** warns that tolerance by itself is dangerously unstable, since it merely buries conflict, which is liable to resurface at moments of strain. Far better, Rastaei argues, to recognise the deep metaphysical truth that the identity of our self depends on the difference of others. This idea can support a more stable peace, as rival identity groups can come to see each other not as threats but as mutual foundations.*

An inevitable boundary exists between the Self and the Other. I am “I” precisely because I do not possess the characteristics, beliefs, and lifestyle of the Other. This difference—whether at the individual or collective level—erects a boundary between us that, in the initial encounter, provokes defensiveness. By virtue of being different, the Other appears at first glance as an “alien” and a “stranger.” This boundary, though natural and unavoidable, is inherently violence-inducing.

It might seem that our encounter with the Other is not always one of conflict or mere tolerance; in romantic or affectionate relationships, for instance, the Other is embraced and differences are celebrated. Yet, even in such bonds, the fundamental difference between two subjects does not dissolve; rather, [love](https://iai.tv/articles/time-to-abandon-the-falling-in-love-fairytale-auid-3694) makes tolerating these differences possible, pleasant, and meaningful. However, in structural, social, and discursive domains, this encounter typically reduces to one of two primary paths: fighting or enduring.

1. **Fighting** can take various forms, ranging from indifference and exclusion of the Other to overt violence. In this mode, difference is perceived as a threat that must be eliminated or subdued. Yet historical experience shows that this path fails to destroy the Other; instead, it frequently unleashes a cycle of violence and retaliation. Even if violence appears victorious on the surface, it inflicts long-term damage upon the individual and the group itself, as the Other inevitably responds in kind.

2. **Enduring** involves striving to accept and tolerate difference. Although this path appears more humane and ethical on the surface, it faces serious challenges. First, merely preaching tolerance is often superficial and ineffective, failing to uproot the deeper causes of violence. Second, even if it halts overt violence, it may subject the individual to persistent psychological strain and internal conflict, as the difference remains an unresolved, alien element in the mind. In this state, a person or group must continue to wrestle mentally with their disparities and disagreements with the Other, struggling to ignore or suppress them without ever attaining genuine peace. Thus, tolerance here is not a serene acceptance, but an ongoing internal struggle that drains significant psychological energy and preserves latent violence within the deeper layers of human relations.

Thus, the boundary between [the Self](https://iai.tv/video/your-true-self) and the Other is not only inevitable, but also inherently violence-inducing. In confronting this boundary, one of two paths are often taken: either to fight the Other and eliminate or control difference, or to endure the Other and ignore difference. The question now arises: Is there a third way that transcends this dichotomy? Emphasizing our shared humanity is not a third way on a par with fighting or enduring. Such an emphasis becomes possible only once the identity of “I” and “we” has already been formed in distinction from the Other. Even the statement “we are all human” presupposes a “we” and a “them.” Commonality, then, does not erase that fundamental difference; it rests upon it.

So if there is a third way, it does not lie in ignoring difference. To find it, we must examine difference from the Other at a deeper level. At the metaphysical level, my identity is constituted through difference from the Other. Insofar as I am an “individuated” being, I require an “Other” from whom I differ. Any non-self is an “Other” in relation to me, and this very difference provides the condition of possibility for my individuation and personal identity. Therefore, dependency on the Other is not an accidental or dispensable attribute, but the fundamental condition of possibility for my identity. Without this boundary, I would be devoid of identity.

This dependency is not confined to the individual level alone; collectively, [our identity](https://iai.tv/articles/you-dont-need-an-identity-auid-1811) is also constructed through the Other. By collective identities, we mean our relation to the Other on a sociological plane. By virtue of numerous universal categories, each individual belongs to various groups and embodies diverse collective identities; for example, one may simultaneously belong to the group of academics, men, citizens of a nation, or adherents of a religion. For each of these collective identities, those who do not share it constitute one’s “Other.” Consequently, [collective identity is formed not in a vacuum](iai-academy/courses/info?course=to-see-the-one-that-sees-you), but always in relation to—and across a boundary with—the non-self. In today’s world, this dependency is manifested even more prominently: national, ethnic, gender, and religious identities are frequently defined in opposition to the “Other,” and this very opposition turns the boundary between Self and Other into a violence-inducing frontier. Yet, these are identity boundaries, and if we take this dependency seriously, the Other is not merely an external, tolerable entity, but an integral part of the conditions of possibility for our individual and collective identity. In this light, the encounter with the Other can transcend the “endurance of a violent boundary” and rise to the “recognition of dependency.”

Recognizing dependency means viewing difference from the Other not as a threat to one’s identity, but as an indispensable element in its constitution. In this view, the boundary between Self and Other remains, yet it is no longer merely a source of violence; rather, it becomes the locus of encounter and mutual dependency. In more concrete terms, an individual can only determinately and distinctly state “I am American (or Iranian, French, etc.)” if non-Americans exist to define this boundary and distinction; without other nations, no specific national identity would emerge. Neither I without the Other, nor the Other without me, can attain a determinate identity. This recognition transcends moral and political dimensions and reaches the metaphysical level—a domain where dependency on otherness is understood not as a deficiency, but as the condition of possibility for our individuated existence.

This is precisely what Emmanuel Levinas articulates so beautifully: we do not achieve identity in solitude and isolation. Levinas refutes the long-standing assumption that a human being can exist on an island of solipsism and still possess identity. For him, the “Other” is not a replica of ourselves, but a genuinely distinct and independent reality. A person breaks out of a self-centered, enclosed existence (the ego) and transforms into a real, living “I” only when confronted with the “Other”—[an identity born solely in relation and response to the Other](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-94-015-7906-3). In the contemporary world, where individual and collective identities are often defined in opposition to one another, this recognition carries profound implications. Many of today’s political and cultural conflicts stem from a failure to recognize this mutual dependency. If we regard difference merely as something to be tolerated, structural violence remains latent. However, if we recognize it as the very condition of our identity, we might move beyond the cycle of violence and superficial tolerance. For instance, in contemporary [identity struggles](https://iai.tv/video/the-rise-and-fall-of-identity-politics)—from ethnic and religious clashes to [political polarization](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-and-how-to-debate-your-enemy-drew-douglas-johnson-auid-2829)—what is often missing is the understanding that “our” identity cannot form without the existence of “them.” Recognizing this dependency offers an alternative foundation for intercultural dialogue: a dialogue no longer built upon eliminating or merely tolerating the Other, but grounded in mutual acceptance of the inescapable dependency of identity.
