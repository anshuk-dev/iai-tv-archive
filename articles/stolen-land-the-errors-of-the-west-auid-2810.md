# Stolen Land: The Errors of the West

*Indigenous ways of being in Western European law*

**By Dale Turner** | *30th April 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,748 words*

![Stolen Land: The Errors of the West](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/24-04-15-indigenous-thought-and-stolen-land.webp)

---

*Who owns which land, possibly surprisingly and tragically in the 21st century, remains a central and violent contemporary question. With almost all of North America's native population decimated after the European invasion, Dale Turner argues we must honour indigenous languages, storytelling, and deep-seated connections to the land. His call to action highlights the urgent need for a different approach to resolving land disputes, rooted in cultural understanding, reconciliation and a revolution in modern law.*

My mother, Ann, is English. She was born in Exeter and raised in Plymouth until she was 18. Then, she headed to Canada to experience living in another part of the world. She joined the Canadian Air Force and my father, Ron, an Indigenous man from a small Anishinaabe community in northern Ontario. My parents’ personalities are as different as the cultures that they came from. I turned out to be a curious amalgam between my introverted British mother and my extroverted Anishinaabe father, of which I am only now beginning to make some sense.

My father made a career in the military, and we ended up living in various places around Canada and the United States, the average posting lasting about three years. Despite this itinerant lifestyle, I grew up returning to my grandfather’s home on an island on Lake Temagami, Ontario. My grandfather, whose generation was the last to speak [Anishinaabemowin](https://lctlpartnership.celta.msu.edu/what-is-anishinaabemowin/) before speaking English, lived most of his 86 years on and around the lake. The territory “on and around the lake” consists of fourteen traditional family territories of the [Teme-Augama Anishinaabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teme-Augama_Anishnabai), whose homelands of close to 10,000 square miles our people call “N’dakimenan”. While spending my summers with my grandfather during my teenage years in the 1970s, our young Chief, Gary Potts, along with a small group of community leaders, including many of our respected Elders, was pursuing the resolution of a claim that began with the implementation of the Robinson-Huron Treaty in 1850 (of which we were unknowingly included). Our argument was simple: we did not sign the Robinson Huron Treaty in 1850, and therefore, we still considered ourselves to be the rightful owners of [n’daki menan](https://temagamifirstnation.ca/about-us/). This belief eventually led to Chief Potts registering a “land caution”, a legal device that froze all development, until the courts could settle the issue of who possessed legal title over the ten thousand square miles of N’dakimenan.

The language we used to understand and articulate who we were as Anishinaabe had evolved into an uneasy relationship between Anishinaabemowin and English – our self-understandings were rooted in our Anishinaabe traditions yet, if we were to win our case in Canadian courts of law, these explanations had to be articulated in English.

\_\_\_

In other words, I grew up around a very unpopular Indian land claim that lasted for over two decades. The first court ruling, decided in 1984 in the Ontario Supreme Court, concluded that we were not an “authentic” Indigenous culture because we no longer spoke our language in our everyday lives, nor did we think of ourselves as a “traditional” Indigenous people – in fact, most of us had converted to Catholicism. The court decided upon the criteria for what constituted an authentic Indigenous culture, and since we did not meet that standard, we were not able to make a claim to Aboriginal title over our homelands. The court also ruled that the province and the federal government had failed in their fiduciary responsibilities and that we would need to renegotiate the terms of our treaty relationship with the Canadian state. I was in my mid-twenties at the time of this unjust biased decision, which unified our community and brought us together to discuss our legal and political options. It was at these gatherings that I learned more about the history of our land claim, the fourteen family territories of N’dakimenan, and the traditional Anishinaabe ways of thinking about the world. Meanwhile, our leaders were balancing a hostile ongoing negotiations process with the various levels of government with a highly public media campaign to discredit and characterize our leaders and community as anti-Canadian and against any kind of resource development in the Temagami area.

This community involvement highlighted the fact that when the Treaty was signed in 1850, translators were required to get the Chiefs to sign the Treaty. Over the next 50-60 years, there was an unfettered period of sometimes brutal encroachment on N’dakimenan on several fronts: by logging and mining companies, by private land ownership in the form of tourist camps and individual property ownership, by the extension of the railway and roads through our homelands to the north, and the growing presence of the Catholic Church. The result of this rapid shift in physical displacement meant that, within two generations, most of our people were forced off of N’dakimenan onto a three-square-mile island in the centre of Lake Temagami called Bear Island. This was to become our reserve. The physical dispossession of our traditional homelands was devastating to our social, economic, and spiritual well-being. However, one of the most powerful forms of assimilation was to make the English language the first language of the Teme-Augama Anishinaabe.

The language we used to understand and articulate who we were as Anishinaabe had evolved into an uneasy relationship between Anishinaabemowin and English – our self-understandings were rooted in our Anishinaabe traditions yet, if we were to win our case in Canadian courts of law, these explanations had to be articulated in English. I remember believing at the time that all Indigenous people needed to do was to produce our own lawyers: the common law itself was capable of listening to and accommodating our explanations. An Indigenous legal community could create the right – empowering – language in a dialogue between our knowledge keepers and practitioners using the normative language of the common law. I concluded, what better way to participate in this anti-colonial project than to study philosophy and go to law school?

I never made it to law school. This is largely because I underestimated the significance of what it means to be philosophical, especially in contemporary Indigenous politics. How we understand what it means for human beings to be philosophical can be a way of seeking political change in the world; but more importantly, for how we understand Indigenous ways of thinking and being in the world.

I have now spent several decades working in Indigenous politics and education, as a professor at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire, USA, and now at the University of Toronto, but also as a consultant in several government and community initiatives. Over the past few hundred years, two important working assumptions have become deeply embedded in Canadian legal and political practices that drive Indigenous politics in Canada. The first is that from the federal government’s perspective, the sovereignty of the nation state is supreme and immutable. Canadian state sovereignty is never up for negotiation in their relationship with Indigenous peoples. At the end of the day, the state can recognize and assert that Indigenous peoples possess the international right of self-determination, and affirm it in a document like the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), because its meaning, in practice, is that whatever self-determination means to Indigenous peoples, these Indigenous self-understandings cannot be, as the final article of UNDRIP, Article 46 states unequivocally, a threat to the unity of the state.

\_\_\_

This faith in the ability of the common law to solve Indigenous legal and political problems has evolved into the recognition of the moral worth of Indigenous ways of knowing and being in the world.

\_\_\_

The second working assumption is that individual rights lie at the foundation of a constitutional democracy. In Canada, Aboriginal rights are protected in the second part of our Constitution. Canadian courts are willing to recognize the existence of Aboriginal rights if they can be explained and justified within a philosophical framework grounded in the sanctity of individual rights. Canadian courts have been able to constrain the meaning of Aboriginal rights by classifying them as a form of cultural right and not as a fundamental political right.

These two working assumptions keep Indigenous peoples marginalized and oppressed in Canadian society. As our court case showed, and as every Supreme Court decision since 1991 has shown, contemporary Indigenous politics has not changed much since the signing of the Robinson-Huron Treaty in 1850. My motivation to study law to empower Indigenous peoples in Canadian society has proven to be limited in its scope. This faith in the ability of the common law to solve Indigenous legal and political problems has evolved into the recognition of the moral worth of Indigenous ways of knowing and being in the world. In my view, the consequence of this shift in attitude has created a movement in Canadian politics to recognize Indigenous law as a third order of law in Canada (along with the English common law and French civil law traditions).

The question of belonging lies at the heart of what it means to be Anishinaabe, but also what it means to be human.

\_\_\_

Indigenous communities have embraced two interconnected political and philosophical projects. The first project is that our survival as Indigenous peoples requires us to continue to participate in the legal and political practices of the Canadian state but to do so “in and on our own terms”. This complex and difficult proviso can be nurtured by the second project, which is to find ways of changing the general public’s attitudes about who Indigenous peoples are and why our ways of knowing and being in the world matter. My contribution will be to this second project. I am currently writing a story about an Anishinaabe boy, Allie, who loves to read, and ultimately becomes a writer, who reflects on the form of discovery in moving from his ancient world to the new world of Cambridge University. This story draws from my community’s experiences over the past few hundred years in dialogue with the venerated Western European philosophical tradition. However, once he leaves the island and experiences the unknown new world in and on its own terms, he returns to N’dakimenan with a radically different sense of belonging. The question of belonging lies at the heart of what it means to be Anishinaabe, but also what it means to be human.
