# The end of the West means even more than you think

*Trump threatens 2000 years of Western thought*

**By Andy Owen** | *5th March 2025* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *3,093 words*

![The end of the West means even more than you think](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-end-of-the-west-is-more-than-you-think2.webp)

---

*Donald Trump’s policies have upended relations with Europe, with many commentators declaring “the end of the West.” But the end of the West is not limited to geopolitics. Andy Owen argues that the end of the West means the end of a more than 2000-year in the making system of thought: liberal humanism. Liberal humanism has as its features a quasi-religious faith in progress, the power of reason, universal human rights, the moral primacy of Western-style democracy, free markets, and the unbounded potential of technology. This system of thought has its roots all the way back in Ancient Greece. We could be witnessing it end today. Andy Owen here argues for what should replace it.*

The international rules-based order that was born from the ashes of the first atomic bombs is fragmenting. The former US secretary of state, Antony Blinken, described this order as: “The system of laws, agreements, principles and institutions that the world came together to build after two world wars to manage relations between states, to prevent conflict, to uphold the rights of all people.” This notion of an “international order”, long seen as absurd in much of the developing world, now seems undefendable by even the most optimistic western diplomat. In his nascent second term, President Trump is accelerating a longer process of the collapse of this order and the more recent post-Cold War American hegemony. Trumpism is accelerating us towards a world where brute force matters more than international law, and how much things cost matters more than shared values. The US is still militarily and economically strong, but the geopolitical system it once supported is increasingly closer to anarchy than order.

\_\_\_

We are now also witnessing a fragmenting of "liberal humanism" the dominant set of Western ideas as to how we should live, leading many to question some fundamental "truths" that Western society, or certainly the English-speaking countries within it, are based on.

\_\_\_

We are moving into what many leaders are calling a "multipolar world", where states cluster around three or more global powers, motivated by survival and mutual benefit rather than ideology. I believe a better description is a “multi-matrixed” world, where states are developing relationships with different partners, who often do not share their values and can even contradict them, on matters of security, trade, and energy. These relationships can be with states or multinational corporations, and partners in one area or on one issue can be direct competitors or adversaries in another. Throughout history periods of transition have created uncertainty and violence. Wars tend to concentrate at these times. This period is unlikely to be different.

This fragmenting, though, is more than the breaking up of political alliances and international norms. Whether justified or not, those states who policed the international order supposedly set a moral example of how to best organise a society within a nation-state. We are now also witnessing a fragmenting of "liberal humanism" the dominant set of Western ideas as to how we should live, leading many to question some fundamental "truths" that Western society, or certainly the English-speaking countries within it, are based on.

Liberal humanism can be defined as a quasi-religious faith in progress, the power of reason, universal human rights, the moral primacy of Western-style democracy, free markets, and the unbounded potential of technology. It also contains a hubristic belief that it is the best single way in which human beings can organise themselves. It is a utopian ideology that looks forward to a time when all societies are democratic, prosperous, and part of a homogenous global system. It is a secular idealism that replaced but never fully escaped the Christian notion of salvation after the Enlightenment, with the belief that our institutions and wider society are progressing in moral terms, replicating the religious story of sin and redemption.

\_\_\_

While technology has advanced, the moral progress of our institutions has not followed. A backlash was inevitable.

\_\_\_

As there have been long-term processes that have contributed to geopolitical fragmentation, over the final decades of the last century and the first of this century, ideas from philosophy, including Jacques Derrida’s deconstruction and Critical Theory which emerged from the Frankfurt School, have questioned and deconstructed key tenants of liberal humanism and the hierarchies of Western society. Deconstruction attacked metaphysics and the idea that there is a realm of truth that exists prior to and independently of its representation by our language and the other signs with which we communicate. On the continent, several prominent philosophers, including Emmanuel Levinas, attacked the idea of Logocentrism, the Western tradition of science and philosophy that regards language as an elemental expression of an external reality. Critical Theory argues that knowledge, truth, and social hierarchies are fundamentally shaped by power dynamics between the oppressed and the dominant, most of which we have long been oblivious to. These ideas have seeped into mainstream thought.

As these ideas have entered the mainstream, having been stripped of some of their nuance and depth, they have created a form of hyper-liberalism. Western societies have experienced unprecedented atomization and a rejection of shared notions of truth and transcendence. Individuals have been told they are sovereign beings, able to define their own identities independent from any external reality, but this focus on identity and individualization has ironically created new collectives, based on identity categories, which while attempting to better represent the full spectrum of human flourishing, has fostered division. The resulting culture wars have provided a distraction from the growing failures of the dominant brand of market capitalism, which include increasing inequality and environmental degradation. The gap between what liberal humanism promised and what it is now delivering is too vast for any illusion to sustain it. While technology has advanced, the moral progress of our institutions has not followed. A backlash was inevitable.

This backlash has been exploited by Trump and other right-wing politicians across the West who mask their self-serving politics by positioning themselves as bulwarks against the fragmenting of Western values. The utopias these new preachers evangelize about are now located in some unspecified time in the past, before the fragmentation, rather than the future. Those still looking forward ignore the fact that our uses of science and knowledge are as fleeting and crooked as we are ourselves, and pin hope of salvation on technology. They put their faith in the God-like abilities of AI or promise redemption in virtual worlds. Science can lift man’s eyes up to the stars, but humans will use what they know to satisfy their most pressing needs and desires—even if the result is ruin. While today’s techno-optimists may eventually develop the technology to get us to Mars, the techno-totalitarian colonies they found are unlikely to give us the belonging and meaning that our society on our current planet isn’t providing.

\_\_\_

Tragedy teaches us that there is much out of our control. Mortal actions are subject to forces that transcend us.

\_\_\_

As much as Europe needs to reassess and reset its security relationships to cope with new geopolitical realities, a wider renaissance in how we think about the world we find ourselves in is needed. The ideas that provided the foundations of liberal humanism are part of one of the richest traditions of thought about what it is to be human the world has produced. There is much there to return to, to enlighten seemingly darkening times. A good start would be returning to the body of thought whose re-discovery sparked the Enlightenment: the wisdom of ancient Greece. A key set of treasures within this that perhaps best describes the world we now find ourselves in are the first written stories in Western culture – the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*– and the Greek tragedies. They describe a world in constant conflict defined by capriciousness, ambiguity, uncertainty, and unknowability, where people are often swept along by forces they interact with but don’t fully comprehend. They also then suggest how best we might approach such a world, pointing us to a form of both political and individual realism.

One of the reasons the tragedies, and Homer’s the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, are still so relevant is that humans, despite advances in technology and material wealth, are still driven by the same conflicting needs and illusions. There are many ways open to us to express our needs and organise ourselves, but we are limited by the same *h**amartia*, or basic human vulnerabilities.

These ancient stories show us that tragedy is not the triumph of evil over good, but the suffering caused by the triumph of one good over another. Tragedy is about morally defensible but incompatible goals. In Aeschylus’ *Seven Against Thebes*, brothers Eteocles and Polynices have killed each other in combat. Afterwards, their uncle, Creon, the new king of Thebes, declares that Eteocles will be honoured, but Polynices is a traitor and will be left on the battlefield and denied burial rites. Creon believes this punishment is necessary for the stability of the state and avoiding future bloodshed. In an act of disobedience, Antigone declares she will bury her brother out of loyalty to her doomed blood. After burying him, she asks: "What shall happen to me? What shall I do?" The chorus divides into two groups. There is no reconciliation.

Tragedy teaches us that there is much out of our control. Mortal actions are subject to forces that transcend us. Characters in the tragedies are pawns in the petty squabbles of the gods. They attempt to defy prophecies, but events outside their control, or the unintended consequences of their actions, see the inevitable fulfilment of what is foreseen. Similarly, we live today in a supposed democratic meritocracy, yet our actions are subject to the invisible gods of market forces and algorithms. We can find ourselves pawns in the petty squabbles of autocratic leaders.

\_\_\_

This recognises that there is a plurality of human values that determines many ways of living, and these values – and those that hold them – will inevitably clash.

\_\_\_

But at the same time, within the confines of fate, we are free to make choices – this is how our destiny plays out. In perhaps the finest of the tragedies, Sophocles’ *Oedipus Rex,* Oedipus’ fate is not just decided by the choices he makes, but also the interaction of forces outside of his control interacting with his choices.

Before Oedipus is born, a curse is laid upon Laius, his father, for violating the sacred laws of hospitality. An oracle tells Laius he "is doomed to perish by the hand of his own son." He orders Oedipus’ murder, but instead Oedipus is left on a mountaintop, where he is rescued by a shepherd. Years later, when Oedipus has just been told by another oracle that he will kill his father and have sex with his mother, he lashes out at the crossroads and kills a stranger not realising he is his father. Unintended consequences of individual choices fulfil prophesied events.

Despite the curse and the prophecies, Oedipus’ rage gets the better of him. As with Achilles' rage in the *Iliad* caused by a perceived slight by Agamemnon, characters are driven on not by reason, but by concepts such as honour, glory, greed, and wrath. The Greeks show us that killing and dying for concepts that defy reason, for often nonsensical ideas, is how many human beings have made sense of their lives. This is something those trying to negotiate peace today must not forget; the unreasonable nature of our species is something we should all be aware of in our day-to-day relations.

Fate requires Oedipus’ partially conscious complicity to bring about its truth. Similarly, our freedom is compromised by the weight of the past, which so many of us look to deny in our constant thirst for the next thing. We are all shaped by our own pasts, our childhoods, our parents’ experiences, and collective group experiences. Tragedy teaches us that if we do not realise this and we try to disavow the past, then at best, we will be destined to repeat what has come before, and at worst, be destroyed by it. The ancient stories place these dilemmas in rich historical and social contexts, where the past weighs down on the present, with everyone aware of the social networks in which they interact and the resulting duties and responsibilities these entail. This contrasts with the ahistorical approach taken by the hubristic planners of the invasions of Iraq and Afghanistan that tried to force an alien political construct on ancient cultures they made little effort to understand or the architects of the homogeneous globalized economy that uproot industries and move them round the world severing social and economic links in place for generations.

\_\_\_

While you seek non-existent definitive answers, big and small, you may also miss the wonder in the uncertain present.

\_\_\_

In the *Odyssey* the concept of *xenia* or guest-friendship is central to much of the plot. *Xenia* is not created by proximity and kinship (*philia*) but by a set of behaviours that create bonds between people of different geographies and cultures. Both are important and they are not mutually exclusive. *Xenia* was how unrelated elite families could connect as equals without the need to turn to war to assert dominance. It is an abuse of *xenia* that sets all the events of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* in motion when the guest Paris elopes with his host’s wife, Helen. *Xenia* can be seen as a basic first attempt at international law. When *xenia* is abused or absent (as it was by Laius in *Oedipus Rex*) violence and conflict follow. This lesson from ancient Greece has never been so relevant.

Faced with the reality that there are moral and political dilemmas for which there are simply no solutions that the Greek tragedies reveal, philosopher John Gray, influenced by Isaiah Berlin, proposes an approach of co-existence and compromise they call *modus vivendi*. This recognises that there is a plurality of human values that determines many ways of living, and these values – and those that hold them – will inevitably clash. It is the search for a way of living together despite this, embracing the multiple forms of human life as a good thing in itself. Gray, who provides an extensive criticism of liberal humanism in his *New Leviathans*, recognises that there is no end of history and conflict will always play a part in maintaining the uneasy equilibrium in which our competing societies and ideologies find themselves. He encourages us to try to end our ceaseless striving for meaning in absolute answers and live more comfortably with doubt. If you view conflicts as black and white, with one side being unequivocally right and another wrong, it leaves little space for *modus vivendi*. Such binary thinking can blind you to the suffering of others and lead you to justify the unjustifiable. Derrida uses a Greek word – *aporia* – to describe the philosophical impasse in which we should try to become comfortable.

While you seek non-existent definitive answers, big and small, you may also miss the wonder in the uncertain present. It is the sensation of life that you lose when you focus on some overarching purpose or commit to finding answers in ideologies and religions. Despite their beliefs in gods and the afterlife, the ancient Greeks were focused on what one does in this world, in the here and now. When Odysseus visits Hades in the *Odyssey* he is approached by the masses of the dead. Speaking with Achilles he is told those that have crossed over the river Styx are “Numb dead people… the shades of poor exhausted mortals”. The afterlife is no substitute for the vividness of life itself.

\_\_\_

It may feel like we are at a threshold, crossing over into the unknown. At such times, those promising simplistic certainties seem attractive. However, history makes more sense as a cycle than as a straight line of progress and the job of philosophy within these cycles is to try and make the intolerable tolerable without the need for illusion.

\_\_\_

The stories we create give us a sense that we are part of something greater than ourselves, which will continue after we die. Whilst the tragedies focus us on what we do in life they recognise our need to feel connected to those who have come before us and those who will follow us. These Greek stories have been re-told and reinterpreted for millennia. One of the most innovative storytellers of our time, movie director Christopher Nolan, is currently working on his version of the *Odyssey,* joining James Joyce, the Coen brothers, Margaret Atwood, and many other artists from many different ages in giving their interpretation of events described in Homer’s tale. Several Greek tragedies including two recent stages of *Oedipus Rex* and a punk version of Sophocles’ *Elektra* have had recent runs in London’s West End. To share in them connects us to those who have shared them before and those who will reinterpret them in futures we cannot imagine.

A great story can, for a time, take us out of the every day and give us that perspective of the infinite. The contemplation these great stories provoke can be an antidote to our ceaseless striving and to the vertiginous news cycles and the flood of social media outrages. Specific ideologies and individual leaders come to pass. The human being remains driven and limited by the same desires and fears.

It may feel like we are at a threshold, crossing over into the unknown. At such times, those promising simplistic certainties seem attractive. However, history makes more sense as a cycle than as a straight line of progress, and the job of philosophy within these cycles is to try and make the intolerable tolerable without the need for illusion. Philosophy should hold up a mirror to us. In that mirror should not just be those alive today but all the shades of exhausted mortals. This mirror should show us that it is the precariousness and fragility of life, and the complex connections of our historical and social context, that give it its majestic, precious urgency. It should show us that meaning can be created by treating human life with dignity even when it is flawed and even in times of conflict. Grace in a human being means embracing the poetic truths revealed in the tragedies and facing the unknown with an openness to the multiple possibilities of imperfect human flourishing.

As European leaders rediscover their geopolitical responsibilities and pull together to navigate new realities it is time for us to resist the false promises of certainty, rediscover the foundational stories of our culture and the timeless realities they reveal, and embrace the aporian life.
