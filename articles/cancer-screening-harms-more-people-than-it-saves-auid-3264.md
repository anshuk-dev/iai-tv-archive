# Cancer screening harms more people than it saves

*We screen. We worry. We don’t live longer*

*When leading physician and health policy researcher H. Gilbert Welch reviewed 30 years of data from a landmark cancer screening trial, he was stunned: screening hadn’t helped people live longer. Not even a bit. In this article, Welch delves deep into the data to show that vested interests seriously overplay cancer screening’s benefits, while its harms are real and too often ignored. Rather than saving lives, it’s more likely to draw healthy people into unnecessary medical interventions. The massive amounts of money we spend on it would be better spent on improving treatments and healthcare for those who really are unwell.*

Does cancer screening help us live longer, or live better? A 30-year [follow up of one of the most influential trials of cancer screening](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1300720)—a trial that was a central reason for starting colorectal cancer screening in the US in the 1990s—produced a stunning result. Screening hadn’t helped people live longer. Not even a little bit.

The trial involved 50,000 individuals aged 50–80, who were randomly sorted into one of three groups: annual screening, biennial screening, and no screening (the control group). The results, 30 years on, are summarized in two graphs.

![chance of corectal cancer image 1](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw1NTNd/chance-of-corectal-cancer-image-1.webp)

Adapted from Shaukat A, Mongin SJ, Geisser MS, et al. “Long-term mortality after screening for colorectal cancer.” *N Engl J Med*. 2013; 369(12):1106–1114, with permission from the Massachusetts Medical Society.

The first shows the chance of colorectal cancer death over time. Note that in all groups, screened and not screened, people continued to die from colorectal cancer. That’s because screening can’t help with the fastest growing, most aggressive cancers (what I called the “birds” that have already escaped the barnyard in the [previous article in this series](http://iai.tv/articles/50-of-some-cancers-diagnosed-by-screening-are-better-left-untreated-auid-3257)). But the curves for the screened groups rise more slowly than the curve of those not screened. At the end of 30 years, 2% of those screened annually had died of colorectal cancer, whereas 3% of those not screened had died of colorectal cancer (with biennial in-between). In other words, annual screening reduced the rate of death from colorectal cancer by 1 percentage point—or a 33% reduction.

![Long term mortality after screening for colorectal cancer copy](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw1NTNd/Long-term-mortality-after-screening-for-colorectal-cancer-copy.webp)

Adapted from Shaukat A, Mongin SJ, Geisser MS, et al. “Long-term mortality after screening for colorectal cancer.” *N Engl J Med*. 2013; 369(12):1106–1114, with permission from the Massachusetts Medical Society.

The second graph contains the result that stunned me when I first saw it a decade ago. It depicts the proportion of the trial group who died for any reason (including colorectal cancer)—that is, the overall mortality. You might be surprised by how common death is, but recall the participants would have been between age 80 and 110 by the end of the study. The stunning finding is that the three curves are right on top of each other; in fact, it looks like one curve. That means the overall rate of death was exactly the same in each year. At the end of 30 years, every curve ends at 71%. People who were screened had the same overall chance of death as people who were not screened at every point in time.

\_\_\_

Screening’s effect on longevity is either nonexistent or so small that it is not evident in 50,000 people followed for 30 years.

\_\_\_

How can that be? Perhaps the few who avoided a colorectal cancer death because of screening nevertheless died around the same time from something else. The [aging soma hypothesis](https://www.cell.com/cancer-cell/fulltext/S1535-6108(20)30477-3) postulates that developing an aggressive cancer is a marker for generalized biological frailty. In other words, people likely to die from cancer are also likely to die from something else.

Or perhaps screening caused some deaths. A lot happened to people in the screening group (follow-up tests, procedures with nasty side-effects—particularly for the elderly), and a lot of it happened to the many people not destined to die from colorectal cancer. A small increase in death among the many could erase the benefit to the few destined to die from colorectal cancer in the screening group.

Or perhaps it’s the role of chance: while one might expect the overall death curves to end at 70% for annual screening and 71% for no screening—to reflect that 1 percentage point difference in colorectal cancer death—by chance it didn’t play out that way. Whatever the reason, screening’s effect on longevity is either nonexistent or so small that it is not evident in 50,000 people followed for 30 years.

In fact, the only cancer screening test shown to actually increase longevity has been [lung cancer screening in heavy smokers](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1102873). We have known for years that [cigarette smokers are at exceptionally high risk for lung cancer death](https://www.bmj.com/content/2/5001/1071); they are 10–20 times more likely than non-smokers to die from lung cancer. Those who are at exceptionally high risk for cancer death stand to benefit the most from screening, simply because cancer death is a major component of their overall mortality.

But for the rest of us screening is a long run for a short slide. The purpose of screening is to reduce the chance of dying from a specific cancer. But most of us cannot benefit, because most of us are not destined to die from cancer. So doctors have to screen many, to potentially benefit a few. How few? For most of us, the chance of dying in the next ten years from any given single cancer [is less than 1%](https://knowyourchances.cancer.gov/big_picture_charts.html). That means 99% go through screening for nothing.

Actually, far fewer than 1% of those screened benefit. The problem is the birds. These cancers grow so fast and are so aggressive that they have already spread by the time they are detected in screening. Screening can’t help with the birds; the question is whether treatment can. And increasingly it can. While screening promoters like to take credit for falling cancer death rates—by pointing out how long people survive with small cancers diagnosed by screening (and ignoring the deceptive effect of overdiagnosis)—the genuine good news is that our treatments for cancer are getting better. [Declines in US cancer mortality](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsr1905447) over the last few decades are largely the result of improved treatment and less cigarette smoking—not screening.

What I called the “rabbits” in [the previous article](http://iai.tv/articles/50-of-some-cancers-diagnosed-by-screening-are-better-left-untreated-auid-3257) can be caught early. They are the cancers that screening can potentially help. But even here, in some cases as treatment improves, the value of finding slow-growing cancers before they cause symptoms is waning. Women with slow-growing breast cancers, for example, do equally well whether their [tumor is small or large](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa1510764)—whether their cancer is caught early or late, that is. Doctors are learning that tumor biology, host response and treatment effectiveness are far more relevant to cancer prognosis than the time of diagnosis.

Multi-cancer screening has been promoted as a way to increase the chance that screening saves your life. Researchers at the private healthcare company GRAIL, for example, have postulated that their screening test for 50 cancers would reduce the death rate for [all cancers combined by 26%](https://aacrjournals.org/cebp/article/30/3/460/72416/Modeled-Reductions-in-Late-stage-Cancer-with-a). Were that true, screening would almost certainly increase longevity since cancers make up roughly one-third of all deaths. Alas, it is almost certainly [not true](https://www.bmj.com/content/386/bmj.q1706)—and claims that screening will “save tens of thousands of lives” look to be nothing more than hype.

Even if the benefit of screening is small at best, maybe you’d still give it a go if you were confident there are no downsides. But there are massive downsides. Overdiagnosis and overtreatment are the most consequential harms of screening. While overdiagnosis is common among those diagnosed with cancer via screening (see [my previous article in this series](http://iai.tv/articles/50-of-some-cancers-diagnosed-by-screening-are-better-left-untreated-auid-3257)), it is thankfully not that common among those who are screened.

The far more frequent harm is a false alarm (formally known as a false positive screening test) and it is important to distinguish this from overdiagnosis (see table). It starts when screening identifies some “concerning” abnormality. This initiates a cascade of events: scheduling more appointments, more tests, and maybe a biopsy before someone recognizes that it was all a false alarm. False alarms can be extraordinarily common with regular screening: almost [half of American women](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2790521) who undergo a ten year course of annual screening mammography will have at least one. It almost feels like a “cancer scare” has become a rite of passage for American women.

This all reminds me what a mentor used to say: it’s hard to make a well person feel better. But it’s not that hard to make them feel worse.

Here’s my personal calculus with respect to prostate cancer. I know I could die from prostate cancer, but I also know I am far more likely to die from something else. Prostate cancer screening does lower the risk of prostate cancer death, but only a little bit. And just like colorectal cancer, it has [no effect on longevity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4427906/). For a 70-year-old male like me, the risk of prostate cancer death is about 1% in the next ten years and screening might reduce that by one-quarter, to 0.75%. So, if I’m screened, then the chance that I avoid a prostate cancer death *because* of screening is 0.25%.

We have overstated the benefits of cancer screening—it’s not at all clear that it helps people live longer or live better. And we have understated its harms—drawing many more people into the system and exposing them to medical intervention.

\_\_\_

That’s a small benefit. Maybe I’d go for it, if it were costless: if it took no time, no money, no aggravation. But screening takes all three. Plus, there are a lot of ways to lose. There is a non-trivial chance—5 to 20%—I would have an [abnormal screening test](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa0810084) and need a biopsy. Prostate biopsies are done through the rectum: they are literally a pain in the ass. Most abnormal screening tests are false alarms; that is, the biopsy will be negative. But about one-third of the time, [prostate cancer will be found](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article-abstract/99/18/1395/923744). That will initiate a lot of activity. Urologists understand the overdiagnosis problem better than most doctors, so they will appropriately raise the question: Is this a cancer that should be treated or watched? I figure I lose either way—surgery or radiation is no picnic, neither is watching and waiting (so-called active surveillance).

More likely to be a loser than a winner? For me that’s a no-brainer: I’ll pass. That’s the deal with screening: many must be tested to potentially benefit a few, yet all who are tested can experience the harms. We have overstated the benefits of cancer screening—it’s not at all clear that it helps people live longer or live better. And we have understated its harms—drawing many more people into the system and exposing them to medical intervention. While screening began with the best of intentions—when cancer treatments were ineffective and all abnormalities meeting the pathologic criteria for cancer were believed to portend a relentless progressive disease—now we understand the heterogeneity of cancer and have better treatments.

The strongest case for screening is the simple stuff: you ought to know your blood pressure and your newborn ought to be screened for rare metabolic disorders (it’s the law). But we are well beyond that now. I’ve focused on cancer screening, believing that if you understand the problem in this most feared of diseases, you’ll understand the general problem created by looking for abnormalities in asymptomatic people. Similar arguments apply to screening for heart disease, dementia and musculoskeletal disease, among others. General population [screening for heart disease](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2208681) using multiple tests did not reduce death, for example. Even in a high-risk population, patients with diabetes, screening [stress tests](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/183751) and [coronary CT scans](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1936754) produced no benefit.

The truth is screening is not very helpful, yet can be genuinely harmful. And it has huge opportunity costs for both individuals and society. Governments and healthcare systems should not throw money at promoting and implementing [high-tech ventures](https://www.bmj.com/content/386/bmj.q1706) looking for things to be wrong in the well. Far better to concentrate on better treatment for the sick.
