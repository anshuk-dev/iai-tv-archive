# The maths on renewable energy isn't adding up

*Solar and wind can't do what nuclear can*

**By Jan Emblemsvåg** | *8th May 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,363 words*

![The maths on renewable energy isn't adding up](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-maths-on-renewable-energy.webp)

---

*In December 2022, Germany watched wind and solar energy grind to a near-halt for weeks, at a cost equivalent to building an entire nuclear power plant. Engineering Professor **Jan Emblemsvåg** goes head-to-head with M.V. Ramana in the IAI today, arguing that Germany's example refutes Ramana’s [recent claim](https://iai.tv/articles/the-hype-behind-nuclear-energy-doesnt-match-the-reality-auid-3415) that a move to nuclear energy would not solve climate change. The anti-nuclear case rests on an accounting trick: comparing an energy source that works around the clock with ones that only work when the wind blows and the sun shines. If you take this into account, the numbers tell a very different story, one where nuclear energy is the only way to solve our climate crisis.*

Professor M.V. Ramana [claims in these pages](https://iai.tv/articles/the-hype-behind-nuclear-energy-doesnt-match-the-reality-auid-3415) that the hype behind nuclear energy doesn’t match the reality and that nuclear energy will not solve climate change. He argued that the actual trends in the energy sector speak against nuclear energy. Professor Ramana attributes these trends to the superior economic performance of variable renewables compared to nuclear energy. But his entire economic argument is based on comparing apples with oranges.

Comparing intermittent and variable energy sources directly with nuclear energy without compensating for performance differences is tantamount to comparing an internet service that is available only in good weather to one that is operational 24/7. Financial advisory and asset management firm Lazard has received a lot of criticism for its [“apples-to-oranges” comparisons](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/energy/lcoe-lazard-misleading-nuclear) because important life-cycle costing principles such as reliability, availability and maintainability (RAM), used in correct cost calculations, are ignored. Indeed, most reputed [organizations fail to produce correct cost estimates](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214629624004882) because they ignore these RAM principles.

The German energy transition, dubbed *Energiewende* in German, is an excellent example. Regardless of how you look at it, [three different cost calculation approaches](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2024.2355642) show that Germany would have ended up with significant savings—and cuts in greenhouse gas emissions— if they had used nuclear energy rather than investing in variable renewables (solar photovoltaics and wind power), which their *Energiewende* is currently based on.

Professor Ramana might be right if we were only looking backwards, but believing that the future is merely an extension of the past is too simplistic. Henry Ford [is credited with saying](https://hbr.org/2011/08/henry-ford-never-said-the-fast) that “If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.” While it’s unlikely that he ever actually said that, the point is that innovations are not merely extensions of the past. Innovations are outliers, [as Hayek said](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781351313087-22/theory-complex-phenomena-hayek). Hence, they are found at the fringes of the possible solutions that exist at a given moment in time. In other words, most solutions will cement the status quo rather than change it. Once these fringes are accepted, change is initiated, and what was once a novelty becomes mainstream.

That’s historically how energy transitions have worked: they have rarely been planned; they started as outliers. Those who failed to understand the situation were likely to face severe problems through misplaced investments and financial losses. For example, in the late 19th  century, the [Norwegian shipping industry](https://www.dagbakka.no/eboker/cross-traders-tanker-tycoons-and-nation-builders-1/part-1-up-to-1905) failed to embrace steam ships in favor of sail ships, resulting in a painful restructuring and huge losses as sail ships clearly lost out to steam ships that were three times more productive. They failed to understand the potential of the new technology and got hung up on its initial limitations—their response was to build better sailing ships. What Professor Ramana fails to understand is that the decline of nuclear energy is more a result of politics than of techno-economic drivers.

If things were as easy as Professor Ramana suggests, why did the sunny United Arab Emirates build a 5.6 GW nuclear power plant when it has abundant sunshine? The simple fact is that you cannot establish advanced or energy-intensive industries with variable renewables. The case of the Melkøya liquid natural gas terminal in northern Norway—where a decision had to be made between wind power and nuclear to decarbonize the industrial site—is instructive. On paper, using average and cumulative numbers, wind power seems to be a good idea. However, when [all effects are accounted for](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14786451.2023.2260008), wind power will actually cost twice as much as two small modular reactors (SMR). The wind power plants required would span an area the length of Germany, and, even then, energy shortages are expected for 1500 hours per year.

In all fairness, Professor Ramana *does* recognize to some extent the performance differences between variable renewables and nuclear energy, but he has no conception of the consequences of using his “complementary technologies to compensate for variations in output of solar and wind farms.” While he is rightfully concerned about the costs of nuclear power in Western countries, he believes that “energy efficiency, demand response, technological and geographical diversity, and some storage” will be sufficient. Again, such ideas have already been studied, and the realities really do not support the hype.

Energy efficiency takes decades to make a serious dent in demand, and typically, due to [Jevons’ Paradox](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0921800905001084), such measures do not lead to reduced demand but more efficient usage, and an increase in absolute consumption due to the falling unit cost. Demand response—where consumers are incentivized to shift or reduce electricity use at times of peak demand—is a good idea until you realize that electricity has become as fundamental as water and other infrastructure. In reality, demand response has proven difficult and has minimal impact on the big picture, although it can in principle add some positive effects. A [survey of more than 1500 companies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421519306573) in Germany shows that only 4% knew about demand response and would consider using it.

Technological and geographical diversity also sounds good until you look at the facts. The problems are so prominent that the Germans have invented a separate term to explain the problems—*Dunkelflaute*,or “dark wind doldrums.” A *Dunkelflaute* is an extended period (from a few days to a couple of weeks) with little to no wind and solar power generation, coupled with low sunlight, often during winter with cold weather. Germany has tripled its power system capacity, but in December 2022 a *Dunkelflaute* rendered both wind power and solar panels effectively useless. With the cost of failing to provide energy in the European electricity trading market at [€30–180 million per hour](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421525004173) for a two-week period, the total cost is about €35 billion on average, or approximately the cost of an entire nuclear power plant. This corresponds to an average residual demand of more than 240 GWh/h for two weeks, or 80 TWh in total.

Large-scale storage is an even worse idea. The investment cost is $125/kWh, according to [Ember](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/how-cheap-is-battery-storage/). Scaled to handle a quarter of the 80 TWh short in a *Dunkelflaute*, this amounts to something in the region of $2500 billion in investment for the European electricity trading market. That corresponds to more than 35 [Hinkley Point C nuclear power plants](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/latest/hinkley-point-cs-cost-climbs-to-35bn-with-confirmation-unit-1-will-power-up-in-2030-20-02-2026/), which Professor Ramana rightfully considers to be very expensive. On top comes the crucial fact that Hinkley Point C has a design life of 65 years, whereas grid batteries now have a design life of 20 years. Note, too, that nuclear power plants routinely outlast their design life by 20 years or more. For batteries, however, the risk of fires can reduce lifespan severely, as illustrated by Moss Landing Energy Storage Facility in California, which was [damaged due to fire](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/02/13/1111843/battery-fire-moss-landing-power-plant/) in early 2025.

Hence, it is easy to agree that Hinkley Point C and similar projects are too expensive, but to argue that the solution is a massive buildout of variable renewables supported by energy efficiency, demand response, technological and geographical diversity, and some storage has no root in economic realities. The EU subsidizes variable renewables to the sum of  [€80-90 billion per year](https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/32d284d1-747f-11ee-99ba-01aa75ed71a1), while nuclear energy receives only  €4-5 billion per year, despite [nuclear being the single largest power source](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/european-electricity-review-2026/) in the EU. Moreover, the EU must invest another  [€2000–2300 billion](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/eu-power-grid-needs-trillion-dollar-upgrade-avert-spain-style-blackouts-2025-05-05/) in the power grid to prevent blackouts like those in Spain. In fact, [as shown in these pages](https://iai.tv/articles/nuclear-waste-heat-can-turn-deserts-into-farmland-auid-3500), the current climate policies are highly inefficient on a global level, which is what counts, since moving emissions around the world adds no benefits. Moreover, current climate policies will take about 200 years to complete and cost $100 trillion. Nuclear can cut that in half, both in time and costs.

Therefore, it makes far more sense to address the cost overruns in nuclear power plant projects, rather than hope for something we have no empirical basis to believe will be an effective climate measure at a reasonable cost. Indeed, the issues are already known; various types of [soft issues](https://news.mit.edu/2020/reasons-nuclear-overruns-1118), such as complex regulations, contracting problems and political interference rather than technological issues, are the primary reasons why nuclear construction projects in Western countries have fared so poorly. All of this is solvable.

The big question is whether small modular reactors will improve this situation. Interestingly, Professor Ramana knows that there are 127 designs under development, yet he chooses to address them as a group. This hides extremely important differences concerning designs, applications and risks—most of these will succumb to these issues, but not all. He is correct that for a given application, a small version of a large nuclear power plant will always have poorer economics, and he uses the US Atomic Energy Commission in the 1950s as his witness. However, that is based on the premise that all other factors are the same, but that is not the case. If you only need 200 MW of power, building a 1000 MW reactor makes no sense.

First of all, the costs of the so-called soft issues in the 1950s were truly minimal. However, as noted earlier, it is exactly these costs that have exploded in recent decades, which invalidates his comparison. Second, the variety in designs is wider than Professor Ramana seems to be aware of. All his examples are light water reactors, which constitute probably 90% of the world’s current reactor fleet, where scaling is much more important due to their complex and advanced safety and construction regime. The same argument breaks down for low-pressure reactors with passive safety, such as molten-salt, lead-cooled, and to some extent gas-cooled reactors. Unlike light water reactors, these have virtually no explosive potential, which means they can use thinner-walled pressure vessels that are relatively straightforward to manufacture. Their passive safety design also means that human intervention is rarely required. Thus, the risk profile is fundamentally different. All three types have been tested historically; light water reactors were simply prioritized during the Cold War because they were further developed at the time and could therefore be more rapidly deployed. With changing times, however, these “new” reactor types are currently under renewed development, and a few units are already in operation.

Professor Ramana’s discussion concerning the economies of scale is also flawed because he is squarely rooted in traditional nuclear industry thinking. Traditional nuclear project contracts are cost-plus contracts with no incentive to reduce costs. However, the small modular reactors are not that large—particularly the smaller versions—and can benefit from an entirely different business model. Today’s project-based model is highly ineffective due to the politics of nuclear, but a corporate finance model used in manufacturing will [slash costs by 80%](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00295450.2025.2525706) compared to the First-Of-A-Kind (FOAK) estimate used in the project-based business model.

The reason is that under the current Western construction regimes, nuclear vendors try to recuperate their development costs on the first couple of units due to the political risks. With the political risks under control, or a market where they do not exist in the same magnitude, the corporate finance model will allow the vendors to capitalize the development costs and write them off over time. With the exact same direct costs, the impact is huge, as just mentioned—and this is before any learning effects are included. Learning effects, cost reductions that arise due to repeated production, and that are well-documented in nuclear construction, mean that 10-20% cuts in costs have been achieved by merely building two identical plants compared to building them one by one. However, political decisions have frequently made learning and repetition impossible.

Therefore, if done correctly, small modular reactors can dramatically change the nuclear industry, but not by continuing as before. The nuclear industry and its political context need an overhaul. Necessary overhaul would address political interference in projects through local content requirements, regulatory changes during project execution, regulatory differences between countries, and the practice of factoring such political risks into the contracts. Capping the political risks through such an overhaul would enable the industry to start depreciating the development costs across multiple units over time—delivering, as noted previously,  staggering cost reductions.

The last section, where Professor Ramana is discussing nuclear risks, is highly inaccurate. For example, Fukushima Daiichi did not melt down in the sense that the containment was breached. Sure, there was a fuel meltdown, but most laypeople will interpret a meltdown as a breach of containment with subsequent large and dangerous radiological releases. In fact, [the reactor was shut down correctly](https://www.amazon.com/After-Fukushima-History-Nuclear-Radiation/dp/1534946306), but the cooling pumps were positioned too low compared to sea level, not protected well enough, and were therefore knocked out by the tsunamis. Lack of cooling subsequently led to overheating the water in the vicinity of the fuel elements, producing hydrogen gas, which ignited and caused the explosions that were widely broadcast.

Another example is his attempt to enlighten the reader concerning radiation and radioactive materials, where his narrative is not close to reality. Concerning radiation, it might be bewildering for Professor Ramana to learn that exposure to [elevated background radiation displays clear beneficial health effects](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33479810/) in humans, to the extent that life expectancy increases by 2.5 years across US states with high background radiation levels. He could also go to [Onkalo](https://www.nucnet.org/news/finland-completes-key-trial-for-world-s-first-deep-geological-nuclear-waste-repository-3-2-2025) in Finland and see a fully functional deep geological repository for spent nuclear fuel (nuclear waste).

When all is said and done, nuclear energy is actually [one of the cleanest and safest source](https://ourworldindata.org/safest-sources-of-energy)s of energy ever invented. The energy transition is something that both Professor Ramana and this author welcome, but it will not take place with slogans, exhortations and mythmaking. The energy transition is a demanding challenge, and we must let science speak.
