# Pure consciousness reveals a boundless reality beyond objects

*Language cannot capture the deepest level of consciousness*

**By Itay Shani** | *13th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,792 words*

![Pure consciousness reveals a boundless reality beyond objects](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/pure-consciousness-reveals-a-boundless-reality-beyond-objects.webp)

---

*When we are conscious, we are conscious of some thing: be it an object, a person, or an idea. But China-based philosopher **Itay Shani** argues that such “ordinary consciousness” is made possible by what he calls “pure consciousness”—a consciousness without objects, structure, boundaries, or form, which meditative and mystical traditions have long been aware of. Pure consciousness, Shani contends, is awareness of a boundless reality that no word or thought could ever capture, that can only be gestured at using negation, metaphor and contradiction. To recognize it is to recognize that there are worlds beyond our familiar world of objects and things.*

When contemporary scientists and philosophers contemplate the question “What is consciousness?” they tend to do so by focusing on *phenomenal states*, namely, mental states endowed with a qualitative feel — a “what it’s like” character. When one is awake, aware, and fully in one’s senses, one is typically subject to a continuous stream of experiences involving rapidly unfolding trains of thoughts, feelings, and images, and accompanied by longer-lasting moods and emotional overtones.

Such phenomenal states *feel a certain way*. The sweet smell of the rose; the icy chill of a morning swim in the river; the tender pangs of longing — all are different from each other in terms of what it is like to have them. They are also, generally, [*directed at certain objects of thought*, real or imaginary](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/7111.003.0006). A conscious experience is therefore said to be “*of*” or “*about*” something. There is something we think of when we think, something we perceive when we perceive, and so on. Moreover, these objects are presented in consciousness *from a certain point of view*—the perspective of the conscious subject. For example, objects are viewed from a particular angle, perceived as approaching or receding, as far or near, as surprising or familiar, and so on. Furthermore, phenomenal consciousness [*unifies* a great multiplicity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-unity/) of objects and properties into a single experience, which may contain, for example, a panoramic view of a town, with its towers, rooftops, and cobblestone streets; the distant sounds of a church bell; the wind’s chill on one’s skin, and so on. [All of these elements combine into a single, multifaceted experience](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-breaks-from-the-physical-world-by-keeping-the-past-alive-auid-3447).

This, then, is the conventional way of thinking about consciousness. It consists of experiences like these, which [have a certain phenomenology](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914), which are *of* or *about* things, presented from a particular point of view, within a unified whole. A natural way of thinking, given that it corresponds to [the general characteristics of ordinary experience](https://iai.tv/video/the-structure-of-consciousness).

But there is more to consciousness than *ordinary* consciousness. Throughout history, across different civilizations, and under various circumstances, certain humans have experienced — or perhaps we should say realized, or directly encountered — a different type of consciousness. An inner reality that defies the regular outlines of common human experience. One in which the above characteristics of ordinary empirical consciousness no longer obtain. Such consciousness is often described as [*pure consciousness*](https://doi.org/10.53765/20512201.31.5.178), a title which is meant to designate the fact that it pertains to consciousness as it is *in-itself*, apart from the specifications of particular objects, perspectives, attributes, attitudes, and phenomenal qualities.

Pure consciousness is often associated with [*mystical* experiences](https://iai.tv/articles/the-reality-of-the-mystical-experience-auid-2620), profound spiritual experiences marked by a sense of transcendence of one’s ordinary awareness, and of unity with the ultimate ground of being. This ultimate ground is, in turn, identified as divine, sacred, or at any rate as a transpersonal source of life, consciousness, meaning, and value. Mystical experiences are perplexing, and some modern readers will no doubt be inclined to skepticism about their significance, their epistemic import, or perhaps even their very existence. Yet one should bear in mind the crucial role such experiences have played, for millennia, in the constitution and renewal of spiritual and religious traditions across the globe: in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Daoism in the east of Asia; Zoroastrianism, Kabbalistic Judaism, and Sufi Islam in the west of Asia and North Africa; the mystery cults, Pythagoreanism, and Neoplatonism in the ancient Greco-Roman world; the devotional traditions of eastern and western Christianity; Native American spirituality; and more. Moreover, it is well attested that mystical experiences occur spontaneously, and in a variety of circumstances, to many individuals who are neither on an explicit spiritual quest nor embedded in an organized religious setting. This was recorded by early observers such as [Richard Maurice Bucke](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Cosmic_Consciousness.html?id=_PthRnk0UhAC&redir_esc=y) and [William James](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Varieties_of_Religious_Experience.html?id=Qi4XAAAAIAAJ&redir_esc=y) at the turn of the 20th century, and it has been documented since, in such platforms as the archive of the *Religious Experience Research Centre* founded by Alister Hardy.

To be more precise, however, it ought to be mentioned that pure consciousness — a consciousness in which no multiplicity and distinctions appear to exist (see below) — is more directly identified with a type of mystical experience that [Walter Stace](https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=UrfWAAAAMAAJ&source=gbs_book_other_versions) famously described as *introvertive* mystical experience, which he distinguishes from *extrovertive* mystical experience, namely, a kind of experience in which external reality continues to be perceived, yet is being transformed under a powerful sense of the [unity underlying all being](https://iai.tv/video/indian-philosophy-and-the-search-for-unity-with-jessica-frazier).

When explicating the idea of pure consciousness, negation plays a pivotal role. Pure consciousness, to repeat, is consciousness purified of the variables which give ordinary experience its characteristic form. This means, above all, that it is free of a subject-object duality: it is not *of* or *about* any objects or their interrelations, and it is cleansed of the distinct attributes under which such objects can be presented to mind. Consequently, it is also purged of the specific phenomenal qualities which accompany thought, perception, imagination, memory, anticipation, interoception, and the like; and of the judgments, evaluations, and attitudes typical of common experience. For these reasons, pure consciousness is also thought to be *aperspectival*: being a locus of experience, but lacking the definite, partial, and circumscribed standpoint of an empirical subject facing an external world. But does this mean that we have no positive conception of pure consciousness at all? While this is not an easy question to address, the short answer is no. It is true that the experiencing of pure consciousness is largely an [*ineffable*](https://iai.tv/video/limits-of-language)affair, being extremely difficult to articulate in ordinary discursive terms. It is also true that, in mystical and spiritual traditions, pure consciousness is sometimes described using metaphors such as “emptiness” (prevalent in Madhyamaka Buddhism but also found, for example, in the writings of medieval mystic Meister Eckhart), and even the contradictory-sounding “brilliant-darkness” (a term coined by the sixth-century Christian mystic Pseudo-Dionysius). Yet, this does not mean that it is utterly impossible to form a conception of pure consciousness.

The key lies in focusing on the other end of that which we negate. Pure consciousness is consciousness *as such*, apart from being a consciousness of one thing or another. It is consciousness in a state of undifferentiated unity: self-gathered, or self-concentrated, and containing no diverse representations or mental states. Yet, although purified of sensory and cognitive content, and freed of the duality of subject and object, it is, nonetheless, not pure nothingness. Rather, when all the formal elements of experience are removed, there remains the unstructured “matter” of consciousness, namely, consciousness itself, in its pure essence.

This pure essence is an awareness, a live *sentient* awareness. It is calm and diffused, yet fresh and clear. It is vast and oceanic in character: a seemingly boundless expanse of consciousness, transcending [the limits of the egoic self](https://iai.tv/video/catching-sight-of-our-self). It is often described as a state of pure bliss, or delight (*ānanda*, [as it is known in the Hindu tradition of thought](https://books.google.co.in/books?id=PlupnDEr5iAC&printsec=frontcover&source=gbs_atb#v=onepage&q&f=false)). Likewise, it is often referred to as radiant or *self-luminous*: the [pure light of consciousness](https://archive.org/details/CitConsciousnessBinaGuptaseeTheEmpiricalAndTheTranscendentalBinaGuptaInFelicitationFolder_201701) which shines on all things, and in which and through which things appear to mind as manifest presences. Thus, while defying the common categories of experience, pure consciousness is neither a total absence, nor emptiness pure and simple. Rather, it is a plenum: a completeness which transcends both emptiness and form. And, as hinted above, this formless substrate — pure consciousness — serves as the very foundation of common experience, being the backdrop against which ordinary phenomenology is made possible ([or so goes the idea](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.53765/20512201.31.5.178)).

Yet although the above descriptions tell us something of value about pure consciousness, it would be a mistake to conclude that they completely remove the clouds of mystery and ineffability which surround it. To see why, we need to turn our gaze towards what is, perhaps, the most remarkable thing about pure consciousness, namely, its numinous connotations. Those who experience pure consciousness do not merely experience a form of consciousness which is markedly different from ordinary empirical consciousness; rather, they experience it as a deeper level of their own being which, at the same time, *transcends* the limits of one’s individual self and connects with a [transpersonal, cosmic consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/schroedinger-and-the-conscious-universe-auid-2192). This transcendent aspect of pure consciousness is what makes it a perennial mystery. The experience of pure consciousness is an experience of (or, should we say, an experience “as of”) coming into contact with something which surpasses all limitations, and therefore defies all distinctions and categories. It is for this reason that no positive characterization of it seems entirely apt, and that those who experience pure consciousness often resort to [the practice of describing it in negative terms (“not this, not that”).](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-not-an-object-we-can-observe-auid-3608)

On a more positive note, the idea that pure consciousness represents a higher plateau of experience which puts one into contact with a [cosmic consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/the-universal-mind-kastrup-goff-idealism-auid-1586) — and that it is possible to connect with such a transpersonal source of meaning and value by delving into the center of one’s own being — is suffused with potent transformative implications. For it tells us that deep inside each of us there lies something noble and subtle that binds us to Being as such, as well as to the rest of creation; something which surpasses our mundane nature, our limitations, and our all-too-human inadequacies; an exalted reality which, with proper orientation and training, we may be able to glimpse, and, in doing so, be inspired, transformed, and grow into better versions of ourselves.

While this has been a perennial message, sounded throughout the ages in various times and places, it bears special relevance for our own era, a time in which the traditional contours of faith, value, and order are severely eroded, and where many of us feel the burden of a serious crisis of meaning — struggling to navigate a world that seems to make less and less sense, yet unable to reembrace the mores and convictions of yore. Yet, if pure consciousness is, indeed, [a portal to self-transcendence](https://iai.tv/articles/transcending-the-self-and-finding-reality-auid-2288), then the key to a revitalization of spirit lies within ourselves. To quote a memorable phrase from a memorable movie, *The Shawshank Redemption*, “Salvation lies within.”
