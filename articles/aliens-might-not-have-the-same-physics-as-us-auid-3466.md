# Aliens might not have the same physics as us

*Would aliens share our physics?*

**By Daniel Whiteson** | *13th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,726 words*

![Aliens might not have the same physics as us](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Alien-physics-revised.webp)

---

*If we ever meet aliens, would their physics be the same as ours? While this might seem like a purely hypothetical, abstract question, it has profound consequences for what the pursuit of scientific truth actually is. Professor of Physics and Astronomy at the University of California, Irvine, **Daniel Whiteson**, argues that if alien physics is different from ours, then our theories are not as objective and universal as we often believe.*

The boldest claim of modern physics is not due to [Einstein](https://iai.tv/articles/without-philosophy-einstein-said-he-would-have-contributed-nothing-auid-3172) or [Heisenberg](https://iai.tv/video/uncovering-the-universe2). It’s [Newton’s great leap](https://iai.tv/articles/what-newton-really-meant-daniel-hoek-auid-2579) to extend the reach of physics from Earth to the heavens. The same laws of physics that apply to apples, he claimed, should also govern the motion of the moon and the planets. While biology is limited to describing action on Earth, physics since Newton purports to describe the Universe. The ambition of this project is staggering: the laws of physics deduced by humans—who have hardly left our tiny little cosmic rock—should somehow determine how the entire cosmos interacts and evolves.

And yet this is not hubris—it is well supported by the data. Distant galaxies do seem to obey the same rules that apply locally. [The second law of thermodynamics](https://iai.tv/articles/without-entropy-there-would-be-no-life-auid-2947), which was worked out by nineteenth-century scientists experimenting with primitive engines and flasks of gas, has recently been verified to also be valid when massive black holes merge more than a billion light-years distant.

But would the denizens of those galaxies agree with humanity’s description of physics? Does the universal applicability of our laws mean they must also be discovered by others? If aliens ever visit us, could physicists achieve what biologists could never dare to hope for, an interstellar scientific conference? Could aliens help leap us forward a thousand, million, or billion years into our scientific future? Is there a single scientific path for civilizations to walk?

Many physicists, including the late Carl Sagan, make a leap well beyond Newton’s bold claim, arguing that, because our laws seem to hold everywhere, they must be discovered by everyone. String theorist Thomas van Riet, for example, is sure that “aliens will discover string theory. I don’t even doubt it.”

![AP Image 1 copy](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw0OTNd/AP-Image-1-copy.webp)

It’s very tempting to believe that unraveling the secrets of nature is a universal project that would be shared by the scientists of alien species across the galaxy. It’s delicious to imagine that if our species met up we could share notes on this glorious galactic journey of discovery, and crack some of the puzzles that continue to befuddle us, about the origins of the Universe, or the conflicts between quantum theory and relativity.

But is Sagan’s leap as well-justified as Newton’s? It’s awfully flattering to imagine that our human scientific tradition has unearthed physical laws which bear no trace of our humanity, which any intelligent species would have to similarly uncover. Without examples of alien scientific traditions to compare to, what justifies this extrapolation from the singular example of human science? Are human physics and math the only way to think about the Universe—­or are they just the only way we can imagine it?

**A lesson from biology**

Nothing would be as illuminating as spending an afternoon at a chalkboard with alien visitors. But until that day arrives, we can only study our local example for clues that it is the only way forward, or that it might be a product of our biology, psychology, or pure random history.

Our colleagues in biology, who are not so quick to assume the universality of their science, use a similar exercise to anticipate how the mechanics of life might operate under different conditions on alien planets. While carbon is the backbone of life on Earth, they speculate that other elements with similar properties, such as silicon, could play the same role in alien biology. Conversely, they note that flight has evolved independently among bats, birds and insects, suggesting that it wouldn’t be surprising to find it in alien biomes.

Applying the same method to physics can help us identify what aspects of our laws of physics might reflect our humanity, and which we have grounds to argue should be universal.

**Scientific aliens**

Let’s begin at the beginning: [can we even be certain that visiting aliens are *scientific*?](https://iai.tv/articles/the-metaphysics-of-talking-to-aliens-auid-3050)

Does having advanced technology capable of crossing the stars require building mental mathematical models that explain *why* that technology works? Do aliens wonder why? How do we know the aliens want to understand the Universe—­that they even do physics, or any kind of science? Could there be technological but nonscientific aliens out there, capable of crossing the vast emptiness of space without understanding the physics of their amazing leaps?

It’s tempting to think that aliens would have to be scientific to develop that technology. But is that leap of logic founded in insight—­or ignorance? Our own history tells us that the connection between science and technology is not so simple. On Earth, civilization has been technological for thousands of years, well before the recent advent of what we consider modern mathematical, empirical science. Blacksmiths learned to make swords that kept their edge by experimenting with various techniques, making discoveries through happy accidents, and refining their craft over generations. The very precise craft of metallurgy that forms the foundation of a skilled sword maker’s work was explained primarily through the how. The question of why was secondary.

![AP Image 2](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw1ODdd/AP-Image-2.webp)

Of course, actually understanding is nice. The why informs the how. Knowing what’s going on with swords gives you more opportunities to fine-tune and innovate, accelerating technological discoveries. Blindly stumbling onto technological tools is much less efficient. So, advanced technology seems more likely to develop in the hand—­or tentacle—­of a species that understands physical laws and how to manipulate them. But science itself might not be *strictly necessary*.

**Emergent aliens**

Are alien physicists investigating the *same phenomena* that we are?

The reductionist approach of modern physics paints a picture of a Universe of microscopic fundamental objects, likely as yet undiscovered, from whose unseen interactions our macroscopic world somehow emerges.

Incredibly, humans can deduce laws of physics which describe the macroscopic world of electrons or baseballs or planets, without yet knowing nearly *anything* about the nature of the microscopic fundamental objects. This emergence of simplicity from complexity is absolutely vital to the project of physics, but remains philosophically mysterious. Why does simplicity emerge?

Think about how hard it is to get a toddler to cross the street safely in traffic. Doing that with two toddlers is more than twice as hard, because they somehow simultaneously go in opposite directions and poke each other. Now consider trying to herd a billion toddlers across a busy intersection. Imagine your shock if they automatically coalesced into a disciplined marching band. But this shocking emergent miracle happens every time you throw a ball across your yard: the billion particles all act as one and allow you to summarize their motion with just a few numbers. Why does this happen? Why is it possible to study the Universe without knowing the fundamental physics?

We can study the Universe at many different length scales, from gluons to galaxies, developing effective theories for each, without even requiring the mathematical connective tissue that would allow us to derive one from the other. If we don’t understand how such simplicity emerges, how can we be certain that it’s a feature of the Universe, that the same phenomena emerge to inspire the investigations of alien scientists?

![AP Image 3](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw3MDdd/AP-Image-3.webp)

It's tempting to take a shortcut around emergence and focus on the fundamental. But unfortunately, we have no guarantees that our tower of effective theories sits on a solid foundation of fundamental reality. [The Planck scale](https://iai.tv/articles/the-planck-scale-isnt-the-limit-physicists-think-it-is-auid-3188), where gravity and quantum mechanics are in unignorable conflict, tells us where our understanding must break down, but contrary to many popular-science misdirections, it is not guaranteed to provide a minimum distance or time interval to reality. It’s entirely possible that the tower of effective theories is infinite, and so cannot be avoided.

**Unique aliens**

Must alien physics find the *same explanations* that we have?

Is there only one way to build the conceptual machinery of physics, to provide an explanation for what all galactic scientists observe? Could an alien Einstein find different laws that also perfectly describe black holes and clock weirdness? Are there competing theories that no amount of galactic marriage therapy could ever bring into harmony? Or is there only one way to draw a map of the scientific territory?

Our conceptual machinery plays a crucial role of filling in the gaps between observations, describing what is happening behind the curtains. When an electron is accelerated, we tell a story that involves the existence of an electric field, but the field itself is not observable, only its influence on the electron. Is there a way to explain the Universe using some alternative story, with some unimagined *schmields* that replace our fields, which are equally effective experimentally?

![AP Image 4](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw2MjFd/AP-Image-4.webp)

Some argue that any such theory must be conceptually equivalent to ours, “merely the same theory dressed in different clothes,” as [philosopher of science John D. Norton put it](https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/1702_jnrsnr_sem/1702_jnrsnr_seminar_2005/docs/underdet_thesis.pdf). Historically, wave and matrix approaches to quantum mechanics were shown to be equivalent, despite their different mathematical building blocks. But our failure to imagine a rival explanation doesn’t mean that there isn’t one. Convergence can just as easily come from groupthink, human minds all settling at the same conclusion because they’ve all followed the same mainstream ideas. Rival ideas outside our capacity to imagine could be obvious to aliens.

**Universal theories**

These unsolved philosophical questions are more than abstract debates about the nature of knowledge. They will determine whether Earth’s physicists can achieve their dream of productive interstellar scientific contact. That requires visiting aliens to be scientific, interested in the same phenomena, and working on a conceptually equivalent explanation. How likely is that? Should we be confident that the project of human physics is a common galactic endeavor? Or is it likely that the explanations we are developing to describe the Universe are only of interest to minds on Earth?

Until the day the aliens arrive, these questions are philosophical rather than scientific. But digging into them inspires us to identify where our humanity might have influenced our science, revealing unexplored paths and helping us prepare for that long-dreamed-of afternoon at the chalkboard with our alien colleagues.
