# Everyone's a nationalist, the Left needs to accept this

*The radical Right doesn't own nationalism*

**By Siniša Malešević** | *6th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,066 words*

![Everyone's a nationalist, the Left needs to accept this](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Nationalism.webp)

---

*Nationalism tends to be seen as radical and is associated mainly with far-right politics. However, **Siniša Malešević** argues that this understanding of nationalism is narrow and misleading. In fact, nationalism is ubiquitous within our culture, from national pride to sport to religion. Progressives must therefore repurpose nationalism, not by copying the discourse of the political right, but by looking back to nationalism’s origins in the French Revolution and the ideas of liberté, égalité and fraternité. Progressives must focus on commonly built and used institutions, shared everyday values and practices, and the common lived experiences of citizens inhabiting the same nation-state.*

In contemporary public discourse, nationalism is regularly associated with radical politics. This label is typically attached to far-right movements that deploy populist rhetoric and promote nativist ideas and militant anti-immigrant policies, such as the Sweden Democrats or National Rally of France. Alternatively, this term is used to characterize radical political parties that advocate separatism and independence of new states or promote the unification of two territorial units, such as Vlaams Belang in Belgium or Sinn Féin in Northern Ireland. Nationalism is also linked to violent events such as wars, terrorism and genocide—from the 1990s wars of Yugoslav succession, to Sri Lanka’s LTTE’s suicide missions for Tamil independence, or the mass killings of Interahamwe militias in the name of the Hutu nation in Rwanda.

Nevertheless, such an understanding of nationalism is too narrow and misleading. This social phenomenon is not necessarily radical, as most forms of nationalist activities are distinctly non-violent and ordinary. The popular association of nationalism with radicalism conceals how significant this phenomenon is in the contemporary world. Rather than being a marginal or radical political project, nationalism is, in fact, the dominant form of modern subjectivity, and this is reflected in nearly every aspect of social life.

\_\_\_

As numerous public polls indicate, the great majority of people in the world identify strongly in national terms.

\_\_\_

As I argue in my new book [*Nationalism as a Way of Life: The Rise and Transformation of Modern Subjectivities*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/nationalism-as-a-way-of-life/0FF3EC95CB79051E18C5B6158C49F66C) (CUP 2025), nationalism is deeply embedded in the dominant institutions, social practices and values of contemporary societies. Nationalism is a worldview that posits the nation as a principal unit of collective solidarity and political legitimacy. This highly flexible and multifaceted discourse underpins the everyday practices, beliefs and lived experience of billions of people today. Nationalism is not confined to the political right, centre or left—[it can easily amalgamate with any political ideology](https://iai.tv/articles/a-new-nationalism-auid-815). Moreover, once the nation-state has become the dominant form of territorial organization in the world, nationalism has acquired this hegemonic position.

Since all nation-states legitimize their existence through ideas of popular sovereignty, cultural authenticity, political equality, and national independence, they inevitably rely on and promote nationalist principles. This dominance of nationalism is achieved through the ever-increasing organizational capacity of states and other social organizations, their ideological penetration within contemporary societies, and their ability to successfully tap into the micro-level solidarities of close kinship and friendship networks. In this way, nation-centric idioms and practices become normalized and naturalized in everyday life.

As numerous public polls indicate, the great majority of people in the world identify strongly in national terms, and express fervent pride in being Greek, Ghanian, Chilean, Japanese, or belonging to some other nation. In such surveys, a sense of nationhood regularly overpowers other collective attachments. The World Values Survey, Gallup and other global public polls regularly record exceptionally high levels of respondents who are proud of their nation-state: in most cases, these statistics range around 80% for most countries. For example, a [recent Gallup survey](https://news.gallup.com/poll/351791/american-pride-ticks-last-year-record-low.aspx) on the US population found that over two-thirds of respondents were either extremely or very proud to be American, while in a [recent Eurobarometer survey](https://europa.eu/eurobarometer/surveys/detail/2230), 73% of EU citizens, across all member states, categorized their national identity together with their own family as the most important form of collective identification.

\_\_\_

We live in a world that is conspicuously nation-centric, and most individuals see this as completely natural.

\_\_\_

Moreover, academic research indicates that most individuals in the world believe in [the myth of common descent](https://theconversation.com/why-the-idea-that-the-english-have-a-common-anglo-saxon-origin-is-a-myth-88272) and assume that their nations have [distinctly ancient origins](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118663202.wberen312). These widely shared beliefs influence everyday behavior for billions of people. Hence, the sense of national pride is also displayed outside of official settings, such as during public commemorations of national heroes, official celebrations of national holidays or the state-sponsored opening of national cultural institutions. This is even more visible in the spontaneous displays of national flags and singing of national anthems at international sporting events, beauty pageants or music competitions.

Every Olympics and football World Cup, one can witness thousands of individuals wearing jerseys and painting faces with their nation-states’ national colors. Sociologists of everyday life have researched extensively how ordinary people routinely reproduce nation-centric images, ideas and practices. This includes deploying “we” and “they” in everyday conversation to map the boundaries of nationhood, using national symbols as a fashion statement, posting “patriotic” images on social media, or engaging in consumerist practices that glorify “our national products” or “our national cuisine.” We live in a world that is conspicuously nation-centric, and most individuals see this as completely natural.

However, despite popular perceptions of nations as immemorial and innate, this form of collective identification is historically very recent. For 98% of their existence on this planet, human beings have lived in a very small, nomadic and non-hierarchical hunting and gathering groups that rarely exceeded 150 individuals. In such a non-sedentary world, there was no place for the development of nationhood. However, even when humans established the first permanent settlements and when social order became deeply hierarchical, there was still no overlap between cultural and political identities.

For much of their sedentary history, human beings have lived in polities that legitimized their existence by invoking the divine origins of rulers, imperial creeds, civilizing missions or different mythological traditions. In this world, nobility never identified with the commoners, and if nations existed, they were confined to a very small group of individuals with aristocratic lineage. It is only in the last two and a half centuries that nationhood has gradually become a mass phenomenon. While before the modern political, industrial, military and scientific revolutions most ordinary people identified primarily in the local, kinship, clan or religious terms, in the contemporary world, nationhood has become the dominant form of collective identity.

The process of transforming the deeply asymmetrical world of empires, patrimonial kingdoms and city-states into one where the nation-state is the only legitimate form of territorial organization was protracted and violent. Hence, nationalism spread externally via wars, revolutions, terrorism, colonial expansion, and genocide. Internally, this process was also coercive as nationalizing states instituted compulsory nation-centric education systems, military conscription, standardized vernaculars, centralized bureaucratic administration, society-wide judiciary, mass media, and other organizational mechanisms that turned a non-national peasantry into enthusiastic Germans, Italians, Paraguayans, Vietnamese, and so on.

The expansion of nationalism was also aided by civil society associations, private corporations, religious institutions, and even sporting clubs. Nationalist ideas and practices are constantly reproduced in the public sphere through state policies, corporate marketing campaigns, specific religious practices, entertainment events, and social media interactions, among many others. For example, the Carlsberg brewing corporation initiated its “the Danish way” campaign to imprint uniquely Danish cultural traits, which are supposedly reflected in the quality of its beer. One such commercial states: “Many say we Danes are one of the world’s happiest nations. So, what’s our secret? Living life the Danish way, of course: enjoying a work-life balance, nature and craft and spending time together feeling [*hygge*](https://denmark.dk/people-and-culture/hygge).”

Similarly, many religious institutions, which nominally espouse universalist ethical principles, [often embrace nation-centric language](https://iai.tv/articles/religions-dangerous-return-to-politics-must-be-resisted-auid-3363) when addressing their followers. For instance, the Patriarchs of the Serbian Orthodox Church often invoke nationhood as a central parameter of religious identity: “Serbia is one, wherever Serbs may live” (Patriarch Irinej) or “Here in Kosovo and Metohija, we are in our home, we understand where we come from… when we are anywhere else, then we are strangers” (Patriarch Porfirije).

\_\_\_

Although the world of nation-states and nationalisms will not last forever, we are likely to live in nation-centric societies for the foreseeable future.

\_\_\_

The link between sports and nationalism is so prevalent that supporters of such politically different nation-states as the USA, France, Iran, North Korea, Turkmenistan, Cuba, South Sudan, Belarus, or Uganda prominently display their national colors during any international competition. In the contemporary world, nationalist ideas and practices are promoted vigorously and continuously by government institutions and representatives, but they are fostered just as much by a variety of non-state agents and ordinary people. Despite its historical novelty, nationalism has now become a hegemonic worldview. In the early 21st century, nationalism retains and expands its global influence through ongoing organizational, ideological, and micro-interactional grounding.

Since nationalism is so embedded in everyday life today, any attempt to fight it head-on is likely to fail. Any significant attempt to enact large-scale social change must navigate this nationalist maze. Progressive movements that loudly reject nationalist discourses and practices are easily delegitimized by their ideological foes as “unpatriotic,” “selfish,” “globalist,” “promoting the interests of others,” “lacking national solidarity,” or even as “being traitors of their own nation.” [Right-wing political movements](https://iai.tv/video/the-philosophy-of-reform-with-richard-tice) regularly decry universalist ethics and cosmopolitanism as elitist, alienated, egotistical, rootless, and detached from their “own heritage.”

In contrast, such movements position themselves as the true defenders of the nation. The typical statement reflecting this position is former UK prime minister Theresa May’s 2016 speech, where she declared that “If you believe you’re a citizen of the world, you’re a citizen of nowhere.” When left-wing organizations embrace nation-centric discourses, they often frame them in anti-capitalist, anti-globalist and anti-colonial language. They regularly portray their political opponents as “foreign stooges,” “imperialist lackeys,” “the corporate elite,” or “self-interested individuals who do not care about the people.”

\_\_\_

Nationalism must be significantly reformulated and repurposed by progressive movements and not left to the radical right to dictate its form and content.

\_\_\_

Although the world of nation-states and nationalisms will not last forever, we are likely to live in nation-centric societies for the foreseeable future. Thus, rather than attempting to completely dismantle such a world, it is more feasible to work within the confines of these nationalist realities. Progressive politics can use nation-centric idioms to mobilize millions of people to implement substantive reforms across different sectors of our societies. Nationalism can be tamed and its mobilizing and legitimizing mass energy deployed for many progressive causes.

The key issue here is to make nationalism much more inclusive, civic, deliberative, solidaristic, and egalitarian than it currently is. This does not mean accepting the rightist rulebook centred on curtailing immigration, glorifying mythological past, promoting ethnonational unity, or normalizing racism and social exclusion. On the contrary, [nationalism must be significantly reformulated and repurposed by progressive movements](https://iai.tv/articles/nationalism-and-the-left-roy-casagranda-auid-2408) and not left to the radical right to dictate its form and content.

To make nationalism more inclusive and appealing, it is necessary to revisit its original formulation, as framed in the aftermath of the French Revolution. In its original form, nationalism was an ideology that advocated social solidarity, mutual help, and shared responsibility among citizens (*fraternité*). It also promoted equality and equitable treatment of all people regardless of their background (*égalité*). Finally, it was a project that rejected inborn privileges and established social hierarchies, advocating freedom for all (*liberté*). Furthermore, nationalism developed as an ideology that challenged the profound structural inequality of the feudal order. As we now live in a world dominated by the billionaire oligarchs who openly despise those who are impoverished and disempowered, and who evermore resemble the feudal lords, nationalist rhetoric and practice can be successfully deployed to delegitimize their economic and political power and eventually undermine their influence.

The common mistake of many leftist governments was to play the nationalist card using the same or similar discourses of the political right. This is a political game that they can never win. Instead, progressives must develop and implement a very different discourse of nationhood that emphasizes the centrality of commonly built and used institutions, shared everyday values and practices, and the common lived experiences of citizens inhabiting the same nation-state.

For example, in the UK context, a shared public appreciation of the NHS, a national institution built on the solidarity of several generations, has often been used as a building block of a more progressive nationalism. The same applies to the welfare state provisions created in Sweden or Norway. Nationalism is not going away any time soon. It is time for progressive forces to accept it and shape it.
