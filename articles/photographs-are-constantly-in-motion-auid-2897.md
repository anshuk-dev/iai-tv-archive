# We must stop thinking photographs depict reality

*On the ecology of images*

**By Peter Szendy** | *22nd July 2024* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,170 words*

![We must stop thinking photographs depict reality](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/the-ecology-of-images6.webp)

---

*When we snap a photograph, we think we’ve captured a moment in time, in reality. It is this experience that inspired Susan Sontag’s ‘ecology of images’, in her seminal essay ‘On Photography’. But philosopher Peter Szendy argues that to organise pictures, photographs and images into a system is impossible. Instead, we need to insist on the open-endedness of images. In doing so, we can better understand their relationship to reality.*

Statistics prior to Covid-19 indicated that there were more than three billion images circulating each day on social media. This is equal to hundreds of thousands of images during the time it took you to read this sentence. In 2019, YouTube boasted on its [official blog](https://blog.youtube/press/) that there were more than 500 hours of video uploaded every minute, equal to 720,000 hours a day, and  the equivalent of more than 80 years worth of video. This is more than a lifetime of images every day. This overflow has probably increased exponentially, not only because the pandemic has drastically shifted our gazes towards screens but also because another iconological tsunami has hit our contemporary iconosphere: AI, which already has generated, according to recent figures, more images in a year and a half than photography has in 150 years. Not to mention other kinds of images made by machines for machines (from automatic licence plate readers, to facial recognition or imaging systems designed to oversee shipping and logistics). American artist Trevor Paglen, in a groundbreaking article, has suggested that these pictures constitute an “[invisible visual culture](https://thenewinquiry.com/invisible-images-your-pictures-are-looking-at-you/),” with humans “[rarely in the loop](https://thenewinquiry.com/invisible-images-your-pictures-are-looking-at-you/)” of their circulation.

Such is, briefly and bluntly stated, the context in which we can—and have to—think about Susan Sontag’s notion of an “[ecology of images.](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/56464/on-photography-by-susan-sontag-ed--susan-sontag/9780141035789)” What did she mean by it? And how are we to appropriate it, build on it, and reformulate it for today or tomorrow?

\_\_\_

Powerful and compelling as it is, Sontag’s idea of an ecology of images remains exclusively anthropocentric: her only question is what images do to us, human beings.

\_\_\_

 "An ecology not only of real things but of images as well:” these are the last words of her 1977 essay [On Photography](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/56464/on-photography-by-susan-sontag-ed--susan-sontag/9780141035789), and their meaning can only be inferred or guessed from the preceding paragraphs. An ecological approach to pictures is meant here as an antidote to the consumerist logic of their infinite surplus: even if images are not threatened by extinction or exhaustion, even if they are “an unlimited resource,” there is still, Sontag argues, “reason to apply the conservationist remedy.” And the reason becomes clear when she returns to the same idea one year before her death in [Regarding the Pain of Others:](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/54582/regarding-the-pain-of-others-by-susan-sontag/9780141012377) we are “flooded” in images “that once used to shock and arouse indignation,” she writes, and as a consequence, “compassion, stretched to its limits, is going numb.” But her conclusion, in 2003, has a distinctly pessimistic tone: “There isn’t going to be an ecology of images,” she concludes abruptly, radically disbelieving the very possibility that one could “ration horror” and “keep fresh its ability to shock.”

Powerful and compelling as it is, Sontag’s idea of an ecology of images remains exclusively anthropocentric: her only question is what images do to us, human beings. Can’t we imagine, though, another way of looking at pictures and their plethoric numbers? Isn’t there a perspective that would match the iconological consequences of the Anthropocene, a concept that has emerged twenty-five years after Sontag’s On Photography?

Paying close attention to the insights of French philosopher Gilbert Simondon, I want to suggest, as a first step, that images do not merely exist as fully-formed and stable things. Images are not given, they are constantly in the process of becoming.

According to Simondon in his 1966 lecture course titled [Imagination and Invention](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781517914455/imagination-and-invention/), images follow metamorphic cycles that he compares to those of insects, from their larval stage to their final stage of development (since Linnaeus, scientists call the latter imago, by the way). Images, Simondon argues, float around (not unlike the simulacra described in [Lucretius’s On the Nature of Things](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/bailey-on-the-nature-of-things)) before they coalesce by being pinned down within a subject, inside a living being. Within the image-bearers that we are, they are thus tied to specific objects, memories, actions, and they end up forming what Simondon calls “a saturated universe” held together by “a systematic mode of connections.”

While opening unheard-of possibilities for thinking about images, Simondon’s perspective remains teleological: he posits an end, a goal-orientedness to the metamorphoses of images. They are supposed to organize into a cohering system. On the contrary, I want to insist on their open-ended becoming. And, above all, on the conflicting or divergent temporalities that stretch them, so to speak, between contrasting forms or formats, between various crystallizations of themselves.

\_\_\_

Images are not given, they are constantly in the process of becoming.

\_\_\_

Let us consider digital images: we are used to thinking about them as existing simultaneously in various definitions, in lighter or heavier versions of themselves (a low resolution .jpeg or a high resolution .png). What we think of as an image, one and the same, is really a plurality of states, and of speeds too (some take less time to download than others). But the heterochronicity of images (the fact that they are made of these different temporalities) doesn’t characterize only the images that circulate on digital networks. Think of the very origins of painting according to Pliny’s mythical account in his [Natural History](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny)): the first hand-drawn image, made by a potter’s daughter who outlined the shadow of her departing lover, was also molded in clay by her father, thereby existing immediately in two different formats (drawing or painting and molding), coalescing according to two different speeds (one takes longer than the other to be formed).

In a word, or two: images should be considered as heterochroneous transformats —existing in or as diverging temporalities and forms.

Paying attention to this tone of images (the word comes from the Greek *tonos*, which means tension) attunes our senses and sensibilities—our sensorium—to an iconology of the Anthropocene, one that would make us aware of conflicting timescales we hadn’t considered before: geological time, evolutionary time (living species and their mutations), and human (historical) time. We glimpse new ways of possibly thinking about nonhuman images, images that were not made by man: not only machine-made images and their frenetic circulation, but also slowly forming images, such as mimicry in animal species (it took millions of years for eyespots to appear on certain caterpillars) or geological formations (like the images found in stones or marbles evoked by Pliny).

The notion of photography, since Sontag’s time, could thus be generalized to include, alongside the whirlwind of snapshots circling in the iconosphere, these slow images that develop (in every sense of the word) during times that exceed by far the dimension of a human life. Photography, then, on the scale of a universal natural history.

Published by Verso, Peter Szendy’s *[For an Ecology of Images](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/761154/for-an-ecology-of-images-by-peter-szendy/)* will be available January 2025.
