# AI's growing threat to challenging ideas

*The great averaging out of everything*

**By Armin Schulz** | *26th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,376 words*

![AI's growing threat to challenging ideas](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/llms-are-a-threat-to-ideas12.webp)

---

*Our public conversation about artificial intelligence oscillates between fantasies of human-like intelligence and fears of sentient AI overlords taking over the world. This overlooks the real danger posed by large language models, argues Armin Schulz. Since they work by reproducing the average outputs of human language-users, they have an inbuilt tendency to produce conventional, conservative outputs. As we integrate these AIs into our lives, there’s therefore a danger that we suppress our distinctively human ability to think creatively and outside the box. If the AI revolution continues to be driven by disembodied LLMs, it risks stifling rather than propelling progress.*

***This is part 1 of a 2-part series on AI and Human Creativity. Part 2 on [the potential of AI for creativity by Jessie Hall and Karina Vold](http://iai.tv/articles/ai-can-uncover-humanitys-unknown-unknowns-auid-3083) is out now.***

It may seem that large language models (LLMs) are coming for us: not a day goes by without another headline about how ChatGPT, DeepSeek and their ilk have achieved another stunning feat (Writing jokes! Winning poetry contests! Passing Law Schools Entrance Exams!). Is it true that LLMs will soon reach human-level intelligence? Will they make human cognition obsolete?

The answer to both questions is no. However, this doesn’t mean that we don’t need to be careful in how we use these systems—for LLMs’ very structure may actually limit, rather than enhance, our thinking.

To see this, let’s start by considering what it means to think like a human. Human cognition is a particular combination of concepts (“He is just hangry;” “That’s institutional corruption”), cognitive abilities (while getting a PhD, we may delay gratification of pleasures for years and for highly uncertain and abstract gains), and dispositions for social learning and engineering (we study at a university to figure out how to build fMRI machines). While there are many interesting details about how this works, what is key in this context is that these concepts, abilities, and dispositions *interact* to make human engagement with the world possible and efficient.

Making our decisions dependent on whether something is a case of “institutional corruption” (say) is difficult: it might require historical analysis, legal research, and formal reasoning. Much the same is true for most of our decisions—whether they concern where to go for dinner (should we eat vegetarian?) or what to do for fun (is going to a concert contributing to climate change and inequality?). If we had to figure out, from scratch and by ourselves, how to apply our concepts to the world, we would never actually get to do anything—we would be dithering our days away.

\_\_\_

LLMs live in a world of words.

\_\_\_

Luckily, we figured out a solution to this: we build tools and rely on others’ insights. We can learn from others what decisions are good to make when. We can rely on technology like calculators, newspapers, algebra, and accounting conventions to help us make our thinking better and more efficient. Importantly, building these tools also requires learning from others: we take existing inventions and insights, and change them slightly. In this way, our concepts, abilities, and dispositions ratchet each other up to new heights.

All of this matters when it comes to LLMs. On the one hand, LLMs live in a world of words. This makes them very restricted in their ability to approach human cognition. It is not just that ChatGPT can’t make you a cup of tea. It is that ChatGPT cannot function at all unless it is provided with verbal prompts. It will never decide to do anything by itself—because, without verbal prompts, it lives in an empty universe. (Incidentally, this is something increasingly recognized by AI pioneers such as Stanford’s Fei-Fei Li, [who sees the next frontier in AI in technology that can see and respond to the world](https://www.ted.com/talks/fei_fei_li_with_spatial_intelligence_ai_will_understand_the_real_world).)

On the other hand, even if these LLMs are inherently restricted from reaching human-level intelligence, they can still pose threats to—but also opportunities for—the way we think and act. In particular, once we have settled the question of whether LLMs can ever think like a human (no!), we still need to settle the question of whether they are helpful tools for enhancing human cognition. Here, things are not straightforwardly positive.

To be sure, LLMs have the potential to be helpful aides to our thinking. In particular, they can put more information at our fingertips quickly and allow us to spread information efficiently to others. They can thus function as the latest iterations of the kinds of tools we have always relied on to make our thinking more efficient. Instead of having to wade through pages of documents to determine whether they show that our client was out of the country at the time a crime was committed, we can have an LLM solve this problem for us in seconds. This frees us to do other things instead. Similarly, after a scientist spends many months collecting data on specific hypotheses, testing, and re-testing, LLMs can make writing up the results—especially given the very stringent constraints of scientific research papers—much easier. This enables others to learn from our insights more quickly, and thus advances human thought and action.  Of course, we need to watch for “hallucinations” (where an LLM sees patterns in the data that don’t actually exist) and biases—but at least the potential for human advancement is there.

\_\_\_

If we continue to rely on the outputs of LLMs, therefore, we end up deepening familiar tropes, rather than producing new ideas. Our creativity is stifled, not enhanced.

\_\_\_

But LLMs can also restrict our thought processes in important ways. In particular, they can and do limit our thinking by shunting us down familiar avenues of thought, rather than encouraging us to think outside of the box. These systems are built to produce output that follows the standard or most common ways of writing. In effect, they are extremely complex sentence completion machines: “sentences that start with ‘The rain in Spain’ tend to end with ‘stays mainly on the plain’—unless the previous sentence was ‘And now for today’s weather.’” If we continue to rely on the outputs of LLMs, therefore, we end up deepening familiar tropes, rather than producing new ideas. Our creativity is stifled, not enhanced.

Of course, it’s not that human cognition is somehow magical: we, too, sometimes just follow common lines of thinking. However, we can do something that LLMs can’t: we can question our tendencies for groupthink. We can say, “I know what people commonly say here—but are they right? Do I want to be part of this?” The structure of our thoughts and how they fit with other thoughts can help us come up with new ideas. It may be true that many people mostly think of neurosurgeons as male, but this may not be consistent with my values or experiences. I may thus formulate new thoughts—e.g. about the need to change people’s expectations.

The conservative bias of LLMs is made even worse by the fact that their availability can make it harder to learn and practice writing (something teachers are getting all too familiar with). Students may be too tempted to just fall back on the outputs of an LLM rather than working on assignments themselves. This is problematic, since the act of writing is itself a powerful thinking technology: writing is not just a tool for communication, but also for helping us figure out what it is what we want to say in the first place. Writing down the steps of an argument frees up our working memory and concentration. In turn, this enables us to use these resources to deepen our thought processes—and thus be more creative. So people who spend less time writing—as they have come to be too reliant on LLMs—are hindered in advancing their thinking. (This point is in fact strengthened by the way LLMs work: they, too, may need to write out solution attempts as they figure out a problem.)

So, in a nutshell: while even the next version of an LLM still won’t think like a human, whether it can help (e.g. by completing rote tasks that require little human ingenuity, such as legal document review) or hinder human thinking is not clear. That’s up to us and how we use it. Let’s choose wisely.
