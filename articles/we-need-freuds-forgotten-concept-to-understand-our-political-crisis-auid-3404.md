# We need Freud's forgotten concept to understand our political crisis

*An interview with Alenka Zupančič*

**By Alenka Zupančič** | *31st October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,006 words*

![We need Freud's forgotten concept to understand our political crisis](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/we-need-freud-s-forgotten-concept-to-understand-our-political-crisis.webp)

---

*We associate Freud with the repression of thoughts and feelings. But he also described a subtler defense: recognizing an uncomfortable truth, yet acting as if it didn’t matter—a phenomenon he called* disavowal*. In this interview, philosopher Alenka Zupančič, a close collaborator of Slavoj Žižek, argues that disavowal is the key to understanding our political paralysis. From climate change to populism to the performative outrage of social media, Zupančič says the problem isn’t that we deny reality—it’s that we acknowledge it endlessly and keep doing nothing.*

**Oliver Adelson: Your latest book spans politics, philosophy, psychology, and more, but there’s one concept that you think is essential for making sense of all these areas: disavowal. What is disavowal, and why is it so key for understanding our predicament?**

Alenka Zupančič: It’s something that I started to think about really intensely a few years ago. So it’s not that it’s been with me the whole time, but I think it captures something essential—as you put it—about this time. And it’s a very interesting concept, because we are used to this other concept, which is simple denial. You know, denial of climate change, denial of this or that.

But disavowal functions in a much more perverse way. Namely, by first fully acknowledging some fact—“I know very well that this is how things are”—but then going on as if this knowledge didn’t really matter or register. So in practice, you just go on as before. And I think this is even more prevalent in our response to different social predicaments than simple denial.

**You mentioned climate change, and I want to pick up on the difference between denial and disavowal here. Do people who declare the truth of climate change but then do nothing—do they perhaps do more harm than the deniers of climate change through their disavowal?**

Yes, this is precisely one of my points in the book—that they are doing perhaps more damage. Or, what is even more important, they are entrapped in this kind of *pas de deux* with the direct deniers, because they present themselves as much more rational. They say, “Look at these stupid people. They just don’t believe in climate change. But we are enlightened. We know all about it.” But in the long run, nothing really happens. The practices remain just the same. You organize a couple of climate conferences, but growth still remains the principle of social functioning, and so on. So I think, not only is it more dangerous because it is more prevalent—I mean, there are many more people who are into this kind of disavowal functioning—but it’s also dangerous because there is thisdance between the two.

The deniers, who are often associated with so-called conspiracy theories—they point to these elites who just use it as some kind of scheme to enrich themselves. Whereas the elites, who do get richer and richer, point at the deniers, saying, “Okay, but they are the real bad guys,” while they do exactly nothing. It is a real problem.

**Do you think this perhaps explains why we have populists and business-as-usual figures in our politics? Do these represent denial and disavowal?**

Precisely. I think we are really tragically condemned to this kind of opposition, which, in my eyes, is not even a true opposition, because the two sides really need each other and support each other.

**Why has disavowal become so public? It seems that the structure isn’t to begin by saying, “I know very well,” but instead by screaming, “I know very well.” Is it so public because people are virtue signaling? Is it a response to trauma? What’s going on?**

There are a lot of things going on. And, of course, this concept is not new. It was proposed already—I mean, it’s there even in Freud already—but the French philosopher Octave Mannoni proposed this formula in French. It’s *je sais bien, mais quand même*. “I know very well, but nevertheless.” And he analyzed this several decades ago in a different context, but I think it became again very present, this mode of functioning, with a slight twist. And this twist moved away from this kind of oppositional configuration—“I know very well, but nevertheless, I continue in my practical life as if I didn’t know”—to “I know very well. I declare that I know. And this is precisely the excuse why I can go on as before.”

\_\_\_

People are really afraid not to be in the know, to be declared naïve.

\_\_\_

So this knowledge that I declare, that I flag, that I demonstrate to my peers or to whichever people, particularly from the same bubble that I’m part of—it’s precisely enough. It’s the very shield that protects me from this knowledge having any consequences in reality. It’s enough that I know and that I am in the know.

**How does social media play into disavowal? It seems like it makes it much easier to have public disavowals. But also on social media, we’re bombarded with grotesque images every day. Does social media itself require and induce disavowal?**

It definitely gives it a kind of platform and amplitude that probably wouldn’t have been there otherwise. But it also encourages it in the sense that social media is public—but not really. It is completely compartmentalized. Within your social media, you have your bubbles, you have your friends, followers, whatever—and basically, in most cases, you are talking to them. So this kind of thing gets more and more inflated within this bubble, but does not really break through.

I also detect on many levels that our principal fear is that we will be revealed “as naïve.” People are really afraid not to be in the know, to be declared naïve—more so than of anything else. I really see this a lot in the case of climate change—“I’m not so naïve” on both sides. You know, “I’m not so naïve as to believe this or that.” I don’t know where this existential fear of being naïve, of being duped by somebody, of not being in the know, comes from, because it’s quite amazing. Sometimes we really fear more to be naïve or wrong than to die.

So there are very strange dialectics going on here, which enable us to preserve a quite fascinating courage—seeming courage—to persist in something even though we know that it is catastrophic. But it’s not exactly courage precisely, because there is this shield protecting us from what we actually know and what should wake us up—or at least induce some shift in the way we think.

**You note that there’s a pathological element in disavowal, but could we ever really live without it? If all facts had their true weight and we had no disavowals, would it be too much for us to bear?**

Of course, I’m not saying, “There is some terrible reality. Just face it directly.” We all have all kinds of mechanisms that help us deal with certain segments of reality—personal or social—which are traumatic. But the point is that there are many different ways of dealing with them. And disavowal, I would say, is one of the most problematic ones, because it locks us in so we can just await—literally passively await—certain catastrophes while fully acknowledging their arrival.

\_\_\_

Disavowal is much less painful. It doesn’t have so many of these consequences, because you don’t repress this reality. You maintain it on the same plane, and you just derealize it completely.

\_\_\_

So I think in some ways, denial is, socially, not really useful, but denial is based on repression, which means that you have something that is so unbearable that you repress it from reality. It’s moved to another level, so you cannot speak of it. It becomes unattainable for you. But then it returns through all kinds of symptoms that sometimes make your life unbearable, and you are forced to do something. Whereas disavowal is much less painful. It doesn’t have so many of these consequences, because you don’t repress this reality. You maintain it on the same plane, and you just derealize it completely. You look at something and you talk about it as if it didn’t have anything to do with you.

And this relates to another thing that Freud analyzed, namely the functioning of what he calls *fausse reconnaissance* or *déjà vu* in analysis. People would come up with a memory or something that was presented in analysis for the first time, and they would immediately say, “But I’ve told you this already.” And Freud points out how this is again a defense mechanism—presenting a thing, even when it first emerges, already as a memory. As something that actually does not touch you so much. It’s something that cannot really get to you, precisely because it is so traumatic.

To switch to the social context, and to the recent "Gaza Riviera" video, it functions a little bit like this kind of *déjà vu*. People say, “Oh, this is the dumbest idea possible. We all know it’s dumb.” But it is out there. It has been circulated for so long that now it’s part of objective reality, almost. And then it can enable the ruthless politics. Although nobody subjectively assumes this stance, nevertheless, it is out there. We got familiar with it, and then we are not so shocked when something like this really starts to happen.

**We’ve been discussing a lot of these problems in psychoanalytic terms. When we’re trying to resolve these problems, do you think it’s a matter of using psychoanalysis or collective politics? Where should the action be?**

I don’t think that simply using psychoanalysis we will solve these problems. I’m a strong believer in politics. I also think that very often the term politics became unpopular in social—even political—discourse, and was, particularly in critical theory, replaced by power. But power is something omnipresent. It’s everywhere. Whatever you do, you’re a part of power. Politics is something else. You can actually address it, you can oppose it, you can try to suggest another politics. So I think psychoanalysis can certainly help us see mechanisms at work. And also to keep an eye open for what is called social politics, some kind of fundamental antagonism, social antagonism. And the moment you have this social antagonism, it means that reality—political, social reality—is not simply some neutral whole in which one part fights the other.

\_\_\_

Psychoanalysis will not and cannot replace politics, but it can certainly help to sustain certain political ideas or perceptions that make emancipatory politics possible.

\_\_\_

Take Marx, for instance. For Marx, class struggle is not, “Okay, there are all these different classes and they struggle among themselves.” Class struggle is the very thing that breaks down the uniformity, unity, and homogeneity of this space. So it is only from certain positions—from certain biased positions—that you can have a kind of true insight into what this antagonism is all about. So I think psychoanalysis will not and cannot replace politics, but it can certainly help to sustain certain political ideas or perceptions that make emancipatory politics possible.

**Many psychologists would say that if you want to understand psychology, if you want to understand humans, you should be studying brain chemistry, not Freud or psychoanalysis. That’s not your view?**

No, not at all. First of all, in psychoanalysis there is this very clear distancing from what is generally known as psychology. Lacan famously claimed all the time that psychoanalysis is not psychology. It is not something that wants to make a theory of human whatever—in my view, it is closer both to politics and to philosophy than to psychology as a kind of explanation of who we are and why we are what we are. It asks more fundamental questions for me.

And I’m always very wary of psychologizations of certain phenomena. Even Freud, when he first started with the so-called hysterics and so on—his insight was that what was on the couch with these hysterics was the society. So the society was on the couch. It was not simply, “These individuals, they need to be cured.” What was happening in the family structure, in the social structure, for these kinds of symptoms to emerge? So it’s a very different approach.
