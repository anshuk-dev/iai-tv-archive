# Shakespeare and the failures of language

*The master of language knew it ultimately forsakes us*

**By Simon Palfrey** | *29th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,460 words*

![Shakespeare and the failures of language](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/shakespeare-language-inverted2.webp)

---

*We think of Shakespeare as the ultimate master of words, but his plays frequently stage the moments where language fails. **Simon Palfrey** argues that Shakespeare purposefully pushed language to its breaking point to show that our deepest experiences—grief, desire, shame, and death—cannot be put into words. When we demand that people articulate these feelings, we are asking for something that cannot exist: a neat verbal account of experiences that are, by their nature, beyond speech.*

It’s fair to say that Shakespeare had as much command over the spoken word as anyone who has ever lived. But he always knew that words might fail us when we need them most. The more inward we travel, the more words may start to coil and falter. Think of the most private suffering—shame, forbidden love, self-loss, imminent death—and there we will find Shakespeare, stalking the lands where shareable words, even *speakable* words, no longer exist.

Forbidden love is the subject of *Romeo and Juliet,* and Juliet in particular is intensely scrupulous about the language it demands. She would rather not speak at all than speak falsely, or still less swear on over-traded icons like the moon. Juliet is fiercely aware of the constraints upon her freedom to speak; but, could she speak her desire, it would shatter the bounds of both her social world and her body:

*Bondage is hoarse and may not speak aloud,*

*Else would I tear the cave where Echo lies*

*And make her airy tongue more hoarse than mine*

*With repetition of my Romeo’s name.*

No words can contain her ardor; she would cry her tongue hoarse, end speech entirely rather than subdue her desire. When she is denied Romeo’s presence her words become either empty situational lubricants (her false contrition and good night to her parents, knowing she will never speak to them again) or hyperbole that admits no conceivable response, correspondent to nothing but her world-annihilating will (her vow to *leap* from *battlements*, or *lurk where serpents are,* or hide in a *charnel house* covered with *dead men’s rattling bones* rather than be without her love). Language is a social art, designed to communicate.For the lovelorn Juliet, such social language is done with; she will act her final *dismal scene* alone, in a tomb that will eternalize her unknowable mind.

In a profound sense these characters have leaped beyond the playworld, into a dimension where words do nothing but echo in unanswerable isolation.

\_\_\_

Cordelia in *King Lear* presents a variation on Juliet’s dilemma. The play begins with Lear’s notorious public love-test of his three daughters: *Which of you shall we say doth love us most?* Perhaps Lear believes, or pretends to believe, in the faithfulness of words to intimate relations. Or perhaps he believes that a rehearsal of words is sufficient; what matters is public compliance with the flattery-show. Whatever Lear believes, Cordelia is quite unable to *heave* her *heart into* her *mouth*. To do so would resemble a foul kind of vomit. *Love, and be silent,* she says in an aside to the audience, as Lear prepares his fateful summons. What can you say, asks her father, to get a richer gift than your sisters?

Cordelia. *Nothing, my lord.*

Lear. *Nothing?*

Cordelia. *Nothing.*

The insult, for Cordelia, is to expect words to perform her feeling*.* There is extraordinary pride here, but the insult to the virtue and simplicity of love is still more. Not just *how* can love be spoken, but *why* should it be spoken? The demand for words is the profoundest failure of trust.

The play’s devastating final moments turn a full circle from this deadly beginning. Lear enters *howling,* Cordelia dead in his arms. Already words are on the shortest leash. He holds a mirror to her lips, to see if the glass will stain with her breath; he sees a feather stirring and dreams that it moves from her breath; but her breath is done:

*And thou no breath at all? Thou’lt come no more,*

*Never, never, never, never, never.*

Cordelia’s *nothing* is now the *never* of all imaginable life*,* the creation prolapsing into a single drummed word, [a word like an event-horizon](https://iai.tv/video/beyond-words).

If love is one absolute that eschews the dayworld of words, the other, as we see here, is death. Shakespeare’s key technique for entering death is the repeated cue: when the actor speaks their cue more than once, meaning that the cued response can come in *before* he has completed his final speech: before, in other words, he has completed his dying. The actor is liberated from punctual succession; he can enter an existential space all his own, a space of dying, at once in life and beyond life. The examples are legion: Mercutio’s *a plague on your houses;* Hamlet’s *the rest is silence, O, o, o,* o; Desdemona’s *Farewell, Commend me to my kind lord, O farewell*; Lears’s *O, o, o o* (Quarto) or *Look there, look there* (Folio), or a variation on these, the terminally departing words of Lady Macbeth: *To bed, to bed: there’s knocking at the gate. Come, come, come, come, give me your hand. What’s done, cannot be undone, To bed, to bed, to bed.* Life goes on around their dying—Romeo rattles on about his reputation, Horatio calls upon flights of angels, the doctor suspiciously watches the sleepwalking Lady Macbeth—but the departing characters are oblivious. At such moments [words have ceased to function](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-and-the-paradoxes-at-the-limits-of-language-auid-3146) as interlocutions. In a profound sense these characters have leaped beyond the playworld, into a dimension where [words do nothing but echo in unanswerable isolation](https://iai.tv/articles/shakespeare-vs-wittgenstein-the-fight-for-meaning-auid-3066).

Caliban is in some ways Shakespeare’s deepest study in the origin and burden of speech. Words come to him belatedly, as a gift of the exiled Europeans. His lexis drips with the physical stuff of the island, the fresh springs and brine pits, the pignuts and scamels. But there is a fatal disconnect between his aboriginal knowledge of the island and his acquired knowledge of words. Linguistic understanding merely profits the master. He loses possession of his body, and with it the island he claims as his own. His only profit is to *curse*. In Shakespeare, the curse marks a point where words announce their own futility. The curse will echo unheard, spoken sotto voce or cried to the empty skies; or it will bring down worse curses. Inevitably Caliban’s curses rebound upon him, renewing his subjection and compounding his physical deformity. But language—that foreign curse—offers no other recourse to express his pain, even as these curses condemn him more than ever to his lonely, wretched, cowering body. Caliban’s curses become a cry of loss, both mourning and recalling his days before the trap of words.

Because the play so closely witnesses Caliban’s acquisition of language, his [words never have the protection of convention](https://iai.tv/articles/the-cultural-case-for-saving-endangered-languages-auid-3135); they are never unthinkingly rehearsed; everything he says arrives with a kind of dew upon it. We can seem to listen in to thoughts as they awaken, words so inward and ingenuous as to be a kind of silence.

*Be not afeard. The isle is* *full of noises,*

*Sounds and sweet airs that give delight and hurt not.*

*Sometimes a thousand twangling instruments*

*Will hum about mine ears; and sometimes voices.*

*That if I then had waked after long sleep,*

*Will make me sleep again; and then in dreaming,*

*The clouds methought would open and show riches*

*Ready to drop upon me, that when I waked*

*I cried to dream again.*

Caliban is telling his fellow conspirators not to be afraid of the ambient noise; to that extent, his speech is an interlocution. But the body of the speech is spoken essentially to no one. The sounds he recalls are not those of social conversation: they are *twangling instruments,* as though self-played; they *hum* about his ears, like invasive insects; they are *voices* that belong to nobody. The island’s sonic field merges imperceptibly into Caliban’s tunnelling consciousness. This speech is about silence *inside* sound, a wonder that corresponds not only to the airy castles of dream but the unknowability of another’s mind. His confederates barely hear, or if they hear they don’t listen: *This will prove a brave kingdom to me, where I shall have my music for nothing*, responds Stephano, trivially reductive. The mismatch between speech and answer is part of the point. Words, as social connectors, cannot reach to Caliban’s experience. He is on his own, hypnotized inside his mind-cocoon.

Shakespeare will often give the most delicate expression to thoughts or sensations that cannot be shared. This is a basic function of his soliloquies. But secret confession is hardly limited to soliloquy or asides. Every metaphor in Shakespeare bears more than one meaning, generating virtualities or ironies quite beyond the intention of the character. In a sense these thoughts remain unspeakable: but they have been spoken. A classic example is Macbeth. He longs to excuse himself from his own actions, but his private meditations are full of *horrible shadows,* latent implications and lurking judgments, projections beyond the moment that Macbeth can speak but never admit, apprehend but never openly see. He will think he is telling a lie, or merely evading a truth, and express irredeemable facts: *The wine of life is drawn, and the mere lees/Is left this vault to brag of; His gash’d stabs look’d like a breach in nature/For ruin’s wasteful entrance.* Words aren’t failing here; they are triumphantly succeeding; but Shakespeare’s irony is working so as to express what can neither be spoken to others nor admitted to oneself. Macbeth’s unspeakable truth is much more than a shameful secret or a hidden crime. It is the profoundest severing of self and soul; the profoundest delivery of life to death, a vertigo that *cannot* be looked in the eye. Could his suffering be told, it wouldn’t be this suffering.

So it is that as Macbeth descends into dread, into the deepest consciousness of self-sabotage and loss, he essentially ceases to speak *to* anyone. Here he speaks about his wife, to the doctor, but really he speaks to and of himself.

*Canst thou not minister to a mind diseased,*

*Pluck from the memory a rooted sorrow,*

*Raze out the written troubles of the brain,*

*And with some sweet oblivious antidote*

*Cleanse the stuff’d bosom of the perilous stuff*

*Which weighs upon the heart?*

The addressee is vaporized. Every speech becomes a silent self-communing, bearing a silent mocking judgment. Macbeth’s words suggest a sublime endgame for language: words are the more humanly excavating the more they cease to communicate. As a social being he is dead; as a communicating creature he is silent. Macbeth’s last acts become a deathly mime.

A variation on speech that annihilates the addressee is the *unanswerable* question: one spoken anywhere but the closing words of a speech. Shakespeare’s actors could extend such a pause for as long as they wished. The beauty of this is that [the potential for silence](https://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-heidegger-and-the-language-of-silence-auid-3361) can work in both directions at once. The addressee of the question can be paralysed in silence; but so too might the questioner, left begging for an answer that will not come. Here is Shylock in the trial scene, gloatingly insisting on his right to his pound of flesh:

*Ay, his breast,*

*So says the bond, doth it not noble judge? [PAUSE]*

*‘Nearest his heart’, those are the very words.*

The unanswerable question is here a mode of torture. A few moments later, however, Shylock learns that his pound of flesh is an impossibility; if he sheds a drop of blood in the cutting then *he* will die.He is trapped, and to mark the fact, the same torturing technique is turned back on Shylock:

Gratiano. *A second Daniel, a Daniel, Jew! [PAUSE]*

*Now infidel I have you on the hip.*

Portia. *Why doth the Jew pause? [PAUSE] Take thy forfeiture.*

Shylock is addressed—*a Daniel, Jew!—*but can say nothing. His forced silence is tauntingly confirmed by the disguised Portia—*Why doth the Jew pause?*—another literally unanswerable provocation. The Shylock-actor is marooned inside his part, unable to speak. For the Shylock-character this speechlessness marks devastation. He is confounded; he has a premonition of absolute loss, confirmed moments later when, having been told he must convert to Christianity and bequeath his possessions to his treacherous son-in-law, the same technique is repeated once again:

*Art thou contented Jew? [PAUSE] What dost thou say?*

The gap between these questions is the space of abyssal loss. Words can render nothing. *I am content,* says Shylock: this doesn’t mean happy, it doesn’t even mean resigned. To be *content* is to be full to the brim, *finished.* His punishment echoes, unexpiated, over the lovers’ games that follow: but Shylock is silenced forever.

But Shakespeare allows other endings than death, and speech can fail with wonder as much as horror or devastation. The miracle of art can produce mute astonishment; likewise the miracle of losses restored. In the climax to *The Winter’s Tale,* these experiences come one on top of another, as children are reunited with parents, and a lifelike statue turns into a mother and wife redeemed from death. *I like your silence,* says Paulina as she unveils the statue, *it the more shows off/Your wonder.* Shakespeare’s late plays all move toward this moment, when lost things return, and we feel as though for the first time the miraculous impossibility of existing:

*They seemed almost, with staring on one another, to tear the cases of their eyes: there was speech in their dumbness, language in their very gesture; they looked as they had heard of a world ransomed, or one destroyed.*

Loved ones have been reborn to one another; it is a second life, but also a return to life’s very beginnings, the gift of sensory organs and human relation. They seem *almost* to tear the cases of their eyes, as though to end the possibility of sight forever. In place of sight there is a kind of [dream of words](https://iai.tv/video/the-limits-of-thought-john-mcwhorter-and-stanley-fish), discovered in the mute astonishment of mime. But the mime will be useless without sight, and so the longed-for promise is of *speech* that might heal the breach. They *looked* as though they had *heard* of a world ransomed, that is redeemed from death. Again, it is the hearing that has closer access to the redemptive miracle—or to its failure, if the world has been not ransomed but *destroyed.* Either way, sound is the primal element. In Shakespeare’s worldview, voice comes both before and after sight; it moves with things that the eye cannot reach. For Shakespeare, voice is life itself, it is *breath*. As Leontes asks of the statue:

*What fine chisel*

*Could ever yet cut breath?*

There is no answer other than the fact of bodily presence, trembling into movement. Words cede to their future promise: and then the play ends. We need to go back to the beginning and listen all over.
