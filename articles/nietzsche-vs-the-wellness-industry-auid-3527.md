# Nietzsche vs the wellness industry

*Self-help will not cure a sick world*

**By Jill Marsden** | *20th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,617 words*

![Nietzsche vs the wellness industry](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/nietzsche-vs-the-wellness-industry-2-posterized.webp)

---

*When the world itself is unhealthy, our modern obsession with self-improvement serves as little more than a plaster over systemic burnout. The booming self-help industry trains us to use our creativity to change ourselves rather than challenge the systems we live in. Drawing on Nietzsche's idea that creativity should be the means by which we reshape the world, **Jill Marsden** argues that we should move from self-improvement to self-realisation. To live well is not just to fix oneself, but to change a world that is making us sick.*

Anyone wishing to determine the value of art today has to know where to look. As arts provision slowly disappears from schools and universities across many parts of the world, the enthusiasm for creativity in the health and well-being sector has never been so strong. Defined as “creative approaches and activities which have benefits for our health and well-being,” the phenomenon of “[creative health](https://ncch.org.uk/what)” has emerged over the last decade as an aspirational force driving better care and better value across the [spectrum of global health services](https://www.who.int/initiatives/arts-and-health).

At first glance, it might seem as if this sector were having a Nietzschean moment. The proposition that active engagement with arts and culture fosters improved quality of life resonates with Friedrich Nietzsche’s conviction, first voiced in [*The Birth of Tragedy*](https://iai.tv/video/babette-babich-nietzsche-the-birth-of-tragedy-and-the-technology-trap) (1872), that art is the highest task and truly metaphysical activity of life. The artistic culture of the ancient Greeks draws Nietzsche’s fulsome praise for its exemplification of tragic wisdom, a philosophical way of life which Nietzsche elaborates in his own work as an *art of living*. Echoes of this idea can be heard in the premise that creative health is “the expression of the ancient wisdom that the exercise of the creative imagination is [beneficial to our health and well-being](https://ncch.org.uk/creative-health-review).” For the ancients, philosophy was a mode of living and knowing which brought about a fundamental transformation in being, and for Nietzsche, this transformation is achieved through art.

Nevertheless, despite the consensus on the health gains of creativity, it would be a stretch to claim that the classical idea of “the good life” or Nietzsche’s art of living have sufficiently permeated the mainstream to vindicate the existential value of art. In fact, to appreciate the value that art has for life today, it is necessary to establish why the concept of well-being, despite its contemporary popularity, has relatively little to do with the art of *living well*.

The “well-being” phenomenon might be read as an artefact of neoliberal work culture, the panacea for the ills of our inherently unhealthy systems.

\_\_\_

Nietzsche asks a question which is striking in its simplicity: what kind of life does art promote? Is it a mode of existence that is affirmed or merely endured? Seen through the lens of *value for life*, art making is never simply prized by Nietzsche for its own sake. Whilst it may seem uncontroversial to regard singing in a choir, or dancing, or embroidering quilts as “good” for you, to do so begs a prior question about the cultural context within which such assertions are made. Denying the possibility that any creative act is simply good in itself, Nietzsche looks beyond the prevailing ideology of the day, posing more searching questions about how and why certain values come to hold sway. In this respect, living well is never simply a matter of “personal” well-being or palliative self-improvement. Rather, with regard to “aesthetic values,” he observes this main distinction: “[is it hunger or superabundance that has here become creative?](https://philoslugs.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/the-gay-science-friedrich-nietzsche.pdf)” The contention in this tantalizing question is that the way of life of a people or culture may be read symptomatically as either the outcome of *lac*k—the progeny of deficit or need—or as the product of exuberance, of potent, *surplus* energies.

Provocatively, Nietzsche suggests in *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) that the phenomenon of Socratic dialectics is symptomatic of world-weariness and decline of vital instincts (as epitomized in the view propounded in Plato’s *Phaedo* that philosophy is about [dying and preparation for death](https://iai.tv/articles/five-philosophers-on-how-to-face-death-auid-2173)). More audacious still, he questions whether a predilection for the hard, horrifying and problematic aspects of existence is necessarily an expression of pessimistic sentiments. Could the artistic embrace of tragedy by the pre-Socratic Greeks be reckoned a symptom of “well-being, overflowing health, the fullness of existence”?

It will not escape notice that in Nietzsche’s speculation about the pre-Socratic Greeks, “well-being” is invoked in the context of overflowing health and tragic superabundance. To be rich in life is to afford the sight of the terrible and the questionable. Tragic art, which is able to depict [suffering and the sacrifice of life’s highest types](https://iai.tv/articles/baldwin-vs-nietzsche-how-to-overcome-tragedy-auid-2970), is indicative of a culture supremely affirmative and strong. To live well in such a culture includes confronting life’s vicissitudes, affirming existence even in its strangest and sternest problems.

What then of well-being as it pertains to popular discourse today and to creative health in particular?  Spanning a range of registers from positive psychology to economic sustainability, well-being in the context of health represents strategies for individual life improvement. According to the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/activities/promoting-well-being), well-being signifies a “resource for daily life,” a state of positivity, encompassing happiness, physical flourishing and overall satisfaction with life. As a desideratum, it seems so self-evidently advantageous as to require no further analysis. However, a Nietzschean critique of values calls into question the *value* of values hitherto “[accepted as given, as fact, as beyond all question](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Genealogy_of_Morals/Preface).” To extoll the virtues of well-being unreservedly is to neglect to ask a broader question about why the phenomenon of “creative health” is proving so attractive at this particular historical juncture.

Seen in the context of the multiple stresses and precarity of modern life, the “well-being” phenomenon might be read as an artefact of neoliberal work culture, the panacea for the ills of our inherently unhealthy systems. In Nietzsche’s terms, it is “hunger” rather than superabundance that is driving this change. The arts are being deployed instrumentally in a time of need.  Indeed, as creative health advocates readily acknowledge, the [wellness trend](https://iai.tv/articles/the-potential-and-danger-behind-the-rise-in-spirituality-auid-3370) is partly conditioned by economic factors such as the increasing burden placed on the public health services by epidemic levels of mental illness and the impact on productivity by chronic workplace absenteeism. The social prescription of art to manage economic crises is not to be deplored any more than it is to be applauded, but it is to be examined philosophically through a Nietzschean lens. In terms of value for life, the danger of adopting an uncritical acceptance of the well-being agenda is that it deflects attention from a host of problems, often systemic ones, which require much more thoroughgoing transformation.

To ask whether community arts programs are “good” for people who are struggling with poor mental health or long-term health conditions is to pose a question that is part of the problem. It risks privatizing anguish, individualizing mental health problems which may well be rational responses to the alienating effects of market pressures and digital overload. Validating “successful” art interventions on the grounds that they are ameliorating suffering may seem right and worthy, but comes at the cost of obscuring the broader determinants of impoverished health, especially if success is measured by returning burnt-out workers to economic productivity. This is where Nietzsche’s philosophy is most instructive. For him, [the role of art is not to repair individuals](https://iai.tv/articles/too-much-pleasure-causes-pain-anna-lembke-auid-2421) who have become “broken,” but to “break" things more definitively: to transform the systems within which our lives are seen and understood.

For Nietzsche, [art matters to life](https://iai.tv/articles/how-the-arts-save-us-auid-2486) because it expresses a creative power more primordial than science or technology, a power of transfiguration. According to Nietzsche, the Greeks of the tragic age reveal the unique capacity of art to give birth to life anew. For a mythically inspired people, not only are miracles possible—philosophy is conceived as a form of artistic invention beyond the limits of what is customary or taken as given. It is this exercise of the creative imagination that is stifled by a culture that rewards conformity to a marketized world. In his own era, Nietzsche despaired of the fetters of fear and convention that led to the passivity and bland contentment of the “[last human](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1998/1998-h/1998-h.htm).”  Rather than risk a questioning that might unsettle established truths, those who are “poorest in life” opt for a “[certain warm narrowness that keeps away fear and encloses one in optimistic horizons](https://philoslugs.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/the-gay-science-friedrich-nietzsche.pdf).” By contrast, those who are “richest in the fullness of life” can confront that which is terrible, threatening, absurd. Perhaps some of the most memorable works of art do precisely this.

Granted, this heroic rhetoric may not appeal to those who see “living well” as a less contrary affair, but art does not have to be grandiose to make great change. At the time of writing this piece, the arts are facing an unprecedented global crisis, with creative subjects being axed across the education sector in favor of “value for money” courses in science, technology, engineering, and maths. What Nietzsche’s thinking reinforces is that aesthetic perception offers powerful resistance to cultural norms, to the dogma that thought is only valuable if it is hooked up to demonstrable outcomes and yields easily quantifiable solutions.  The Greeks honored miracles in their art because they inhabited a world in which “[anything is possible at each moment](http://nietzsche.holtof.com/Nietzsche_various/on_truth_and_lies.htm).”  Art reminds us that [perception is itself a creative act](https://iai.tv/articles/art-is-never-a-representation-of-reality-auid-3492) and that the standardization of experience is neither inevitable nor necessary.  As the homogenization of life accelerates with the ingress of artificial intelligence technology, Nietzsche’s philosophy shows us that living well is liberation from seeing the world as a utile “resource” and that many ways of reinventing the world are yet possible.
