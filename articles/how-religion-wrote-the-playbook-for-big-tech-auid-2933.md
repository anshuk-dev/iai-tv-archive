# How religion wrote the playbook for Big Tech

*Studying religion from the outside in*

**By Paul Seabright** | *30th August 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,943 words*

![How religion wrote the playbook for Big Tech](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/the-divine-economy.webp)

---

**On the surface, Facebook is about as far removed from the Catholic Church as physically possible. But, perhaps by thinking of religions like platform businesses, we can get to the heart of how religions work, why they do what they do, and that the adage ‘if the product is free, you’re the product’ is just as applicable to religions as it is to YouTube. Paul Seabright argues that thinking of religions like an economist can discover valuable new insights into [The Divine Economy](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691133003/the-divine-economy?srsltid=AfmBOoqECxaM5Im-vXB9sanzaQzjiMsIttEAdTskLHi3qUKvK27_8gGx).**

Religion seems like an odd subject for the economist. After all, religion deals with gods and demons, the passage of the mortal soul and the moral laws that construct our reality. But why is any one religion the way it is? Why do they have rituals? And how do they inspire loyalty? One way to approach these questions is to investigate how religions respond to the social and material environment they share with other institutions.

When I began studying religion as a social scientist one of my motivations was to understand what kind of psychological phenomenon religiosity might be. I once envisaged studying religion from the inside out, beginning with religiosity and concluding with the different institutions that allow it to be expressed, thereby answering questions like “Why would religiosity lead one to do this or believe that?" [Now I find it more instructive to begin from the outside](http://iai.tv/articles/why-we-get-the-leaders-we-do-auid-2880), documenting the richness of religious institutions and then zooming in to understand the many ways in which they solicit the psychology of those who take part in them.

\_\_\_

An advantage of being an economist is that one needn’t be ideologically inclined to see businesses as boring, soul-destroying, or run by people who are only interested in profit

\_\_\_

This is not a novel insight. In his (aptly titled) *Varieties of Religious Experience*, William James wrote that “There is religious fear, religious love, religious joy and so forth. But religious love is only man’s natural emotion of love directed to a religious object; religious fear is only the ordinary fear of commerce, so to speak, the common quaking of the human breast in so far as the notion of divine retribution may arouse it; religious awe is the same organic thrill we feel in a forest at twilight or a mountain gorge; only this time it comes over us at the thought of our supernatural relations”.

We can now examine religion from James’s pluralistic perspective with a vastly greater range of social scientific tools than were at James’s disposal at the beginning of the 20th century. An advantage of being an economist is that one needn’t be ideologically inclined to see businesses as boring, soul-destroying, or run by people who are only interested in profit. As I write in *The Divine Economy*, “Religions are, after all, businesses….Like most businesses, they are many other things as well—they’re communities, objects of inspiration or anxiety to observers from outside, cradles of ambition and frustration to their recruits, theatres of fulfilment or despair to those who invest their lives or their savings within them. But they are legitimate businesses—a fact that should give pause to some of their detractors, but also empower those (their supporters as well as their critics) who believe they should be accountable to society as other businesses are*.*”

The economic perspective means we can take seriously the fact that to survive and prosper, religious movements need not only to [be psychologically persuasive](http://iai.tv/articles/politics-and-the-evolution-of-the-dogwhistle-auid-2869) but also to have a sound business model. The book examines what makes the difference between movements that thrive, and those that disappear without trace, like the overwhelming majority of those ever founded. A key notion is that of a platform: “Platforms are organizations that facilitate relationships that could not form or could not function as effectively, in the platforms’ absence. Platforms reward those who create and manage them by appropriating some of the benefits those relationships make possible.” Think of Meta’s Facebook or Amazon as secular equivalents.

\_\_\_

The members of religious movements are simultaneously its greatest assets, an insight that is central to the notion of platforms.

\_\_\_

This is another way of saying that religious movements are communities, but it puts the emphasis on which aspects of communities matter the most. I’ve been influenced in the adoption of this notion by my empirical observations that the members of religious movements are simultaneously its greatest assets, an insight that is central to the notion of platforms. Seeing how often people would tend to find spouses among fellow members, for instance, made me realize that the congregations were also functioning as dating platforms. The twist is that you wouldn’t go to church to find a spouse whose only reason for going to church was to find a spouse.

A central feature of the religious history of the world since the year 1900 is that religions which put their platform functions front and centre have flourished and expanded their allegiance as a share of the global population, often at the expense of local and folk religions that perform more specialized ritual functions. This doesn’t mean religious uniformity – far from it. But the religious diversity that interests me comes first from the circumstances that shaped the organizational diversity of religious movements, and not (as in most theologically-focused histories) from intrinsically motivated doctrinal diversity. Instead, doctrinal diversity makes sense mostly as reflecting the constraints of competing to attract, retain and motivate recruits. Having a unique selling point or theological ‘brand’, to put it in business terms.

There are many ways of developing a religious community as a platform consistently with these constraints. In the book I explore four kinds of decisions religious leaders must take, and that affect how the movement develops. These are questions about a movement’s *mission*, its *structure*, its *strategies*, and its *message*. Here are some examples of what we can learn.

The *missions* of religious movements can be extremely varied but are usually embodied at the beginning in the person and charisma of the founder (or founders). The greatest challenge facing any movement is how to ensure the mission survives the death, withdrawal or eclipse of its founder. In *The Divine Economy,* I look at the large literature on succession in secular businesses to highlight the factors that make for an effective transition once a founder dies or steps down.

\_\_\_

If the founder’s spirit can be everywhere, the successors must move in real space: size creates a need for decisions about the movement’s structure.

\_\_\_

There’s a tradeoff between continuity (which is often provided by a family succession) and the need for fresh ideas and perspectives, which typically requires input from outside. The latter is most important when the environment within which the movement operates is changing rapidly – as it was, for example, for the disciples of Jesus in the first few decades after his death, and when the arrival of a fresh entrepreneur in the form of Saul of Tarsus was crucial to the movement’s survival.

Any religious movement that survives the eclipse of its founder (and the great majority do not) may soon face a new challenge: that of handling a growing number of members. These members may join in search of many different things. What motivates a medieval crusader or a modern jihadi may be quite different from what motivates a Buddhist nun or a Sufi mystic.

But numbers change the trade-offs: if the original movement was limited by a constraint on the number of people who could interact personally with the founder, the absence of the founder removes that constraint. Many more members can now aspire to indirect rather than direct experience of the founder’s spirit, however literally or metaphorically they may understand that spirit to move among them. If the founder’s spirit can be everywhere, the successors must move in real space: size creates a need for decisions about the movement’s structure.

The *structures* of religious movements can be as varied as their missions, but structural decisions have consequences. To take just one example, the Catholic Church is an astonishingly flat hierarchic organization for one that is now so large. It has almost as many members as the populations of China and India, yet it functions with far fewer layers of responsibility, and much less comprehensive mechanisms of information gathering. It’s impossible for the higher levels in the Church’s hierarchy to monitor what is taking place at the lower levels to a degree consistent with their being held responsible for all that is done in the Church’s name.

\_\_\_

Religious violence not as the inexplicable bubbling up of some uncontrollable passion, but as the conscious implementation of a political and economic strategy

\_\_\_

This has been a source of strength in the past, since itallowed what Peter Brown has called “micro-Christendoms” to function more or less unhindered, relying on soft power to steer the immense vessel, and responding in a largely reactive fashion to the occasional grave crises that resulted. Local flexibility allowed the Catholic Church to align its engagement to what suited local communities. However, it caused major upheaval when the invention of printing made it impossible to keep the faithful from having direct access to the Bible, and competing Christian movements sprang up in Europe and later across the world. And today, in the age of social media, it has opened the Church to the (justified) charge of complicity in sexual and other forms of abuse, which have always existed but can no longer be kept hidden as before.

The *strategies* of religious movements have also been shaped by economic considerations, even when these are cloaked in theological justification. It’s sobering, for example, to investigate religious violence not as the inexplicable bubbling up of some uncontrollable passion, but as the conscious implementation of a political and economic strategy. Work by Sascha Becker and Luigi Pascali has shown that anti-Jewish pogroms in early modern Europe were much more intense in places where Christians and Jews were competing over economic resources. Saumitra Jha has shown similar findings for the Hindu-Muslim conflict in modern India, and Rohit Ticku, Anand Shrivastava, and Sriya Iyer corroborate this for temple desecrations in medieval India.

The violence between Christians and Jews in Europe, and between Hindus and Muslims in India, was never over matters of doctrine or ritual, since they were not competing for members but for secular economic advantage. Where Protestants and Catholics have engaged in mutual violence, it has been exactly because they were fighting over members, their greatest asset, for whom matters of doctrine and ritual were everything. In this as so often, it was not the doctrine itself that provoked the violence (since religious teachings contain both violent and pacific injunctions among which the movements’ leaders can pick and choose according to their purposes), but the resources over which the movements were competing.

It would be foolish to assert that the doctrines of religious movements are never an intrinsic source of subsequent economic and social developments, reflecting only the underlying conditions of religious competition. Still, the ways in which the constraints of economic competition shape those doctrines, and many of the other strategic choices that religious leaders make, deserve much more explicit attention than they have received up to now. It may be sobering, [but it’s the opposite of cynical](http://iai.tv/articles/misinformation-is-the-symptom-not-the-disease-daniel-walliams-auid-2690). If you think religious conflicts are the consequence of empirically undecidable metaphysical differences there is little you can do but shrug your shoulders, but to the extent that they have roots in the congestion of claims to scarce resources, you can think constructively about compromises. The platform model of religious competition both emphasizes the positive contribution of religious movements to human flourishing and locates the nature of religious differences to the messy consequences of the need for human communities to inhabit a shared space.
