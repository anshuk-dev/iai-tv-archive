# We need to stop talking about politics

*Political debate doesn't change minds, relationships and experiences do*

**By Sarah Stein Lubrano** | *30th September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,216 words*

![We need to stop talking about politics](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/we-need-to-stop-talking-about-politics.webp)

---

*Studies show political debates rarely change minds. A cornerstone of a thriving democracy is meant to be free and fair debate. Indeed, for most democratic nations around the world a highlight of an election cycle are leadership debates that are often widely viewed and discussed. But what if these political debates don't change people's minds, nor allow people to access new information or arguments in a useful way? Author of Don't Talk About Politics, Sarah Stein Lubrano, argues that for our politics to change people's minds it needs to create the social conditions for people to have meaningful conversations naturally. Whether we revive the cafe or pub culture that many people are priced out of, or recognise the promise of social media beyond profit motives to bring disparate people together. A healthy political culture doesn't just happen, it needs to be built.*

**Debate doesn’t work**

A friend of mine has often been a radio panelist. So have I. We’ve both noticed the same thing: we’ll often be asked to performatively disagree with the other panelists, to create more disagreement than actually exists. *It makes the show more interesting*, the producers say.

Maybe so. But creating a false sense of debate also leads to a wildly distorted sense of what’s possible or true. As Emily Maitlis, a former newsreader for the BBC, put it when describing the period of media coverage leading up to Brexit: “It might take our producers five minutes to find 60 economists who feared Brexit and five hours to find a sole voice who espoused it… But by the time we went on air we simply had one of each; we presented this unequal effort to our audience as balance. It wasn’t.”

\_\_\_

Researchers analysed the effects of 56 TV debates on 31 elections around the world from 1952 to 2017. The study tracked nearly 100,000 respondents, looking at whether debates helped undecided voters change their minds or caused decided voters to switch teams. They found no evidence for either.

\_\_\_

This regular aspect of staged debate demonstrates so much of what is wrong with our public sphere, the arena in which our society talks about political and social issues. The idea that issues are “up for debate” is often held up as a sign that democracy is working, and yet debating is often ineffective, polarising, and even counterproductive. Researchers analysed the effects of 56 TV debates on 31 elections around the world from 1952 to 2017. The study tracked nearly 100,000 respondents, looking at whether debates helped undecided voters change their minds or caused decided voters to switch teams. They found no evidence for either. An analysis in the *[Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/wonk/wp/2012/10/03/what-political-scientists-know-about-debates/)* drew on several studies done by a variety of political scientists, and similarly found that “the effects of debates on eventual votes are likely mild, and, in most cases, effectively nil.” This is remarkable given how much our culture emphasises debate and argumentation, from essay-writing in school to parliamentary protocol.

**Our friends and our lives change our minds**

It’s not just debate that is ineffective and counterproductive. As I demonstrate in my new [book](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/dont-talk-about-politics-9781399413916/), people’s views on political issues are seldom changed by exposure to arguments alone. There are several reasons for this. One is a series of cognitive biases, like [confirmation bias](https://www.verywellmind.com/what-is-a-confirmation-bias-2795024) and [cognitive dissonance theory](https://www.verywellmind.com/what-is-cognitive-dissonance-2795012), that tend to lead us to hold on to our existing beliefs.

\_\_\_

Two other things tend to structure what we believe instead: our own actions, and our relationships.

\_\_\_

Another reason arguments don’t change minds when it comes to politics is that two *other* things tend to structure what we believe instead: our own actions, and our relationships.

Yes, our actions tend to change our beliefs when it comes to politics (and not the other way around). Studies related to the “[gateway vs. getaway debate](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/gateway-or-getaway-testing-the-link-between-lifestyle-politics-and-other-modes-of-political-participation/EE37A2794AA982B44474C73D735BB3F4)” show that people often first try out a “gateway” action (say, recycling more) and then come to care more about an issue (say, climate change). Our experiences matter a lot too. Research on climate psychology shows experiencing climate breakdown (like a severe weather event) also tends to lead us to greater care for the environment. During a period of unemployment, having healthcare (say, through Obamacare), seems to prevent people from losing trust in society, while losing their healthcare access lowers people’s social trust dramatically. If denied an abortion, one is likely to change one’s views on abortion access at least a little (and not in the way you might guess!) What happens to us directly changes our political views more than argumentation.

And the other big factor in our political beliefs, it turns out, is [the *people* in our lives](https://iai.tv/video/the-psychology-behind-the-culture-wars), who serve as vectors of trust and windows onto new ways of thinking about the world. Research exploring the [social contact hypothesis](https://www.simplypsychology.org/contact-hypothesis.html) shows that if we make a friend at work in the right conditions, this will often make us less racist or more in favour of gay rights. Our friends help us care about issues in ways we didn’t before, as we come to care about them. Even new acquaintances can do this, in the right circumstances.

\_\_\_

These studies are, I’d argue, unintentional demonstrations of, and statistical evidence for, the way our friendships and other relationships change our minds about politics more than abstract arguments could.

\_\_\_

We can see some of what this looks like in the practice of [deep canvassing,](https://deepcanvass.org/) one of the few interventions that has proved strikingly effective on people’s beliefs. This technique starts by knocking on a door and then asking the person being canvassed to rate where they stand on an issue on a scale of 1-10. Generally, people tend not to choose an extreme answer. The deep canvasser then asks the person they are canvassing why they aren’t at a “zero” or “ten” in their beliefs, whichever they’re closer to on the spectrum. This lets the person articulate their views themselves, in a way that allows for ambiguity. That process seems to help them be open to a shift in beliefs. Next, the canvasser shares their own experiences, which often contradict the views of the person being canvassed. For example, a canvasser might listen to someone’s views on why people should go to the bathroom of their assigned sex, and then share their own experience of how difficult such a policy makes their life.

Remarkably, even during such a short period of relationship building and narrative exchange, the canvassed person’s views generally [move significantly, and permanently](https://bookshop.org/p/books/how-minds-change-the-surprising-science-of-belief-opinion-and-persuasion-david-mcraney/16895921):

the overall shift… measured in [one deep canvassing study about trans rights] in Miami was greater than ‘the opinion change that occurred from 1998 to 2012 towards gay men and lesbians in the United States’... a mind change of much less than that could easily rewrite laws, win a swing state, or turn the tide of an election ... Had the canvassers been experts, had they continued having conversations over several weeks, had those conversations been longer, the evidence suggests the impact would have been enormous.

This is promising amid our current bleak political landscape, but deep canvassing is expensive, time-consuming work, and one has to be trained to do it. Instead, what is described above is a model for how constructive conversations (rather than arguments) can change our minds, from the opening up of ambiguity, to the building of a relationship, to the sharing of personal experiences. These studies are, I’d argue, unintentional demonstrations of, and statistical evidence for, the way our friendships and other relationships change our minds about politics more than abstract arguments could.

**Build social infrastructure and take back Twitter**

To make change at scale, what is needed is not more deep canvassing but rather the kinds of spaces, or [social infrastructure](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02723638.2021.2003609#d1e146), that allow people to have conversations like this naturally, as they encounter new friends. Social infrastructure is, quite simply, the spaces and places that allow people to gather relatively easily and cheaply, especially with neighbours and relative strangers. Unfortunately, those of us in the UK and the US are facing the destruction and decline of much of the important social infrastructure around us—from shrinking green spaces to closing pubs.

But we could reverse this trend—and we should want to allow people to go back out in the world and try ideas on for size, first through new conversations and friendships, and later through actions. Many spaces and groups allow us to engage in both; consider, for example, a parenting group that then gets one involved in creating safer playgrounds and streets, a workplace union that pushes for better working conditions, a gardening club that takes on ecological issues, and so on.

Online spaces, in stark contrast, tend not to build up the same kind of momentum in our lives. This is striking given the way online spaces like Twitter have (at least in past digital eras) seemed like [hopeful places for democracy](https://iai.tv/video/changing-the-world) for some users and commentators. As the journalist Vincent Bevins notes, around the time of the Occupy Protests and the Arab Spring,

Andrew Sullivan, for *The Atlantic*, published a piece titled ‘The Revolution Will be Twittered.’ In the *New York Times*, Nicholas Kristof claimed that ‘in the quintessential 21st-century conflict ... on the one side are government thugs firing bullets ... on the other side are young protestors firing ‘tweets’; … Mark Pfeiffle, a former deputy national security advisor in the George W. Bush Administration, tried to give the Nobel Prize to Twitter.

I suspect Twitter (or “X”) won’t be getting that prize now. But the point is larger than any one platform. In practice, online spaces tend not to function nearly as well when it comes to building meaningful relationships or actions for people. We are generally less likely to encounter people different from ourselves, or to build a relationship of meaningful size and duration with them. It’s very unlikely we’d have a conversation that looks like deep canvassing on Twitter, in part because Twitter is engineered to reward hot takes and short form, provocative content. More than this, the social contact hypothesis literature mentioned above suggests that to build meaningful relationships that change our prejudices, it’s necessary to feel we have shared goals with those with whom we differ. This is rarely true online.

\_\_\_

Research shows those engaged in activism are happier, and they see their lives as more meaningful, than those people who hold similar views but do not engage in activism.

\_\_\_

To make online spaces truly democratic, and to make them places where we could come to better understanding, we would have to be able to radically reshape them so they mix us differently and point us to shared possibilities for action. And that probably won’t happen so long as these platforms are owned by large corporations and belligerent billionaires. It’s more profitable and beneficial to those in power to keep people angry, scared, distracted, and scrolling. If we can’t nationalise such platforms, we should seek to replace them, or change their ownership structure to put control in the hands of the users.

**The Happy Activist as the Good Political Thinker**

For now, the best political thinking will be happening offline, and it will look remarkably like community organising, activism, and rebuilding the social fabric. All this is far more difficult than simply debating things, on or offline. But there is an upside. Research shows [those engaged in activism](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2010/mar/02/brain-food-activism-makes-you-happy) [are happier](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/political-activism-good-you), [and they see their lives as more meaningful](https://inthesetimes.com/article/smile-youre-an-activist), than those people who hold similar views but do not engage in activism. Yes, really. Despite the activist burnout, internal splits, and general pessimism of many activist groups, statistically, activists are happier than non-activists who share their beliefs. There’s even some evidence that this is causal: that it’s the activism itself that is making them happier.

And this does not surprise me, because activism involves so many of the things that psychology research demonstrates are the sources of meaning and joy in life: agency, community, action. I’m particularly struck by the research that suggests the happiest activists are those who feel their actions have the most direct effect, or most affect their opponents. Thinking the world is on fire seems to more makes you a lot less psychologically well, but thinking that the world is on fire and that you can do something about it with other people makes things bearable again, even inspiring and meaningful.

So, while it might be ironic to write it here, on a forum for the free exchange of ideas, when it comes to politics we should cease the focus on debating things or leaving them to “[the marketplace of ideas](https://iai.tv/articles/the-marketplace-of-ideas-must-embrace-the-irrational-auid-3159).”  The things that really change people’s minds on politics lie elsewhere.

In fact, we should rethink what it means to think about politics altogether. Politics is not about “independent” thinking, as in thinking on one’s own. Rather, it’s about thinking together with others, and doing so well by relying wisely and choosing varied networks of people. It’s also about trying out actions in the world, living so that we encounter new ideas in the flesh. We should consider those who live out their ideas with others—in groups, in clubs, in activism, in organising—the best political thinkers. And we should seek such a life for ourselves, both to become happier people and to make the world a better place.
