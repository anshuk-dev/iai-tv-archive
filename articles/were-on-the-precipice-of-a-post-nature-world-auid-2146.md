# Harari and Žižek warn of a post-nature world

*The two thinkers share a stage at HowTheLightGetsIn*

**By Alexis Papazoglou** | *3rd June 2022* | *Read time: approx. 5 mins* | *1,006 words*

---

*On a fine summer day In Hay, at the packed main stage of HowTheLightGetsIn, the world’s biggest philosophy festival, Yuval Noah Harari and Slavoj Žižek appeared together on a panel for the first time. Nature was on the agenda. Is it an existential threat to us, or a source we can continue using freely to improve our lives? It’s not nature we should be worried about, both thinkers agreed. It’s what comes next: a world post-nature. [**Watch the video debate here.**](https://iai.tv/video/nature-friend-or-foe)*

*![Harari Zizek](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw0MDBd/Harari-Zizek.jpg)*

*Yuval Noah Harari and Slavoj Žižek debate the nature of nature. Günes Taylor hosts.*

If there is such a thing as celebrity intellectuals, Yuval Noah Harari and Slavoj Žižek fit the bill better than most - even if the latter flinches at being called one. Both thinkers have found popular success beyond the narrow confines of their academic specializations – medieval history for Harari, Hegelian philosophy and Lacanian psychoanalysis for Žižek. The world, it seems, wants to know what they think on all the most pressing issues of our times, from the war in Ukraine to the climate crisis and our posthuman future. Which is what brought them to HowTheLightGetsIn at Hay, the world’s biggest philosophy festival, to debate the question of nature: friend or foe.

The answer is unsurprisingly nuanced: nature is neither our friend nor our enemy. As Zizek and Harari so often claim, everyone is failing to ask the right questions. Because we’re about to enter a post-nature era, and that will change everything.

![PHOTO 2022 06 02 23 25 06](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw0MDBd/PHOTO-2022-06-02-23-25-06.jpg)

After a long period of Enlightenment thought that saw nature conquered by reason and tamed by technology, its place in society is back on the agenda in a big way. The COVID pandemic and the climate crisis are both reminders that we have neither fully understood nature, nor taken conclusive ownership and control over it. But being confronted with it once again has meant that people have interpreted nature either as inherently good, in contrast to the morally corrupt humans, or as vengeful, punishing us for our hubris in exploiting the environment. Nonsense, proclaimed Harari and Žižek in agreement. Nature is neither good nor bad – it’s outside of morality. Žižek went further still, and in his usual colourful way, undermined the image of a nurturing nature with the line “If nature is our mother, then she’s a bitch!” As for the vengeance part, the universe is indifferent towards us.

\_\_\_

## Despite being a self-proclaimed Hegelian, even Žižek said “I’m not an idealist, I’m a naturalist!”

\_\_\_

The agreement, that surprised the speakers (“When will the knives come out?” wondered Žižek) continued when both of them seemed to argue that the distinction between the natural and the non-natural was itself, well, artificial. The idea that all that exists can be understood as part of nature has been around since antiquity but still animates much contemporary philosophy. Despite being a self-proclaimed Hegelian, even Žižek said “I’m not an idealist, I’m a naturalist!” It might not seem such an original thought, but over the past few centuries, our scientific and technological successes had convinced us that humans are somehow above nature, masters of the universe. Even today it sounds strange to our ears to call a nuclear reactor or the COVID vaccine, or the war in Ukraine “natural”. But in some sense, they are. Their existence doesn’t violate any natural law and they are made of the same physical stuff as everything else.

On the other hand, Žižek stressed, what we call “nature” is always culturally mediated. What we refer to as “nature” today and what we referred to as “nature” in the 16th century are not the same thing. And the way we appeal to nature is deeply ideological. When homosexuality was considered “unnatural”, Harari reminded the audience, that was a political statement, and a misguided one at that: nature on its own doesn’t yield moral rules, it doesn’t tell us what’s right and what’s wrong. Nature just is.

\_\_\_

## We are on the verge of creating what Harari called “inorganic lifeforms”.

\_\_\_

But all this might be about to be put to a test. We are on the verge of creating what Harari called “inorganic lifeforms”, referring to advanced Artificial Intelligence. Will we still be able to understand those as natural? As part of a similar trajectory, we are about to start changing our biological makeup, changing our nature, if you like, in radical ways. This might excite some transhumanists, who dream utopian dreams, and the scientists who are focussed on using these tools for solving narrow, specific problems in their fields, but Harari strikes a more sombre tone. This is what ruthless dictators have been dreaming of. In the past, when dictators fell from power, at least what they left behind them was still human. In the future, that might no longer be the case.

Stalin, Žižek interjects, wanted to do exactly that! Create an army of genetically engineered workers who could labour beyond the limits of any human and survive on some bare sustenance. The way Stalin tried to do this of course involved an anecdote that only Žižek could get away with recounting.

![PHOTO 2022 06 02 23 25 02](assets/Uploads/_resampled/ResizedImageWzYwMCw0MDBd/PHOTO-2022-06-02-23-25-02.jpg)

So if we are about to enter this post-human era, what about nature? Life 2.0, Žižek warned us, will also change Life 1.0. Nature will no longer be nature, that too will be fundamentally altered. Harari gestured in the same direction.  Enhancing humans will also have the effect of downgrading humanity in its current configuration. If we genetically engineer humans to be more intelligent, braver, more efficient, that will eventually lead to the disappearance of all our other features, those that will be deemed less desirable by the human engineers. Selecting for some features will mean the disappearance of others.

The vision of the future that Zizek and Harari imagine goes radically beyond the idea of AI putting humans out of work, or enhancing our social frameworks. But do these developments really mean we are about to enter a post-nature world? Or is this yet another anthropocentric view of things? Is this really post-nature, or just post-human? Or might those two futures be one and the same thing?
