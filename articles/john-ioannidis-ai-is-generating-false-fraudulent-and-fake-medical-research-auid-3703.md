# Why most published research findings are still false

*94% of medical treatments lack evidence of benefits*

**By John Ioannidis** | *14th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,772 words*

![Why most published research findings are still false](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/John-Ionnidis-AI-is-generating-false-fraudulent-and-fake-research.webp)

---

*The US spends nearly 20% of its GDP on health and publishes 3 million biomedical papers a year. So why is life expectancy stalling, or perhaps even falling? Stanford professor of medicine **John Ioannidis** argues that vested interests and basic research mistakes are destroying biomedicine, and that two decades after his bombshell paper, “Why Most Published Research Findings Are False,” the situation has only worsened. AI is generating a new deluge of fraudulent research, and medicine is drifting ever further from quality evidence. In this interview with the IAI's Alasdair Craig, he lays bare the crisis, and suggests ways we might begin to fix it.*

**Alasdair Craig:** **You’ve said that “we need to fight against medicine. It’s becoming really dangerous.” Why did you say that?**

John Ioannidis: Spending on health in the United States is [close to 20%](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet) of GDP, and in other countries it’s also increasing. We have about three million papers published in biomedicine every year. Most of them are probably subpar. Many of them are false or misleading. [One study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00210-025-04275-9) estimated that 5.8% of biomedical papers published in 2023 were entirely fake, and the proportion is increasing. [Another study](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(22)00100-7/abstract) has found that among 1,567 medical interventions, only 5.6% had high-quality evidence supporting their benefits (that is, over 94% lacked high-quality evidence supporting their benefits).

We have lots of discussions that are misinformation and full of very weird thinking about medicine. So we’re entangled in a mess that is not leading to better health. In fact, [life expectancy in the United States has not really increased](https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy) or has even gone down.

We see the same experience in many other countries. We are not getting what we should out of an enterprise that includes some amazing researchers and some very well-intentioned people.

**You’ve argued that** [**evidence-based medicine**](iai-academy/courses/info?course=medicine-the-need-for-a-new-paradigm) **has been “hijacked.” What do you think is going wrong in the research process?**

[We have a lot of research that is not useful](https://iai.tv/video/why-most-published-research-findings-are-false-john-ioannidis). I’m not talking about basic science, which we do need. Actually, we need more of it. By “basic science” I mean research that has practically no deliverables: we don’t really know what we will find and what domain or field or disease it might be useful for eventually.

When I say that a lot of research is not useful, I’m talking about research that tries to generate evidence to enable us to treat specific diseases. Multiple factors make that kind of research very unreliable at times.

For example, there are very strong conflicts of interest. There is research that is run with a specific ideology in mind. There is very poor use of statistical methods and questionable research practices that lead to “significant” results that are not really significant. There is a lot of uncontrolled bias. There are [poor study designs](https://iai.tv/articles/medical-trials-are-not-the-whole-truth-auid-2989). There is fragmentation of research in small studies that are meaningless, where if they do get some signal, it’s very likely that it will be false.

\_\_\_

Evidence-based medicine has long become finance-based medicine, and it is moving towards becoming corruption-based medicine.

\_\_\_

And there is fraud that is probably getting worse, especially with the advent of artificial intelligence. We have a very large number of papers now that can be generated by AI, and that can be entirely false and fraudulent and fake—besides the classic fraud that we had in the past. [A recent study](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2420092122) found that scientific fraud is currently growing at much faster rates than the scientific enterprise as a whole.

So there are multiple threats to the research ecosystem that are making it very difficult to obtain reliable evidence.

**What is the solution to the problem of vested interests in medical research? Should we move to more state-funded medical research, or would that just produce its own biases?**

I think [we need more independent research](https://iai.tv/video/medicine-magic-misunderstanding), and public funding has a better starting point for being independent. When it comes to basic biomedical research, I think we do need public funding because, otherwise, who is going to fund it? If there’s no clear deliverable, it’s unlikely that for-profit enterprises will be willing to fund it.

When it comes to evidence-based research, I would argue that, again, we need public funding if that can be coupled with independence. So, not public funding that some public officer would be controlling for the benefit of some industry sponsor, but genuinely independent investigators.

We should also have industry involved in this, of course. But I think that they should be focused on the translational path, meaning investing in delivering the best products in terms of interventions, diagnostic tests, devices, technologies, and then have independent researchers with public funds, who would be testing those rigorously and without bias.

Currently, what we see is the opposite. We see that industry is testing its own products, and of course, it has every vested interest in generating the results that will be best for its agendas. Meanwhile, government spending mostly focuses on delivering products for industry. So, there’s a misallocation of funds. [Evidence-based medicine has been hijacked](https://www.jclinepi.com/article/S0895-4356(16)00147-5/abstract) by stakeholders who do not really focus on getting the best outcomes for society at large, but mostly for their own portfolios. Evidence-based medicine has long become finance-based medicine, and it is moving towards becoming corruption-based medicine.

There’s also the problem of people moving between government and industry. Officials, after they finish their government career, often move into industry, which I think is problematic, because even during their government tenure, they’re often thinking about their industry careers, and that affects their decisions and what kind of research they propose. So, publicly funded research is not a panacea.

**I’ve heard you say that medical scientists are shifting around our definitions of disease, and that this often makes people seem like they are sick when in fact they aren’t.****What made you say that? And how do we solve this problem?**

We do see that for many conditions, there is a [widening of the definition of who is sick](https://iai.tv/video/the-diagnosis-delusion). This applies to almost any condition that you can think of that may have a large market, where products, interventions, devices and technologies may be sold and profited from. Information bombardment, [including on social media](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2830758), aims to make more people feel sick.

This doesn’t mean that these conditions do not need to be treated. For example, we know that diabetes is serious. However, one can [shift the definition](https://iai.tv/articles/naming-wont-solve-mortality-auid-3245) of *prediabetes* to increasingly lower thresholds. Likewise, we know that hypertension is a major problem and it needs to be treated. However, one can move the boundaries of what counts as hypertension and where we need to start with medical treatment as opposed to other ways of trying to control it.

So, across the board, and especially for common conditions, we see that the definitions of who is in need of medicine—especially expensive medicine—are being widened [to make more people feel or believe that they are sick so that they need to be (expensively) treated](https://iai.tv/articles/cancer-screening-harms-more-people-than-it-saves-auid-3264).

Again, we have a major crisis in mental health, and we need more attention and more resources to deal with this. But many of the definitions that are being pushed are making practically [the large majority of the population has a mental disease](https://iai.tv/video/mental-health-in-an-age-of-over-diagnosis).

With dementia, we currently do not have very effective interventions. But the “solution,” unfortunately, has been to promote medications that are not very effective and to broaden the type of measurements that we use, to try to show that these interventions have some benefit. Well, in fact, that “benefit” is probably not clinically relevant.

So we do see a major problem where every specialty is trying to push the boundaries of who is in need of treatment, who should use the products, the drugs, the interventions that are marketed, and how one can create blockbuster interventions with billions in profits for pharmaceutical companies.

**Finally, can we think about** [**the role of experts in public life**](https://iai.tv/video/the-ignorance-of-experts)**? Evidence-based medicine tries to provide a framework that physicians should use when they’re treating their patients. I wonder whether you think there should be a similar framework that is used by experts when they make public pronouncements about health care?**

We do need expertise. However, we see very often that experts go beyond their remit. And in science communication, it’s very easy to be wrong, to convey more certainty than there is about specific conditions, to have our own personal biases spill over into that communication, to try to promote our own agenda, to ensure that our research will continue to be funded or to receive attention. So experts, in a sense, are at the other pole of evidence-based medicine. Evidence-based medicine, in a way, was launched as an antithesis to experts, to expert-based medicine—as a cure to expert-based medicine.

What we see, however, is that experts have absorbed the language of evidence-based medicine, and they make their proclamations saying that what they convey is evidence-based, which sometimes is really harmful, because these proclamations deviate from what the evidence would say.

**But should an expert not try to make sure that what they say is evidence-based? Sure, if it’s in fact not, then that’s a problem. But presumably, they should always aim for that standard. For example, in a podcast called Plenary Session in July 2021, you said that doctors were going crazy and intubating people with Covid-19 who didn't need to be intubated, and that this was causing deaths.****And you were criticized for that. Critics said that you lacked strong evidence for that claim when you made it, and that it contributed to a damaging loss of public confidence in healthcare professionals. That’s where I wonder whether bringing evidence-based medicine into the sphere of experts might not be helpful?**

I think that it’s very difficult to communicate science accurately. We have to be careful, because different statements can be taken out of context. For example, you mentioned one example where if you isolate that piece, it may sound like I was crazy. And perhaps I am, and I’m sure that I make many mistakes. However, that was in the broader context of saying that when the pandemic hit, we had very little knowledge about how to treat patients most effectively. And our success rates with treatment were probably very low. We had no efficient treatments, no interventions that we knew how to use, and therefore our outcomes were suboptimal. This is one example where well-intentioned people may say something that may be taken out of context, especially in a [polarized environment](https://iai.tv/video/overcoming-opposition-paul-dolan). It can be completely misconstrued or may be used for other purposes.

So we do need to be very careful with science communication. And I think that this is not easy, especially when there are many entities out there who are trying to use information for their own purposes. There’s no easy way to solve that problem.
