# In the age of tragedy, everyone's a sadomasochist

*We should not mix our pleasure with our pain*

**By Paul Kottman** | *20th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,676 words*

![In the age of tragedy, everyone's a sadomasochist](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/in-the-age-of-tragedy-everyones-a-sadomasochist.webp)

---

*We think of tragedy as exploring suffering, but it actually teaches us to enjoy it. From Plato to Freud, philosopher **Paul Kottman** argues that our appetite for tragic drama cultivates a sadomasochistic tendency — converting pain into pleasure — that ultimately threatens our capacity for love, loss, and genuine wellbeing. To live well we must resist the temptation to aestheticise our suffering and learn to keep pleasure and pain apart.*

“The idea of pleasures that are not somehow painful,” writes Adam Phillips, “has become literally inconceivable, so wedded are we to our perpetual dismay.” Might pure pleasure, free of pain, be a mere *idea*—one that could become “inconceivable”?

It is worth wondering whether the [difference between pleasure and pain](https://iai.tv/articles/too-much-pleasure-causes-pain-anna-lembke-auid-2421) is nothing more than a theoretical construct, a fragile cognitive achievement. In practice, after all, we seem highly adept and even eager to find possible pleasures in every sorrow. One of the most surprising things about suffering is how easily we can be tempted to enjoy it, and how creative we are in cultivating that enjoyment. Likewise, [it seems natural to feel that pain](https://iai.tv/articles/on-the-cusp-of-the-unnatural-auid-1685) somehow, somewhere attends every pleasure. Our raw capacity to feel pleasure and pain may not come equipped with the capacity to tell the difference between the one and the other.

Perhaps this is why our species developed the ability to laugh or cry. The fact that smilingly “pleased” people typically *look* different from the weeping or the suffering is, after all, oddly encouraging. For, our laughter and tears offer signs, or reassurance, that pleasure is really different from pain. Or, that the difference between pain and pleasure is not, in the end, just theoretical but really is “there” in bodily life. Laughter and tears are physically palpable, reassuringly sensuous, not mere ideas.

In the final book of [Plato’s *Republic*](https://www.sciencetheearth.com/uploads/2/4/6/5/24658156/plato_-_the_republic.pdf), when Socrates at last lays out his “greatest charge” against the mimetic poets and tragedians, he emphasizes that watching dramatic imitations of weeping or laughter is intrinsically pleasurable. When we “hear Homer or some other tragedian imitating one of the heroes sorrowing and making a long lamenting speech or singing and beating his breast, you know that we enjoy it, give ourselves up to it…” And the same applies, Socrates says, to “what provokes laughter” (*Republic* 605d, 606c).

On the one hand—Socrates is pointing out that human beings, “by nature,” “hunger for the satisfaction of weeping and wailing,” just as we hunger for the pleasure of laughter. This is why we enjoy comedies and tragedies alike. The pleasures of weeping and laughing to which the poets appeal seem like “pure” pleasures—free from pain. “You know that we enjoy it,” says Socrates.

On the other hand—and this is what disturbs Socrates—the pleasure we take in watching tragedies is a pleasure taking in suffering: “the satisfaction of weeping and wailing.” It is, to use a more familiarly modern term, sadomasochistic pleasure.

\_\_\_

The worry is not that we might miss out on pure pleasures, but that we might miss out on pain.

\_\_\_

Now, one could be forgiven for thinking that all Socrates is referring to here is the pleasure we take in watching a representation of suffering, as when we watch a tragic play; a kind of aesthetic enjoyment taken in imitation, not the real thing. Aristotle, in the *Poetics*, suggests something along these lines in his own remarks on [the appeal of tragic poetry](https://iai.tv/articles/tragedy-as-self-deception-auid-1827), when he notes that we enjoy seeing imitations of things (such as dead bodies) that, were they real, would be unpleasant.

But Socrates here wants to say the opposite. We do not enjoy weeping because we enjoy artistic imitations, says Socrates; rather, [artistic imitations of suffering are enjoyable](https://iai.tv/articles/baldwin-vs-nietzsche-how-to-overcome-tragedy-auid-2970) because we like to weep and wail. We find it sadomasochistically pleasurable. There is a “part of the soul [psyche]” that just “hungers for the satisfaction of weeping.” And it is this hunger, this sadomasochistic propensity, to which artists and tragic poets cater. This sadomasochistic “satisfaction in weeping” troubles Socrates because it blurs any difference between pure pleasure and pure pain—between what provokes laughter and what provokes tears. After all, if weeping is satisfying, then it cannot be reliably taken as a sign of distress.

But Socrates is disturbed by something further, too. Part of the sadomasochistic enjoyment we take in weeping, he notes, is in watching others weep and in pitying them. We take pleasure in watching the sufferings of others, for instance in [tragic plays](https://iai.tv/articles/shakespeare-vs-wittgenstein-the-fight-for-meaning-auid-3066), because it allows us to feel pity for the heroes. But “that enjoyment of other people’s sufferings is necessarily transferred to our own,” says Socrates, “and the pitying part, if it is nourished and strengthened on the sufferings of others, won’t easily be held in check when we ourselves suffer.” What Socrates finds disturbing in tragedies, in other words, is that it helps bring about situations in which the “enjoyment” we take in pity “won’t easily be held in check when we ourselves suffer.” Our sadism turns into its conjoined twin, masochism. The enjoyment we take in others’ suffering starts to accompany our own suffering. We find ourselves in danger, Socrates suggests, of developing and refining our propensity to take pleasure in our own suffering. We might as well call it “self-pity.”

\_\_\_

The difference between pain and pleasure matters because, without it, we may never find out what we really care about, or how.

\_\_\_

Strikingly, Socrates’ worry here is something like the inverse of the one voiced by Adam Phillips above. The worry is not that we might miss out on pure pleasures, but that we might miss out on pain. Our capacity to endure pain as pain—and not as another chance for further pleasure—is “corrupted” by the part of our psyche that loves to weep and to pity. And it is corrupted further by the tragedians who prey upon that part of our psychology.

But why should the experience of pain without pleasure be something we should worry about missing, or anesthetizing? Wouldn’t we rather have pleasure with our pain, as much as possible?

In our “private pain,” says Socrates, “we pride ourselves if we are able to keep quiet.” “Keeping quiet” seems to indicate a kind of [stoic resolve](https://iai.tv/articles/the-stoic-self-auid-1572), but I think it also means: enduring the pain without turning it into pleasure. The difference between a life in which pain can be converted to pleasure and a life in which pain is not converted to pleasure is the difference between a life that sustains loss and a life that does not. And a life without loss is, as Freud says in *Mourning* *and Melancholia*, a life without love. It is a life with nothing to lose. And nothing to long for.

Without love and loss, we may still laugh and cry. But we would have no reason to do so. We would be left only with mad laughter, mad wailing. Having reasons for our laughter and tears is what underwrites, not the difference between pain and pleasure, but the significance of that difference: its mattering. The difference between pain and pleasure matters because, without it, we may never find out what we really care about, or how.

As [Jonathan Lear has pointed out, Plato depicts a proto-psychoanalytic “structure” of the psyche](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4182410). In Plato’s picturing, the psyche is divided or conflicted within itself, just as the polis is. For its part, the appetitive part of the psyche is incapable of adequately enduring pain without turning it into pleasure—just as the appetite-whetting parts of the polis have a propensity for cultivating sadomasochistic pleasures. The appetitive part of the psyche hungers for pleasure, and the tragedians—if allowed into the polis—all too successfully feed that hunger.

On the other hand, the rational part of the psyche (and the polis) does not deny the appetitive part; rather, it regulates it in order to prevent all appetites from becoming sadomasochistic (of course, the latter is an outcome that would suit the tragic poets just fine). Another way of putting this would be to say that the rational part of the psyche is what allows the entire psyche both to be internally conflicted and to recognize the stakes of that internal conflict. The appetites and passions, on their own, cannot do this.

So, to return to the problem with which I began, if the difference between pleasure and pain can be blurred by our feelings of pleasure and pain—if pain can be enjoyed, and if unpainful pleasures are always elusive—then how are we to hold fast to love and loss? Or, more to the point, why do so? Why endure pain without trying to squeeze pleasure from it? “So that we may become better and happier instead of worse and more wretched,” says Socrates in answer to the latter question, before admitting: “I have no other way to say it.”

We may wonder whether invoking this difference between greater happiness or wellbeing [*eudaimonia*] and wretchedness merely repeats the sadomasochistic problem on a different level. In other words, some might begin to suspect that Socrates has a sadomasochistic streak of his own: “For the sake of wellbeing, endure pain and loss!” And yet, crucial to Socrates’ picture of the difference between wellbeing and wretchedness is the fact that one cannot live well and wretchedly at the same time – whereas one must, sadomasochistically speaking, [enjoy pain as pleasure in the same moment](https://iai.tv/articles/killing-pain-kills-pleasure-brock-bastian-auid-2678). A good life and a wretched life are different lives, different possibilities in life.

So, it is important to Socrates that any appeal to wellbeing, unlike invitations to pleasure, requires that we bear in mind a proto-psychoanalytic picture of both the psyche and the polis as internally conflicted.

Tragedies, of course, are eager to show the polis as internally conflicted. But tragedy mistakenly treats all internal conflicts as political. And by appealing only to our hunger for pleasure, tragedy ignores, or remains unconscious of, the part of us that has reasons to cry or laugh, to endure loss or pain without wallowing in it. As we weigh our own therapeutic needs, it might be worth returning to Plato’s ancient question: By keeping suffering and pleasure safe from one another—by resisting the siren song of the tragic poets—can we promote wellbeing?
