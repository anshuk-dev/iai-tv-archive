# The age of Left-Right politics is over

*Far-right is the wrong label for Trump*

**By Jean-Paul Faguet** | *7th November 2024* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,607 words*

![The age of Left-Right politics is over](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/political-division-end-of-left-right.webp)

---

*Politics in the West is fundamentally changing. The likes of Donald Trump, Marine Le Pen and Germany’s AfD are often seen as a rising ‘Far Right’. This is wrong, argues Jean-Paul Faguet, because it implies that these politicians exist on the same Left-Right axis that has long defined Western politics. That axis is increasingly irrelevant. The traditional working class is disappearing: most people, from office workers to Uber drivers, self-identify more as individualistic entrepreneurs than part of a working-class. The real political struggle – in which Trump and co. are already engaged – is to define a new axis of political competition, which reflects society’s real dividing lines.*

As the circus of the American election ground to its finale, many may have wondered if this is the future of politics throughout the West. Does the rise of Germany’s AfD, Geert Wilders in Holland, France’s Marine Le Pen, and others herald the end of politics as we thought we knew it? Was the Brexit referendum a fever dream, or does it mark a permanent shift in politics in the UK? We tend to ask such questions in national terms, or, less often, at the level of Europe. Much of the commentary is descriptive: *Look at these people! They’re so different from what we’re used to!* But what’s going on is far bigger and far more important – a seismic shift in how societies organise their political competition, and something that’s happening in many countries around the world. The old politics of Left-versus-Right is being replaced by something new.

\_\_\_

People who work – the large majority – no longer think of themselves as ‘workers’ with a common culture and common interests. We are all free agents now

\_\_\_

Let us begin with an extreme example of this phenomenon from the developing world, [from my own research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0032329219845944?journalCode=pasa). Here the shift happened sooner and its effects were bigger than in Europe or North America – so far, at least. This allowed a new equilibrium to emerge faster, which we can study. That country is Bolivia.

*Bolivia?* I can hear readers’ disbelief. *That poor South American country up in the mountains? The* coup d’état *champion of the world?* Yes, Bolivia. It has suffered many of the same economic shocks, technological disruptions, and social and environmental changes as far more developed countries. But Bolivia’s democracy was never as institutionalized as those of richer, more developed countries, and so the disintegration of its political system began earlier, and proceeded faster, than elsewhere. Adjusting heavily for context, Bolivia is a canary in the coalmine, offering useful insights into how political disintegration works, and clues about where it may be going in Europe and North America.

**Political collapse in Bolivia**

Bolivia is famous for high levels of political instability since independence two centuries ago. What is far less well-known is that in modern times its political party system was remarkably robust. Through multiple coups, stratospheric inflation, guerrilla insurgencies, and numerous international price shocks, Bolivia’s political party system endured. Every time the generals retreated to their barracks, it was not just the same parties but often the same leaders who returned the country to democracy. To drive the point home, Hernán Siles Suazo was Victor Paz Estenssoro’s Vice President, Jaime Paz Zamora was Siles Zuazo’s Vice President, Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada was Paz Estenssoro’s Minister of Planning, and Gen. Hugo Banzer was the coalition partner who kept Paz Zamora in power. This was not the sort of chaotic political party system that we see in many developing countries, where parties are little more than personal vehicles with little ideology or organisation, appearing and disappearing erratically. This was the alternation in power of well-established parties neatly arrayed along a common axis defined by the left-wing MIR, the centrist MNR, and the right-wing ADN. In Bolivian terms, ‘left’ meant pro-worker, pro-peasant and pro-poor, with higher taxes and more extensive government expenditures; ‘right’ meant pro-business and pro-landowner, with lower taxes and a smaller state.

Between 2003–2005, this entire system collapsed. Not only did massive demonstrations force the elected President to resign and flee the country, but a series of electoral and deeper political earthquakes led all the main parties described above, plus others, to lose votes, flounder, and literally cease to exist. What happened? Common understandings are based on journalistic accounts that stress a series of popular mobilisations against water privatization in Cochabamba, and a gas pipeline to the old enemy Chile. While these events certainly occurred, they were not the causes of Bolivia’s political collapse. It’s fanciful to think that a system that withstood 53,000% inflation, several guerrilla insurgencies (Che Guevara was killed in Bolivia), and huge international price shocks was felled by a plan to sell a water company or build a pipeline. What actually happened is that tectonic plates deep in Bolivian society shifted, leaving traditional parties bereft of support – and so they duly collapsed.

**Cleavage shifts**

To understand this, we need [cleavage theory](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Cleavage_Structures_Party_Systems_and_Vo.html?id=kjVRNgAACAAJ&redir_esc=y), a set of ideas from political sociology first proposed by Martin Lipset and Stein Rokkan (1967) that propose that a country’s politics tend in the end to reflect the deep divide – the cleavage – that defines its society. In Western Europe, interactions between two overarching historical processes – national revolutions and the Industrial Revolution – produced social cleavages between centre vs. periphery, the state vs. the Church, urban vs. rural areas, and workers vs. capitalists. Across Western Europe, these cleavages gelled into Left-Right political party systems that combined cleavages in slightly different ways according to countries’ particular histories and cultures, but generally contrasted pro-worker leftist parties advocating for a larger state against pro-capital rightist parties advocating for a smaller state.

Many developing countries across the world have broadly similar systems, as indeed did Bolivia. But the stunning thing is that this particular organisation of politics *never* suited Bolivian society. Bolivia never had an industrial revolution, and even today has relatively little industrial capital. And although most Bolivians work hard, it never had a self-identifying working class (think ‘proletariat’). It’s true that miners were highly mobilised during the 1952–53 Revolution. But at their historic peak in 1960, unionised workers totalled only 4 percent of the population, and have declined significantly since then. Being ‘working class’ was not a primary part of most Bolivians’ conceptions of their own identity: indigeneity, for example, was far more important. A political system that opposed the interests of workers vs. capitalists was ill-suited to a country that had neither. What Bolivia did have was 300 years of Spanish colonialism that completely remade its society, economy and politics. The Spanish destroyed the Inca empire, remade the economy, and reorganised land rights. They brought their language and religion to Bolivia and inserted a new ethnic and cultural divide into the heart of society. Indeed, the very concept of ‘Bolivia’ as a discrete political entity is a Spanish invention.

The new party system that emerged since 2005 is strikingly different ideologically and structurally to the system that collapsed. It is anchored, at one end of the axis, by the MAS, whose roots lie in indigenist village movements that rejected the previous system and claim legitimacy from the authenticity of their indigenous rural symbols, traditions and language. The other end of the axis is occupied by a changing set of parties and movements rooted in urban areas, the modern economy, and a far more ethnically diverse immigrant population. Instead of left vs. right, we can think of this as an identitarian axis of competition that opposes ethnic/rural vs. cosmopolitan/urban, along which Bolivia’s new parties and movements align. This axis matches much better the underlying cleavage that defines Bolivian society.

Cleavage theory predicts that parties will make spending promises and stake out positions across very different issues (defense, immigration, education, gender, etc.) that fit together reasonably well, and hence are self-reinforcing. Parties’ programmes will thus feature what political scientists call ‘issue coherence’. This, plus the difficulty of changing political organizations, explains why political party-system change can feature long periods of stasis followed by sudden, unexpected lurches amongst punctuated equilibria. The mechanism of change is illustrated below. It involves political entrepreneurs convincing voters that the old politics – e.g. left-right politics (red-blue circle) – is no longer relevant for them, and that a different organisation of issues, platforms and parties (green-brown circle) better fits the challenges and opportunities they face. An illustration of such a story might be: *All of us, workers and capitalists, are cooking the planet. Continuing to fight over left vs. right will lead to environmental catastrophe. We must reorganise our politics to focus on the existential crisis that faces us: the environment, which cuts across both workers and capitalists, and demands that we organise the energies and resources of us all differently.*

**The Future of the West**

What does all of this mean for the West? Simply put, the political parties of the West will disintegrate from the bottom up as their roots in civil society shrivel. The cleavage will shift and new parties will rise up – are already rising up – to take their place. Which cleavages are activated in this process will determine what sorts of politics Western countries have, and what sorts of nations they become.

Consider, first, the political changes we see before us. All over the West, long-established parties with enviable traditions of organisation and achievement, like Germany’s Social Democrats, Holland’s Labour, France’s Socialists, and Christian Democratic parties in Germany, Ireland, and Holland, are in steep decline. Elsewhere, party names remain, but their ideological and policy content are unrecognizable. Trump’s isolationist, protectionist, anti-science, anti-institutionalist Republicans would have been anathema to Republican voters of 1950, 1970 or 2000, for example. These developments have taken most analysts by surprise, myself included. But perhaps they should not have.

\_\_\_

Traditional Western parties were arrayed along a left-right axis that is increasingly irrelevant to almost everyone. Changes in the workplace, allied to deeper changes of consciousness in society, mean that there are fewer manual workers than in, say, 1950. More powerfully, people who work – the large majority – no longer think of themselves as ‘workers’ with a common culture and common interests. We are all free agents now. We no longer labour at assembly lines wearing similar clothes and doing similar tasks. Doctors, lawyers, office workers, and Uber drivers alike conceive of themselves as entrepreneurs with outside options, who might at any moment strike out and grab a better deal for themselves. Leftist parties that speak for workers and the union movement will not survive such changes. And without that left, there cannot be the traditional right either: it’s pointless defending the interests of business when everyone is a businessman, so the right has to morph too.

This is why the term ‘far-right’ is wrong, when applied to the likes of Trump, the AfD and Marine Le Pen. They are not further along the same old axis; they are on a different axis altogether. We should refer instead to the new parties of the left and right. The new left is environmental and identitarian, focused on climate change and the rights of ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+, and others. The new right is also identitarian, focused on nationalism and defending the rights of the West’s ‘traditional majorities’, typically Europeans and their descendants in North America, Australia and New Zealand, and to a lesser extent against environmental action. The old axis has disintegrated from below as voters increasingly considered it irrelevant, and the new axis now taking its place divides people by their identity and attitudes towards the environment.

This poses a huge problem for our societies – but also, potentially, an opportunity. The old politics of left-right tended to be positive-sum in the sense that it was possible to reach agreements between labour and capital that expanded the pie for everyone. Left-right conflicts could quickly become an amenable task of dividing up the spoils of growth. Identitarian contests, by contrast, are categorical by definition, and so tend strongly to be zero-sum. There is no equivalent to workers and capitalists working together to grow the economy. You are either a member of a given group, or you are not. If the other group is in power, you are out of luck. Very little averaging or fudging are available when groups are constructed to be exclusive.

The environment, by contrast, offers many opportunities to compromise for mutual gain. This dimension of competition is innately positive-sum, and offers us some hope that the new politics can become cooperative and constructive. It gives me cause for hope in the new generation. But the ‘feel’ of the moment, as we watch the old politics disintegrate and a new system rise, is far darker. Saving the planet is being shouted down by nativist calls to tribal loyalty laced with violence.

This implies that our politics are fated to become nastier and more vitriolic, based on winner-takes-all rather than the search for consensus amongst groups conditioned to negotiate. The Brexit referendum and the last three US presidential campaigns are all examples. Rising politicians, often from the ‘new right’, work assiduously, through sharp, polarizing language, to elevate one of the many characteristics of any society into the central divide around which political competition revolves. The figure below illustrates this process. Any society (the circle) is made up of individuals with many different cultural, economic, and political characteristics. It is possible to divide up such an electorate in many different ways. The dominant cleavage (the thick black line) imposes itself when politicians convince people that *this* division is most important, and people vote accordingly.

![circle of political division2](assets/Uploads/circle-of-political-division2.jpg)

Transformative politicians succeed not by out-competing rivals in the old issue space, but rather by convincing large numbers of voters that a new issue space matters more, and hence that their rivals are irrelevant. When those new issues centre on identity, simple descriptive facts about people harden into a social cleavage that divides us from one another. This would be an extraordinary reversal in the West, the home of the Enlightenment. Remember that not so long ago in the UK, Catholics and Jews were British Others, barred from voting, sitting in Parliament, or attending university. Now both are mainstream Tories, Labourites, establishment stalwarts, and Brits. The mantle of otherness has moved on. That it could is a tribute to a politics that did not ossify such differences – that worked to make them inessential. The demise of that politics, and the rise of identity clashes, threaten to alienate us from each other even as they remove the means for finding agreement. It is a sad and dangerous turn for the West that may forever change who we are.
