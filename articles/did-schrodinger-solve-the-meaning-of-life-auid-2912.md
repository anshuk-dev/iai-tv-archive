# Did Schrödinger solve the mystery of life?

*We can’t find life without knowing what it is*

**By Samuel McKee** | *7th August 2024* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,264 words*

![Did Schrödinger solve the mystery of life?](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/what-is-life.webp)

*From Aristotle to Darwin and Schrödinger to Marie Curie, understanding life has been a scientific and philosophical goal since humans were first able to conceptualise their subjectivity. Sam McKee argues that there is no point in searching for life in other worlds when we do not know what it is on our own planet. Many a debate today centres around a dispute over the definition of life, whether that be abortion politics, assisted suicide or evolutionary biology. McKee argues that we must focus on the definitional question of life before we look for it elsewhere, and to do so we must ask new questions.*

I sat recently with a Mars rover engineer, who was enthusiastically sharing with me the search for life’s signature on the surface of the red planet. As a board member of the Mars Society UK, these engineers and astrobiologists are our eyes and ears. On the drive home, it struck me how difficult we seem to find it today to define life: whether that be in evolutionary biology in a Linnaean manner, in the politics of an abortion debate, or in philosophy in the ongoing disputes over whether we possess free will or moral agency. The new humanist calls for assisted suicide show our struggle over those that are functionally living, yet not maintaining a quality of life as they had previously been. Life is both a present reality and something of a spectrum that is hard to pin down.

Yet eighty years ago, Schrödinger launched the question with such clarity and confidence that it arguably inspired the molecular biology revolution of the 1950s. [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick) and [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watson), Nobel Laureates who solved the structure of the DNA double helix in 1953, both credited Schrödinger’s 1944 work *What is Life?* as having inspired them to tackle the problem. This work was bold and ambitious, providing a sense of enthusiasm that the key to the problem was only a short distance away. Linus Pauling, who had earlier won his own Nobel prize in chemistry for elucidating the nature of chemical bonds, then remarkably predicting (and then discovering) alpha helices, also entered the race for the DNA structure with his typical exuberance. By hypothesising the question of life, Schrödinger may have contributed more than anyone else to the genesis of molecular biology. Following the Second World War, the exodus of physicists into biology (like Crick) hastened this effect.

Schrödinger’s work is still pertinent today, theorizing over many philosophical quandaries with how life works, and the seeming contradictions between the physical mechanisms of life and thermodynamics.

But what is the nature of life, and why does the question matter? The question remains in the air, but what progress have we made from his ideas?

Perhaps the answer lies in utility. Darwin’s [*Origin* of *Species*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species) increased the hunger and speculation for the discovery of the nature of heredity. Shortly afterwards Mendel launched genetics as a discipline. The parallels with Schrödinger here are obvious. So is the race to discover the nature of life valuable for its usefulness in discovering adjacent mechanisms, the questions within its orbit?

One of my favourite modern science books is [*What is life? How chemistry becomes biology*](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/what-is-life-9780198784791) by Addy Pross. In many ways, it ventures beyond Schrödinger’s speculation. If we can find the Carmen line (the imaginary line in the upper atmosphere where Earth ends and space begins) of biology – the point where once crossed one is no longer in one world but outside it, then that might be the greatest discovery in science thus far. Philosophers of science have been driven mad by the question too. It has been as elusive as its neighbour – defining a species. This may end up becoming one of those “I know it when I see it” problems, where intuition and rationality do the heavy lifting that language alone cannot accomplish. Life is obvious to the eyes, but now our new ventures into artificial intelligence and machine learning are blurring the question even further. What role does sentience play? How do we distinguish lower life from higher-order life? Slime mould can solve a maze in an optimum manner, over other higher-order organisms, but what does this really *mean?* A snowball is capable of growth and reproduction, yet no one would mistake it for a living entity. It was fascinating being a biology postgraduate during the pandemic and seeing the debate over whether viruses are alive reemerge.

How can we rephrase the question in a fashion that inspires new researchers or opens up new avenues of investigation? What interdisciplinary venture of philosophy and science will push the buttons of the next Crick and Watson who are waiting in the wings?

I propose that the question needs revisiting, for both clarity and utility. We need to specify the question of life in a way that leads to the next rung of meaningful progress, the clarity of which takes us to the next smaller puzzle to solve. This may lie in neuroscience, in evolutionary biology, in genetics, or perhaps somewhere more surprising. We also need to clarify life’s version of the Carmen line. The answer here may lie in the origin of life research, or in the more adventurous world of synthetic biology.

I imagine Schrödinger disappointed from afar at our inability to have solved the puzzle he so well-articulated eighty years ago. Francis Crick likewise thought we were as close as ever to solving the problem of the nature of the mind by the time of his death. Schrödinger was not asking a new question, but he was able to articulate it in a manner that was consciousness-raising. It was a popular book that pushed academic researchers. How can we rephrase the question in a fashion that inspires new researchers or opens up new avenues of investigation? Can we rearticulate the question of what life is in a meaningful way that progresses science, assists in developing new technology, and helps expand our current possibilities? Or does the nature of life remain in the realm of philosophy, unmoved since the ancient Greeks? I do not like to believe that this is the case, yet much of the above belongs to the humanities over the sciences.

Perhaps I am wrong. The answer could well lie inside the rocks of the red planet, waiting to be discovered by NASA’s Perseverance Rover. Astrobiology and astrochemistry are relatively new disciplines, full of the promise and excitement that an emerging new field comes with. The new tools of James Webb, Chandra and ESA’s JUICE have enabled previously unimagined capabilities for exploring the universe in search of life’s signature, allowing us to peer back in time at how the universe unfolded into a place we could call home.

We know that life is possible, we have the recipe and the ingredients. Can we reexamine and perhaps rewrite the question in order to trigger a renaissance on the life question?
