# Moving past morality

*The moral moment and what comes next*

**By Joel Marks** | *10th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,895 words*

![Moving past morality](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/24.09.10-Moral-Moment-psd-copy.webp)

---

*We assume that our picture of how the world should be reflects not only our attitudes and desires, but also some transcendent morality. Thus, we feel it is not enough to say that we are upset by how a friend has behaved – we must say that the friend has behaved badly, in some objective sense. In this piece, Joel Marks proposes that this impulse, though natural, is mistaken. There is no objective morality, Marks argues, and we would avoid considerable strife and conflict if we did not mistake our desires and preferences for a value-laden reality.*

There is an attitude one finds everywhere, in oneself as well as in others, that I would like to see vanish from the face of the Earth. It is best captured in what I call the moral moment, a kind of snapshot view of the attitude I am talking about. Suppose, for example, that you wanted something very much. It does not matter what. It could be something purely selfish. It could be something quite selfless. Somewhere in between is an instance from my own life, when I was introducing a friend to a large concert hall I had been to many times for classical music. I knew for an incontrovertible fact that the best listening is from the very top row of the top balcony. The sound rises from the stage far below and is directed by the curve of the vast ceiling right back and down to that very spot. There is simply no comparison in quality and volume to the sound anywhere else in the auditorium. Whenever I go to this venue I find a handful of other cognoscenti seated in that row, eager in our anticipation and taking a certain satisfaction mixed with pity for those who follow conventional wisdom and pay top price for a seat in the orchestra or first balcony.

On this occasion she was highly sceptical of my claims for the superiority of seats so far from the stage. So when we entered the balcony, having trudged up several flights of stairs, she refused to sit in the top row. Instead she descended a great distance to the very front row of this sparsely filled peanut gallery so that at least she could get a slightly closer view of the symphony orchestra. I myself accept the tradeoff of a diminished view of the musicians (albeit a magnificent view of the whole hall) for a sublimely enhanced experience of the sound. Thus to my mind my friend had chosen the worst of both worlds, since she would still have a poor view of the musicians but now as well an inadequate auditory experience.

I was frustrated beyond measure. Why? Let me count the ways. I now faced the awful choice of forgoing a musical experience I had been so looking forward to (Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring on a grand scale) or else sitting alone instead of with my friend. Furthermore, whichever way I chose I would be missing out on the experience of sharing the exceptional, almost magical effect on the sound quality I had been hoping for so many years to share with my friend. I was also pained on her behalf, because she was going to miss out on something I knew she would have tremendously enjoyed. So I could not help but see her as displaying a teenage mentality: wilfully resisting what was for her own good. To top it off, she would never know that she had missed out; so she would likely harbour some lingering resentment toward me for having dragged her up to the top balcony for nothing and deprived her of the experience she preferred.

So what do I mean by a moral moment? It is the attitude in which I myself was immersed when having all these thoughts and feelings. I can give you my analysis in a nutshell: I was objectifying things that were in fact only subjective. For along with my frustration at my friend there was a host of judgments: She is selfish for being utterly indifferent to my preferences. She is egotistical for putting her intuitive judgment of the acoustics ahead of mine based on actual experience. She is foolish for being so stubborn and refusing to try something novel. The ethical upshot: What she did was wrong, and she herself has some serious character flaws.

But the attitude that generated (or resulted from or simply was) these judgments is itself flawed, I maintain. Or better put: The attitude is inimical to the sort of world I would like to live in when I consider the matter reflectively. For even to call the attitude “flawed” is itself to exhibit the same attitude, this time directed at myself. There are no objective values that transcend our considered beliefs and desires. That, at any rate, is my main thesis. I call it amoralism, and sometimes even letheism (from “Lethe,” the river of forgetfulness in Greek mythology), a term of art in philosophy for the view that there is no such thing as truth in an absolute sense. Thus, even my claim that there are no objective values must be understood as not intended to be an absolute (i.e., objectively true) assertion. It is simply my considered view for which I can give reasons.

\_\_\_

The belief or assumption that there is objective truth, and in particular about values, has its root cause, I believe, in fear and desire.

\_\_\_

The belief or assumption that there is objective truth, and in particular about values, has its root cause, I believe, in fear and desire. In the soul of every individual is the need to believe and feel that everything is OK, or at least that everything will turn out OK in the end. But life in this world hardly provides evidence for that wishful surmise. In this way we become liable to massive delusion. The delusion is precisely the belief that things ought to be a certain way. This is indeed the primal moral moment: The moment when Adam and Eve ate the fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. (Thus I think a better name would be “tree of the illusion of right and wrong.”)

\_\_\_

What is considered right or wrong can vary from one extreme to the other, depending on who is making the judgment. And since these judgments are the face of strong desires, they are likely to be held on to with intensity.

\_\_\_

And how ought things to be? Why, of course, just the way we want them to be. This explains why what is considered right or wrong can vary from one extreme to the other, depending on who is making the judgment. And since these judgments are the face of strong desires, they are likely to be held on to with intensity. But because they disguise their source and appear to be judgments of fact rather than feelings of preference, their intensity is even more unyielding that that of the desires that give rise to them. In particular, they are immune to rational critique because the judgments are merely facades and not what is really at issue. (Philosopher Eric Campbell gives an exquisite account of this.) The result is sclerosis of views and irresolvability of conflicts. That is why I consider moral moments to be counterproductive and I advocate a general amoralism.

Imagine what a world of amoralists would be like. (Cue John Lennon if you will.) There would be no right or wrong behaviour, no good or bad people, no just deserts, no retribution, no outrage or perhaps anger of any kind, no contempt, disdain, no hatred, no praise or blame, no guilt …. What would there be instead? Simply desires (and aversions) and likes (and dislikes). I myself would also urge rationality as part of the mix, but in a sense unconnected to absolute truth; thus what I mean is simply that I would like everyone to be prepared to consider reasons for and against what they desire before acting on it, and to expect the same of others. This implies that rational people could have opposed rational desires. So differences of desire would not disappear from an amoral world of the kind I envision. All that would be gone are beliefs that one’s desires have the imprimatur of absolute morality (or that one’s beliefs have the imprimatur of absolute truth). No more double counting, as philosopher Ronnie de Sousa would put it.

\_\_\_

Besides rationality I would urge the cultivation of compassion.

\_\_\_

Even though amoralism is based on eliminating something, it is not easy in practice to give up making judgments. Since the moral attitude is clearly innate, it has presumably served a crucial function in our survival as a species. My view is that human circumstances have changed to the point where moralism has become less useful or even downright lethal. However, as I have also suggested, I do not advocate leaving a vacuum in its place. Besides rationality (of the sort I have characterised) I would urge the cultivation of compassion, and many other traits besides. But other folks undoubtedly value different things from what I value, so the world is not going to stop turning even if it goes amoral. I would only expect it to turn more smoothly than at present.

I chose to sit apart from my friend. My experience of the concert was ambivalent in the extreme. The performance was indeed the musical experience of a lifetime; but as my aesthetic bliss increased, so too did my sadness at not being able to share it in its full force and my disgust at my friend for denying both of us this opportunity for absurd reasons. Fortunately, my amoralism chipped in when my friend and I reunited afterward. However much she may or may not have borne her own moralist grudge against me, I was able to divorce my disappointments from the judgments they had engendered and simply resume friendly relations for the rest of the evening, since this is what I rationally desired and realised was the rational way to achieve it. In due time I could appreciate that my friend may have had reasons for her behaviour that were as rational as mine, and even if not, if she had been a wilful teenager, I had been a little boy who was inconsolable about not getting what he wanted.

An ordinary example, to be sure, but the happiness or misery of everyday life and human relationships is often constituted by a thousand small things; and meanwhile, analogous to Newton and his apple falling on the same basis as the planets in their orbits, I sense that couples bicker on the same basis as nations go to war. So my ambitions for amoralism are high. My general recommendation, therefore, it to put aside judgments. Focus on what you really want after due consideration and then figure out an effective way to attain it consistently with your other desires. Now practise practise practise. Over time I predict you will experience more peace and satisfaction and be more effective. But I’m not promising you a rose garden!
