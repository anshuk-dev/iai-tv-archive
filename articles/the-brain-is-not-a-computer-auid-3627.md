# The brain is not a computer

*Thought is emotional, ancient, and not all in the neocortex*

**By Dorothea Olkowski** | *21st July 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,817 words*

![The brain is not a computer](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-brain-is-not-a-computer-2.webp)

---

*Artificial intelligence is advancing on the assumption that the brain is essentially a computer and if that is true, replicating the mind in silicon is only a matter of time. Philosopher **Dorothea Olkowski** argues this is one of the biggest mistakes in the history of science. Consciousness does not originate in the cortex, the seat of reasoning and language, but in the brainstem, the most ancient part of the brain, shaped by body chemistry and emotion. The brain's signalling system is too chemically complex, too slow-acting, and too entangled with the whole body to be reduced to the simple yes/no binary logic that underlies all computation.*

The dominant assumption behind artificial intelligence is that the brain is essentially a computer—that thought is computation, and that consciousness, given sufficient processing power, can be replicated in silicon. The neuroscience tells a very different story. What the most important recent research reveals is that consciousness is not primarily a cortical function at all, let alone an algorithmic one: it arises from the oldest, most primitive part of the brain, is saturated with body chemistry, and is constitutively shaped by emotion.

The science I wish to focus on is neuroscience, and the first neuroscientist I would like to bring to your attention is the Estonian-American neuroscientist and psychobiologist Jaak Panksepp, whose work came to light for me when I started doing research for a course on cognition and emotion. Panksepp’s family fled Estonia for the US when he was still a child. He completed his graduate studies at the University of Pittsburgh and the University of Massachusetts. His distinguished career began as Head of Affective Neuroscience Research at the Chicago Institute for Neurosurgery and Neuroresearch and the Falk Center for Molecular Therapeutics, along with two simultaneous university positions. Given this remarkable career, one wants to investigate what has prevented Panksepp’s work from being more widely known.

[Panksepp was a pioneer in the research of affective neuroscience](https://www.nature.com/articles/npp2017168)—the study of the biological bases of emotion. Insofar as the field initially relied on the study of animals to understand humans, it was either ignored or discounted by a majority of researchers in the field. Beyond this, the study of human emotions was also not much respected—this has been the case for my field, philosophy, as much as for neuroscience. Nevertheless, Panksepp’s associates and students recognize him for creating “a grand unifying, neuroevolutionary theory about the brain substrates of the ancient mind that all mammals still share,” a theory which they compare to the “paradigm-shifting discoveries of more famous twentieth-century scientists”—notably Albert Einstein and Richard Feynman.

Panksepp is currently widely recognized for providing significant evidence that optimal brain development requires healthy play experiences, which he characterizes as the most complex and recent of positive emotions. With this research in hand, Panksepp fled the cudgel of behaviorism—the supposition that all behavior is merely an effect of external forces on an otherwise blank-slate mind—to study so-called “primary process mechanisms”: seeking, rage, fear, lust, care, panic, and grief. What makes his work so radical is his assertion that these primary processes, including play, do not originate in the neocortex—the most recently evolved region of the brain, responsible for reasoning and language—but rather, they arise from the most ancient one, the brainstem.

The existence of primary processes—a concept developed by Sigmund Freud and based on the study of dreams—refers generally to affective content. Given the general dismissal of all things Freudian in the modern era, even [Freud’s most useful ideas](https://iai.tv/video/the-unconscious-mind) were and still are often dismissed. Nevertheless, Panksepp puts forward several foundational propositions for a science of primary processes: that emotional feelings reflect the neurodynamics of brain systems generating instinctual behaviors; that primary process affective consciousness is fundamentally an unconditional gift of nature, not an acquired skill; that it can be studied in animals because it is an intrinsic function of the brain; and that consciousness is best approached through the instinctual emotional action tendencies of animals—seeking, fear, rage, lust, care, panic, play, and grief. These principles carry with them the further claim that primary process consciousness reflects raw subcortical sensory and perceptual feelings. By contrast, the ability to have thoughts about experiences is a secondary level of consciousness arising largely in the upper limbic system, and only the third level—reflection, or thoughts about thoughts—is a function of the neocortex.

What this means, in short, is that our richest and most fundamental experiences of being alive are not generated by our most sophisticated brain structures. They arise from the oldest. Panksepp argued that core emotional feelings and sensory affects arise outside of the neocortex and do not require higher cognitive functions. How this happens and what this means for the study of complexity was taken up by other neuroscientists such as Mark Solms, who dared to investigate not merely dreams but the experience of dreams—the so-called subjective element. One crucial discovery—supported by the research of additional neuroscientists including Panksepp—is that the circuit of the brain that drives dreaming as well as seeking is in the brainstem. Solms bluntly states: “I don’t believe that the cortical theory of consciousness is valid… animals and human beings can be conscious even if they lack a cortex entirely.” It is endogenous affect—feeling arising from within the body itself rather than from external stimulation—that enables us to feel our way through new situations and so turns into conscious awareness of the world.

Solms augments this claim by referring again to Panksepp, who argues that sensory affects such as pain, disgust, and surprise are simultaneously internal feelings and external perceptions, arising from different parts of the brain. Clearly pain and disgust feel differently, and surprise does not feel the same as fear. In addition to these feelings, perception contextualizes the experience. Perceptual consciousness arises when the cortex is aroused to provide detailed representations or images that guide action with the aid of unconscious subcortical responses that are automatized memories—such as that of how to ride a bike—so that “we do not discover the world afresh with each new cycle.” Crucially, what appears to consciousness is not raw sensory signals transmitted from the periphery, but rather predictive inferences derived from memory traces of such signals and their consequences.

Nevertheless, when it comes to the question of consciousness, even if the cortex is obliterated, consciousness persists and affects pervade consciousness at all times. This is why brain scanning during states of grief, seeking, rage, and fear—all of which are states of consciousness—shows that the highest metabolic activity occurs in the core brainstem and other subcortical regions such as the midbrain, while the cortex shows deactivation.

These findings have a direct bearing on how the brain processes information—and on whether that processing resembles anything like computation. Solms refers to fully responsive wakefulness as arousal. The cortex, in isolation and disconnected from the reticular activating system—a net-like formation of nerve cells lying deep within the brainstem, between the brain and the spinal cord, which regulates wakefulness and attention—displays erratic, high-amplitude waves not associated with consciousness. It is only when connected to the net-like core of the brainstem that the cortex displays the low-amplitude, high-frequency waves most associated with conscious awareness. Neuroimaging confirms that the cortex becomes conscious only to the extent that it is aroused by the brainstem. Solms asserts that “these facts are not controversial.”

What is controversial—and what matters enormously for the question of artificial intelligence—is the nature of the signalling involved. When neurons transmit messages using molecules called neurotransmitters, in addition to targeted binary (yes/no) neurotransmission—often associated with digital computation—there is a second type of transmission called post-synaptic modulation. It is described as a messy, chemical process arising from the brainstem and other subcortical structures, and even from non-neurological bodily structures. These neuromodulators—chemical messengers that regulate the overall state of the brain—come from all over the body, including the pituitary, adrenal, thyroid, and sex glands, as well as the hypothalamus and especially the brainstem. They wash over the network and regulate the overall state of the cortex, processing information differently depending on the emotional state involved. This process does not resemble what takes place in a computer—even though it is probabilistic and adjusts to the current situation—because it also has slow-acting and long-lasting effects as chemicals linger and habitual states are established. Furthermore, language itself, Solms argues, expresses thoughts we already have as well as new thoughts, and has been shown to boost image perception as well as whole semantic categories, especially abstractions. In this account, we have not even begun to address the complex of subjective and intentional systems intrinsic to my own research that contribute to consciousness insofar as they have existential and qualitative value. None of this is binary. None of it is algorithmic.

**The Contradiction at the Heart of AI**

Unfortunately, and in spite of his decades of neurobiological research establishing all of this, Solms reverts to a reductionist position—in part, I suspect, because his much-admired predecessor Sigmund Freud sought to reduce all psychological phenomena to a purely physical causality. Solms is enthusiastic about this aspect of Freud but situates his reductive hopes less in physical forces than in electronic ones. He argues that gradually replacing natural neurons with artificial ones will not, in the end, make any difference to the nature of intelligence. He also believes that silicon brains will be capable of experience like that of sentient beings. He bases this on the success of artificial interfaces between the brain and spinal cord that allow paralyzed animals, including humans, to move. The reduction of psychophysiological function to formal statistical mechanics is a means for him to the production of consciousness in a so-called embodied robot, which he speculates could be created by reverse engineering the functional organization of living systems, where consciousness is equated simply with the feeling of uncertainty. Solms cites the Turing test as crucial to making the judgment that the robot has consciousness, because he believes that it circumvents prejudice. This is a striking reversal: having demonstrated that consciousness is a deeply embodied, chemically mediated, evolutionarily ancient phenomenon, Solms then proposes that it can be reproduced in silicon. The contradiction deserves scrutiny.

Some AI experts want the Turing test to be abandoned because, for them, it is [no longer relevant](https://iai.tv/articles/the-turing-tests-of-today-are-mistaken-auid-2790). Others, such as Eric J. Larson, a philosopher who has spent most of his career as an artificial intelligence research scientist studying natural language processing, argue that the Turing test is so difficult that no computer has ever passed it. On the simplest level, it demands that [machines using natural language must be intelligent.](https://iai.tv/articles/llms-show-language-does-not-describe-reality-auid-3578) The test is hard because using natural language as humans generally do requires lots of common-sense inferences which are not within either the logically certain or the probabilistic abilities of AI. Evidence for this has been provided by researchers who ask questions using sentences that combine words seldom if ever found together in web searches—like “armadillo” and “transportation.” Additional types of questions require contextual knowledge of words with several possible meanings, as in the case of the word “pen,” which can refer to either an enclosure or a writing implement. A sentence may be perfectly grammatical but still require real-world knowledge to either understand or to dismiss as nonsensical. Noam Chomsky’s famous grammatically correct sentence—“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”—comes to mind. Any child can tell us that it makes no sense even though no one can fault the grammar. What a child brings to language that no algorithm does is a body, a history, and a brainstem full of ancient affect.

**The Brain as a Complex System**

This decision, to count on the Turing test, returns us to the brain and body as a complex system not reducible to numbers or algorithms, and calls for a proper understanding of the relation between chaos and complexity. As I [argued in my previous article](https://iai.tv/articles/life-defies-the-second-law-of-thermodynamics-auid-3611), chaos describes a solution whose outcome is very sensitive to initial conditions—small changes lead to great differences in outcome—and whose evolution appears quite random despite being governed by deterministic rules. And as the [evolutionary biologist Stuart Kauffman](https://iai.tv/video/the-blueprint-for-life) argues, “the reason complex systems exist on, or in the ordered regime near the edge of chaos is because evolution takes them there.” Natural selection tunes their parameters until they arrive at the region between order and chaos where complex behavior lives. If life arose as collectively autocatalytic sets, then the first life was already exploding and struggling to control the explosion. This “supercriticality” continues until limited by factors such as food supply and energy, but also by the fact that we metabolize our food rather than simply merge with it. Natural selection has always sifted for useful variations, but what makes order possible in a far-from-equilibrium universe is that life itself—self-organization—is a natural expression of tendencies toward order. Living things may mutate too slowly and remain trapped at a certain optimum, or they may mutate too fast and fail. Hence, self-organization is the essential pre-condition of evolution—and it is precisely what no algorithm can replicate.

The neuroscientist Walter J. Freeman, who carried out research for many years at UC Berkeley, brings these threads together. Freeman concludes, based on thirty years of research, that perception cannot be understood as the activity of individual neurons—the common approach in neuroscience. Rather, perception requires “the simultaneous, cooperative activity of millions of neurons spread throughout expanses of the cortex.” Freeman states that to grasp choral music, for example, we must hear the performers modulate together in response to one another. This, he argues, is all part of the chaos-complex behavior of vast collections of neurons that shift abruptly and simultaneously from one complex activity pattern to another in response to the smallest inputs. For Freeman, the chaotic behavior of collections of neurons is what makes it possible to respond to perception at all, to generate novel activity, and—significantly—to experience fresh ideas. This is not computation. It is complexity.

One significant problem is that due to the focus on big data by AI researchers, as late as 2013 there was no unified conceptual framework for addressing questions of complexity. It has been argued that “big data without a big theory to go with it loses potency and usefulness.” Despite the work of many eminent theorists from different disciplines who created and emerged from the Santa Fe Institute’s Complexity Studies, it seems as if AI has taken up all the air in the room. Yet researchers studying the brain as a complex system are doing precisely what AI cannot: attending to emergent properties born out of interconnectedness and unpredictability. Their work shifts the conventional interest of prediction and control in science toward understanding the conditions that complex systems are sensitive to and their observed dynamics. Complex systems are non-linear, with output that is not proportional to the input, and they evince circular rather than linear causality—making them deterministic but not predictable.

In 2011, groups of scientists from Wake Forest University and its Medical School proposed using “[network science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3621511/)” as a tool for studying brain connectivity by viewing it as a system with various interacting regions that produce complex behaviors. This requires a shift away from analyzing data that compares single variables to the model of complexity theory, which utilizes network scientific methods. The brain encodes physiological processes encompassing wide and intermingled ranges of spatiotemporal scales, so that a protein misfolding at the molecular level can lead to emotional imbalances at the behavioral level—the so-called butterfly effect. Thought, emotions, and memory phenomena span timescales ranging from microseconds to years, and the implication is that neurological phenomena have serious implications for social life.

Explaining behavior as a function of groups of factors constituting a dynamic whole reflects the dynamic nature of a complex system that cannot be understood by analyzing the system as comprised of independent elements. This reveals the limits of reductionism: a complex system cannot be understood by only studying its constituent parts, such as individual neurons. Researchers argue that knowledge about the complex interactions within a system is the only way to understand the behavior of the system overall via structural or functional connections and interactions.

The argument, then, is this: consciousness is not a product of the cortex alone, nor of computation, nor of any algorithm. It is a whole-body, evolutionarily ancient, chemically mediated, [self-organizing phenomenon](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-the-only-truly-creative-thing-in-the-universe-auid-3581)—one that complexity science is only beginning to adequately describe. The swirl of claims surrounding artificial intelligence depends on ignoring precisely this. For those of us who want to know more about the relation of the brainstem to consciousness—and the manner in which our consciousness, memory, perceptions, and emotions are not reducible to either the cortex alone or to algorithms—the science of complexity is and will continue to be of the greatest value.
