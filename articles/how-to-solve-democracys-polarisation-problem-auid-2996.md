# How to solve democracy's polarisation problem

*The flaw at the heart of democracy*

**By Alex Guerrero** | *12th November 2024* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,884 words*

![How to solve democracy's polarisation problem](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/elections-piece2.webp)

---

*Every election, regardless of the winner, ends with a nation divided. Old and young, men and women, rich and poor... elections divide us. But are elections really a necessary part of a democracy? Alexander Guerrero doesn't think so. In place of elections, we should appoint our political officials by lottery. While such a claim may on the surface appear unrealistic or outright dangerous, we should remember that the jury system, vital to the carrying out of the rule of law, works in a similar way. Government by lottery has a vast history, and with strict frameworks in place to play roles comparable to judges and the prosecution/defence in the system of law, Guerrero argues, it just might work. And at least, he argues, it is better than our current system, in which we approach a political, and existential, precipice every four years – the precipice we approached last week.*

Here in 2024 in America, half of us threaten the other half (and possibly the world) with disaster.  We do this every two and four years.  Preventing disaster is of great moral importance.  We all should do our utmost to accomplish that.  So, vote, and get others to vote.

But why is it so hard to do anything more?  Why is the positive political vision so meagre?  Why is our experience of political community so much like being trapped on a boat with a tiger?  Fear, even dread, danger at every turn, our attention and energy devoted to outmaneuvering a wild, nearly incomprehensible force that in turn ignores us or aims to do us harm.

You might be thinking: the looming disaster, the tiger on the boat, is just one guy, and he will eventually go away.  Unfortunately, that guy is more a symptom than a cause.  There will be, and are, many more like him.

The pathologies of American presidential politics are in part a result of the poor performance of our political institutions.  People on both sides are angry and dismayed at how inept our institutions are at addressing the serious problems we face: economic precarity and struggle in meeting our basic needs (housing, healthcare, food, clean water) even amidst astonishing wealth for the 1%, environmental disaster, crumbling infrastructure, global instability and war.  People lash out at false causes and turn to false prophets to save us.  When this doesn’t work, and it won’t, we turn elsewhere, anti-incumbency sentiment rules.  Change, change, change.  Drain the swamp.

Why must we live at the precipice of disaster?  Why are our political institutions so ineffective and unresponsive?

Surprisingly, the problem is the heart of democracy in its modern form: the election.  Electoral representative democracy is deeply, [possibly irreparably broken in the modern world](http://iai.tv/video/the-democracy-show), given the size and scale of modern political communities and the global nature of the problems we face.

We were supposed to be able to elect representatives who would act on our behalf and vote them out if they did otherwise, but the size, scale, and attendant complexity of modern politics makes it impossible for us to hold our representatives meaningfully accountable. Policy complexity, citizen ignorance, entertainment news, echo chambers, distrust and the erosion of shared understanding —among other problems—have rendered modern electoral democracies incapable of solving the urgent problems we face.  Elections create and intensify divisions where none need exist.  They focus our attention on the issues that divide us most and direct our effort to defeating and even dominating the other side, rather than on identifying and working together on issues where we have common cause.  We are broadly ignorant and intensely divided, and the most powerful among us take advantage of this to control who is elected, what policies are enacted, and which problems are ignored.

Elections reliably and predictably select an elite, non-representative group to rule over us.  Our candidates and elected representatives are much wealthier, much more likely to have attended elite schools (often simply because of their greater family wealth), much more likely to be lawyers or businesspeople, much less likely to have been homeless, unemployed, incarcerated, or to have raised children as a single parent.  This affects what they know and care about.  Donald Trump has five children.  I’m sure he doesn’t know how to change a diaper.

Elections create short-term incentives, causing politicians to [ignore or downplay catastrophic concerns relating to climate change.](http://iai.tv/video/the-tears-of-the-west)  Rather than thinking about the long term or even the medium term, elections encourage those in office and running for office to focus our attention on emotionally charged issues that will distract us without requiring substantive alteration to the bottom line of the wealthiest and most powerful.

Some of these concerns will be familiar.  The response might also be familiar: the Churchillian shrug (“the worst form of government except all those other forms that have been tried”). I encourage us to move past this shrug and introduce a new form of democracy: lottocracy.

Lottocracy incorporates many new elements, but the most striking is the shift from using elected representatives to using representatives selected through lottery.  This use of lottery selection in politics, also referred to as “sortition,” is not new.  It was a core feature of the original democracy in Ancient Athens and has been used in the modern context through [hundreds of citizens’ assemblies](https://participedia.net/) that have been created for various political uses around the world.  Lottocracy goes further, recommending moving away from elections entirely.

The basic elements of lottocracy are these:

(1) Single Issue: rather than a single generalist legislature (whether unicameral or bicameral), in a lottocratic system there are several standing, single-issue legislative bodies, with each legislative institution focusing on one policy area or sub-area (e.g., agriculture, immigration, health care, trade, education, energy, etc.)

(2) Lottery Selection: the members of each single-issue legislature are chosen by a pure (non-stratified) lottery from the relevant political jurisdiction.  People chosen will serve 3 years terms, with 450 people serving at any one time.  The terms would be staggered so that only 150 new people start each term.  Those selected would be paid a significant salary (commensurate with or higher than their current income) for their participation and afforded legal protections so that they could not be penalized from their employer or in other ways.  It would also be permissible to decline or defer participation.

(3) Learning Phases: the members of the single-issue legislatures hear from a variety of experts, advocates, and stakeholders on the relevant topic at the beginning of and at various stages throughout each decision-making session.

(4) Community Consultation: beyond the learning phases, the members of the single-issue legislature spend structured time talking to, interacting with, and hearing from members of the public, including activists and stakeholders affected by proposed action.

(5) Direct Enactment: the members of the single-issue legislature have the capacity either to directly enact policy or, in some cases, to do so jointly with other single-issue legislatures.

(6) Structural Assemblies: there will be a number of assemblies, consisting predominantly of randomly chosen citizens, who will have responsibility for monitoring and maintaining the basic organization and support of the network of single-issue legislatures, including setting rules of procedure and deliberation, resolving jurisdictional issues, considering topical merger proposals, allocating funds based on budget proposals, and regulating the process by which people come to speak in the learning phase.

I also suggest that the executive function should not be fulfilled by an elected executive but instead by a network of executive assemblies.

There are many questions about each component, and about how they work together.  I discuss these at length in [my book](https://www.lottocracy.org/), considering both the promise of lottocratic systems and potential concerns.  I focus, for example, on how single-issue legislatures would function and what would be done in cases of issue overlap, how we might respond to skew in who accepts or declines, how experts should be identified and selected, how deliberation should be structured to avoid a few dominant voices from having undue influence, and much else.  I also consider how rights of broad political participation, political equality, political power, considerations of accountability and legitimacy, and our basic ideas about democracy are illuminated once we move past the electoral representative framework.  I also argue that lottocratic systems would fare much better with respect to what I call political sensibility: the capacity of the political system to identify and understand the political problems in a community and to act effectively in response to those problems.

\_\_\_

The potential advantages are considerable.  Consider the problem of elite influence and capture of our elected representatives.  Random selection prevents corruption or undue influence in the selection of representatives.  Those chosen don’t need to raise funds for re-election, making it easy to police and limit financial influence of the most powerful.  Regular rotation makes it less valuable to buy off individual members compared to capturing a Senator who might serve for 30 or 40 years.

The learning phase, single-issue focus, and extended deliberation and discussion would result in more informed, detailed engagement with political issues, without the need to (somehow) reform the mass media system.  The single-issue focus would enable us to focus on problems and issues, rather than the personalities and pathologies of candidates.  Without elections, the intense short-term incentives would be eliminated, allowing people to think about their children’s generation and the generations and fate of the world to come.

Without regular elections pitting us against each other, politics might become more collaborative and respectful, as people interact over time, in person, and think about how to work together in careful and respectful deliberative settings.  Single-issue legislatures enable us to focus on issues where there might be broad agreement about problems and attractive solutions to those problems, rather than spending all our time on issues where we disagree.

Using lotteries means that those in political power will be a genuine microcosm of the political community—genuinely demographically representative—bringing the full range of concerns and problems into view.  Those selected will be deeply invested in addressing those problems, as they and those they love are affected by them—rather than perhaps being only interested in seeking and maintaining power over others.  People will also bring the full range of knowledge and experiences into the political process, with a proportional number of people in the room having firsthand knowledge of being an engineer, police officer, primary caretaker of children, recent immigrant, disabled person, scientist, and so on.

These many potential advantages combine, I argue, to give us hope of reclaiming genuine power for the people, and a more genuinely responsive and effective and—indeed—*democratic* political system. Even if you remain unconvinced, a significant hope for the idea is that your response will be *I don’t think that will work, but what about this*...

A democratic political community needs to see reasons for hope and optimism about our future living together.  It can’t survive with the feeling that disaster is always right around the corner.

We need to expand our imagination regarding political and legal institutions.  We need to think not just electorally, but institutionally.

*This work is based on Alex Guerrero’s new book Lottocracy published by Oxford University Press in 2024.* Lottocracy *makes the case that electoral representative democracy—although the best form of government that has been tried—runs into deep problems in the modern world.*
