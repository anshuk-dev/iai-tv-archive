# The "sovereign individual" is a dangerous fantasy

*Silicon Valley’s fatal fear of friction*

**By James I. Porter** | *26th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,121 words*

![The "sovereign individual" is a dangerous fantasy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/sovereign-individual.webp)

---

*From Peter Thiel to Donald Trump, the ideal of the “sovereign individual” who bends reality to their will is newly dominant. Technology will soon enable a “bizarre genius” to live free from all constraints, prophesizes Thiel’s favorite book. But this vision fatally misunderstands the nature of the self, argues **James I. Porter**, author of books on Nietzsche and Greek culture. Individuality is forged only by the friction of contesting with others, and subjective experiences become meaningful only in relation to the experiences of others. To remove this resistance would be to erase meaning itself; absolute self-sovereignty would be self-annihilation.*

"Nothing is self-sufficient, neither in us ourselves nor in things."

- Nietzsche, *The Will to Power*

“Sovereignty, the ideal of uncompromising self-sufficiency and mastership,” [wrote Hannah Arendt](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Human_Condition.html?id=ARBJAgAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y), “is contradictory to the very condition of plurality. No man can be sovereign because not one man, but men, inhabit the earth.” Arendt was taking on the tradition of liberal individualism, familiar from Kant, which posits that individuals are rational agents whose autonomy and inalienable freedom are the foundation of their realization as self-sufficient beings in a community of peers. Dissenting from this still widely held view, Arendt argues that “sovereignty is possible only in imagination.” The fantasy of sovereignty, be it over oneself or over others, is a compensatory mechanism—a defensive illusion—that papers over the intrinsic “frailty” of the human condition.

This condition of frailty has resonances with what the French philosopher and author Georges Bataille called “[the principle of insufficiency](https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=A2MQBAAAQBAJ&printsec=frontcover&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q&f=false).” Bataille suggests that inner experience can have no other value or authority beyond what it finds outside itself in a community of other beings. For this reason, inner experience, despite its name, is intrinsically an experience of otherness, extimacy, incompleteness, and self-insufficiency. Conversely, the principle of insufficiency is tied to a “principle of a community.” Each is the condition of the other.

Today, the critique of sovereign individualism is a given for thinkers as diverse as Friedrich Nietzsche, Maurice Blanchot, Theodor Adorno, Judith Butler, and Fred Moten, all of whom recognize that vulnerability, dependency, and interdependency are the conditions that make social existence possible and, all too often, impossible. We might call this a shared insufficiency. These traits, which may appear to be concessions, a give-away of agency, are in fact not disabling to human agency. They are its animating force. But they also set out the constitutive limits of the human condition.

If the critique of the fantasy of self-sovereignty is so widespread, why should we care? One reason is that the sovereign fantasy is alive and well. It is an intuition that backs everyday conceptions of what it is to be a self today. It serves as a motor of neoliberal ideology, according to which the needs, demands, and free choices of the individual consumer eclipse communitarian, social, and environmental responsibilities. And Silicon Valley’s ideologues [make it the centerpiece of their postliberal vision](https://theconversation.com/this-libertarian-manifesto-loved-by-peter-thiel-urges-a-cognitive-elite-to-see-selfishness-as-a-virtue-263742) for the future. [*The Sovereign Individual*](https://books.google.com.do/books?id=O4aOAAAAIAAJ&lr=), the bestseller endorsed by Peter Thiel, takes the speculative fiction and fantasy of sovereignty to new heights. It promises a purely digital fantasy of power that is unfettered by physical reality, guided by “bizarre genius,” subservient to no one, and enslaving all others through the lightly veiled threat of cyberterrorism. Trumpian autocracy would look like a quaint idea were this fantasy ever to materialize. But cyber-based sovereignty, too, admits of vulnerabilities, dependencies, economies, and communities (be they fellow sovereigns or “losers and left-behinds”), never mind the outer constraints of power grids and natural ecologies or the inner constraints of a megalomania made vassal to its own myths.

Plainly, contemporary critiques of the sovereign self are not idle interventions. This is where Bataille opens up a much-needed alternative. Whereas Arendt and other progressive thinkers for the most part restrict themselves to the realms of human communication and community, Bataille grounds his principle in a picture of a universe that supersedes the human world. His view also happens to have a genealogy that can be traced back to ancient Greek and Roman thought, as he knew full well, the most prominent example being Heraclitus, the early Greek natural philosopher active around 500 BCE. To see how this plays out, a closer look at Bataille’s principles of insufficiency and communication will be needed.

**The illusion of the isolated self**

Bataille’s theory of insufficiency is designed to upend what he calls “the illusion of sufficiency”—namely, “the illusion of a being that is isolated, folded back on itself and which possesses the power to exist without some sort of exchange.” By “a being,” Bataille understands not only human beings but any existing part of the universe. His reasoning follows a surprisingly materialist logic. His point, in a nutshell, is that everything that exists, every being, animate or not, belongs to (is a part of) some greater being (or composite whole), while every being is in turn composed of smaller individual parts or beings. Because of this mutual imbrication of all that is, no being is self-sufficient or autonomous, and none possesses a stable self-identity (“ipseity”). All beings are insufficient relative to all others, as is the world they collectively produce, for it too is as dynamic and relationally defined as its parts and their relations: it remains an “uncompleted” and “incompletable” world that never achieves a final summation (*somme* or *sommet*).

With examples drawn from the animate and inanimate world, Bataille depicts a network of beings that encompasses both human and nonhuman spheres, from organic sponges to the sky, earth, atoms, cells, ocean waves, animals, words of language, and social formations of various sizes (villages and empires). Across every instance of existence, every whole and [every part is unstable, uncertain, fleeting, and impermanent](https://iai.tv/video/heraclitus-dream). None is ever untouched by mediation, or, as Arendt would say, none is unconditioned. To be is “to be in relation.” Each being “communicates” with other beings who together compose an ever-changing environment or milieu.

Communicates but also contests. Beings cohabit, but not peacefully. For “the sufficiency of each being is unremittingly contested by every other [being],” that is, “by its neighbors.” My existence feels insufficient to me simply because you exist in my midst. I cannot be self-sufficient because your existence contests my being: it chafes at me and harasses me; it is inconvenient ([as Laura Berlant would say](https://www.dukeupress.edu/on-the-inconvenience-of-other-people)); it challenges my sense of propriety and autonomy, and this is true whether we are in combat or in love. And, though insufficiency suggests a certain felt lack of being, it does not arise from a lack in Being, but rather from a surplus of beings. Bataille’s thinking is not ontological (for “Being is nowhere”). It is [mereological](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mereology/) and relational, and it carries strong ethical implications.

Owing to the intensity and even competitiveness—the mutual contestation—of every instance of being, whatever exists is no more than an element within “the extreme complexity” and “labyrinthine constitution of being,” one that is an ensemble of warring constituent parts, or better yet, “particles”—hence the focus on the compositionality of beings that together produce unstable and irrational wholes. At most, ipseities and identities are fleeting things; they are our ways of expressing, badly, the vortices of reality. In short, the self-sufficient ipse, be it human or inhuman, is an illusion, no more than “a resting point in the vast flux of things.”

The language, like the thought, is at once Heraclitean and Nietzschean. Bataille phrases this flux in terms of “contagions of energy, of movement, of warmth, or transfers of elements, which, within you (*intérieurement*), constitute the life of your organic being.” But the crucial upshot is that “life is never situated at a particular point: it passes rapidly from one point to another (or from multiple points to other points), like a current or like a sort of streaming of electricity. Thus, there where you would like to grasp your timeless substance, you encounter only a slipping, only the poorly coordinated play of your perishable elements.”

Bataille is offering a multipronged critique of ipseity, of individualism, and of totalitarian politics (this is a wartime essay) that lays the foundation for a new kind of communication and sociality, that is, a new ethics and a new politics. But its foundation is a theory about [the world as a physical process](https://iai.tv/video/organisms-are-processes-not-things-with-wahida-khandker) that is endlessly in motion and that has no regard for individual entities. Bataille is sketching out a democracy of things that includes us as one of its minor constituents: “from one single particle to another, there is no difference in nature.” And yet particles, when combined (as they always are), are “undermined by [their] profound inner division,” their mutual porosity, and their ephemeral makeup. As mentioned, Nietzsche is one influence. Heraclitus, one of Bataille’s lesser-known obsessions, is another. Further connections among later ancient philosophies imprinted by Heraclitus could flesh out this picture. [Marcus Aurelius](https://global.oup.com/ukhe/product/meditations-9780199573202?cc=gb&lang=en&) could have written whole sentences from the last quotation (minus the appeal to electricity).

**Heraclitus: reality is flux**

Bataille’s understanding of reality “[at the scale of the universe](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262047333/the-limit-of-the-useful/)" sets out much of the gist of Heraclitus’ cosmology. Bataille is not deliberately annotating Heraclitus, but he is capturing the force of what he calls the “explosive vision” of the Presocratic’s cosmic world, “the world of rivers and flames,” of aggressive and unstable flux, and, most importantly, a world that knows no individuated identities. As the German classicist Bruno Snell wrote in 1926, with Heraclitus “the self appears for the first time as a problem,” and not as a newly minted entity that marks its own triumph.

One of the key moments in Heraclitus’ reframing of the relationship of humans to their surroundings appears in the following fragment: “Those who are awake have a world (kosmon) that is one and in common (*hena kai koinon*), but each of those who is asleep turns aside into his own private world (*eis idion*).” In sleep, our mind is cut off from what encompasses it (to periechon, the “environment,” with which the mind is “cognate”). In our daily lives, we behave like sleepwalkers, oblivious to the externalities that “dwell like a foreigner in our bodies.” The “private” self is a deprived self, whereas the waking, extroverted self is fully alive. Aliveness here is established by the self’s connectivity to nature, but also by its extroversion, the outward-facing posture of the self. Heraclitus’ word for “connectivity” is *ta koina*, the bits of reality that are “shared,” held “in common,” amongst all that exists. Bataille’s word for this kind of connection is tied to the same etymon: “communication.”

The communication that both thinkers have in mind is deeper than words, deeper than selves, deeper than the divide between the human and the nonhuman, and disruptive of privatized interiority. Faced with our surroundings, we become strangers to ourselves. We become what we are: tiny bits of a cosmic “biosphere” (so Bataille) that is never equal to itself and never sufficient by any measure.

The ethical implications are evident: a certain weakness (*impuissance*), humility, and passivity supplant the purported strength that inflates the fantasy of self-sovereignty. As for the political implications, these will depend on the moment we inhabit. Bataille was confronting fascism and a natural world whose resources were being depleted by extractive capitalism. As difficult as it may be to fathom, we seem to be facing a similar combination of adverse circumstances today. Now more than ever, we need to fortify our politics with a reinvigorated ethics. A problematized self, grounded in self-insufficiency, is a good place to start. That is how democratic bodies can find the collective will to assemble, confront, and resist the presumed autonomy of those momentarily in power.

But it is equally necessary to stand back from the fray, to take a bigger view of things, and to recalibrate what is at stake and who the players are. Would-be autocrats may be sovereign in their own minds (“My own morality. My own mind. It’s the only thing that can stop me”), but they are this only as individuals: that is what gives the lie to their fantasy. Viewed against the backdrop of an indifferent universe, they appear as what they actually are: tiny specks that flicker for a brief second and then disappear again as if “[nothing [had] happened](https://genealogiesofmodernity.org/journal/2021/8/16/retelling-the-human-story).” Here, the three levels of insufficiency canvassed above—the insufficiency of being beyond the human at the cosmic level and the shared insufficiency beyond the individual at the ethical and political levels—are interlocking and mutually informing. But none of these three kinds of awareness is sufficient without the other. Whence the renewed urgency of Heraclitus’ warning from two and a half millennia ago: it is time to wake up again.
