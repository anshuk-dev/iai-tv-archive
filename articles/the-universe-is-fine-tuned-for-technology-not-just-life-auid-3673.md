# The universe is fine-tuned for technology, not just life

*Cosmological evolution selects for black-hole-creating universes*

**By Jeff Shainline** | *7th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,126 words*

![The universe is fine-tuned for technology, not just life](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-universe-is-fine-tuned.webp)

---

*The universe seems to be fine-tuned for life. Were nature’s constants even slightly different, life wouldn’t exist. To explain this, scientists reach for bizarre ideas, like an infinite multiverse. But physicist and technologist **Jeff Shainline** thinks we’ve been missing a crucial clue. The universe isn’t just fine-tuned for life, but also for the creation of technology – humanity itself is just one step in this longer evolutionary process. He uses this observation to build a new case for an overlooked explanation of fine-tuning: Lee Smolin’s proposal that universes reproduce through black holes and, like organisms, evolve across cosmic generations towards fecundity. Life and technology, Shainline argues, are not the point of the universe, but parts of its reproductive strategy.*

A deep puzzle has loomed over physics since the 1950s. The world we observe—from sub-atomic quarks to lightyear-spanning galaxies—has the properties it does because a short list of numbers takes certain values. These numbers are often referred to as constants of nature (I will refer to them as parameters), and they include quantities like the speed of light, the masses of fundamental particles, and the strengths of the fundamental forces. The puzzle arises because these numbers have very special values that allow life to exist. This is called fine-tuning. As an example, life depends on carbon, and carbon is made in stars. But the specific process by which carbon is made in stars is extraordinary, relying on a very peculiar coincidence of properties involving electromagnetism, gravity, and the strong nuclear force that causes alpha particles to fuse into carbon nuclei. If the parameters that describe our universe were even slightly different, stars would not create carbon, and life as we know it would not be possible.

It therefore appears that the parameters of the universe are [fine-tuned](https://iai.tv/articles/fine-tuning-points-towards-a-cosmic-purpose-phillip-goff-auid-2600) to enable life to exist. There are several proposed resolutions to this puzzle. One is a statement of observer bias. According to this worldview, a vast number of universes with different parameters exist, and we find ourselves in this particular one simply because it is hospitable to our existence. This is referred to as the [weak anthropic principle](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-anthropic-cosmological-principle-9780192821478?cc=us&lang=en&). Many physicists accept this explanation, finding it favorable because it gives no special role to life or humans, while many others find it unsatisfactory because it assumes [a possibly infinite multiverse](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-the-multiverse) and only explains our residence in that infinitude by appealing to random chance. At the other end of the spectrum is the worldview that there is some property of nature that necessitates the eventual existence of intelligent observers. This is called the [strong anthropic principle](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-010-2220-0_25), and it asserts that our universe must eventually produce life. A third [explanation is theism](https://iai.tv/articles/fine-tuning-points-towards-a-cosmic-purpose-phillip-goff-auid-2600), which states that an omniscient, omnipotent deity selected these parameters during intentional creation, perhaps specifically for the purpose of allowing life to exist. Under some forms of this worldview, humans specifically are the reason the parameters take the values they do.

To many, none of these explanations is satisfactory, since they involve profoundly improbable chance, or lack clarity as to why intelligence is required, or invoke a deity controlling nature from outside. To me, they also miss an important piece of the fine-tuning picture: fine-tuning doesn’t appear to be limited to life; the parameters of the universe also appear to be tuned to support advanced technologies—a fact completely missing from the anthropic principle, and one that I think points to the resolution, as I’ll describe below.

In the early 1990s, [physicist Lee Smolin put forth another idea](https://iai.tv/articles/lee-smolin-the-laws-of-the-universe-change-auid-2174). He proposed that the physics of the universe is described by this particular set of parameters because it evolved to have these parameters. According to [Smolin’s formulation](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/9/1/016), our original, progenitor universe was a simple vacuum fluctuation, of which countless multitudes emerge and evanesce continually. But every so often, one occurs which, upon collapse, happens to make a copy of itself, resulting in reproduction, which is an essential ingredient in evolution. [Smolin argues](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-life-of-the-cosmos-9780195126648) the reproductive process occurs through spacetime singularities, commonly referred to as black holes. When this form of reproduction occurs, the offspring produced is not an exact copy of the region of spacetime that collapsed. Rather, it is a slightly modified copy with properties that differ minutely from its parent due to quantum effects occurring within the black hole. According to Smolin’s theory, many generations of universes have occurred since the progenitor vacuum fluctuation, and the generations have gradually evolved in complexity to produce ever more offspring, ultimately by creating very large numbers of stars, because large stars produce black holes at the end of their lives. The general process is called [cosmological natural selection](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2004.05.021) (CNS), and it provides a different resolution to the anthropic principle. Under CNS, the parameters of our universe evolved to maximize the production of offspring universes through black holes via star formation. This picture provides a causal explanation that does not appeal to the improbable chance of the weak anthropic principle, avoids the issues with an ultimate cause of the strong anthropic principle, and has no need to appeal to a deity.

There are problems with CNS as Smolin originally formulated the idea, since it states that parameters should be optimized to maximize production of black holes made by stars. Empirically testing the claim suggests that this is probably not the case. The parameters of the universe don’t appear especially finely tuned to maximize black-hole production via stars. The second problem is that if everything is optimized for stars, we are left without an explanation for our existence. Smolin asks us to believe that the universe is the way it is because cosmological evolution fine-tuned the parameters of physics to construct complex, long-lived universes that build many stars, and those parameters coincidentally happen to support our existence as a by-product.

Smolin’s idea resonates with many as an elegant explanation of the anthropic principle but remains short of broad acceptance due to these problems. Yet the idea led to an important extension: CNS could have originally selected for stars, but then subsequently for life, because intelligent life could ultimately manufacture far more black holes—and therefore offspring universes—than stars alone. This intellectual lineage is rooted in the same observations as the anthropic principle: the parameters of the universe do appear to be, at least in part, fine-tuned for life. Properties of carbon, properties of water, the details of emission of light from stars, fortuitous properties of macromolecules like proteins and nucleic acids—it can be hard to unsee all the coincidences. The mathematician Louis Crane took the idea one step further and [argued that CNS selects for life because advanced civilizations use black holes as energy sources](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10699-010-9182-y), connecting cosmological evolution to the specific observations of parameters tuned not just to make stars, but also to make life. If there is another layer of cosmological natural selection—one in which life can produce offspring universes—then it makes sense that parameters are finely tuned to enable our existence. Those parameters have been selected because they produce life that maximizes fecundity, so such universes are common and we shouldn’t be surprised to find ourselves in one. This means we should expect the universe to be finely tuned not just for stars, but also for life. And indeed, the tuning for life is what’s gotten everyone’s attention over the years.

To me, this is almost completely satisfying. It resolves the anthropic principle in a naturalistic framework while including fine-tuning for many aspects of the universe—including life—in the process. But still, there is one piece missing—and by adding this missing piece we can address the first problem identified above for CNS, that parameters don’t seem to be optimized to simply maximize production of black holes made by stars.

I’m a physicist, but not a theorist of the fundamentals like Smolin and Crane. I’m a device physicist. I’ve spent my career studying ways to use the laws of physics and the properties of the material world to build advanced technologies, mostly in the domain of computing. At one point in my career, it struck me that the same kind of fine-tuning we see related to stars and life is also present in the domain of technology. The fundamental physical properties of silicon are improbably aligned to allow transistors with supreme performance for computing. The properties of superconducting materials such as niobium and the cooling properties of helium are uncannily capable of supporting extraordinary feats of device physics. The universe provides us with high-speed superconducting circuits that have exactly no electrical resistance and can operate like qubits or like neurons and synapses in the brain. The parameters of physics support superconducting wires that carry extreme amounts of current to make huge magnetic fields to hold plasmas in advanced fusion reactors. These properties are so incredibly useful for advanced technologies, and yet are supported only within a narrow range of physical parameters, pointing to the same kind of fine-tuning that launched the anthropic principle hullabaloo in the first place. And of course, stars and then life are first required to conjure these tools into existence.

A simple extension of cosmological evolution can explain this. If Smolin’s hypothesis is correct, and black holes produce offspring universes, then lifeforms also have the potential to participate in this process, as Crane proposed. But a human can’t produce a black hole barehanded, and even a T. rex can’t do it with its jaws. Technology is required. If a living creature is going to produce singularities, it must invent or discover tools that can produce these quantum-gravitational states. Therefore, the parameters of the universe must also be tuned to support powerful technological performance. This leads to a modified model with a new prediction: careful inspection should find the parameters of our universe are tuned at a compromise point between the demands of stars, life, and technology. This careful inspection can be carried out with a series of computer simulations. Details are given [here](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1367-2630/ab9d9d), but the takeaway is that, unlike most anthropic reasoning, this hypothesis can be tested, and the tests don’t even require extreme apparatus like particle colliders, space telescopes, or gravitational interferometers.

To me, this is the most satisfying resolution of the anthropic principle. It is not based on astronomically improbable chance. It’s not based on observer bias. It explains why the universe is so peculiarly adapted for us—but not only us, also stars, and also technology. And the model points to an evolutionary trajectory in which earlier universes reproduced only through stars, but then complexified through mutations to later generations with life and then technology. Within this framework, the present moment of explosive growth in computation sits comfortably in the larger context. If this explanation is correct, we should expect to be able to construct advanced technologies capable of forming black holes. These include computational technologies and energy technologies, in accord with recent tremendous progress in AI, quantum computing, and fusion energy. Semiconductors are central here, but so are superconductors. The theory can be tested by assessing whether the parameters that define the universe are finely tuned to enable long-lived stars, but also carbon-water life, and semiconductor-superconductor technology.

Nothing in this piece is settled science, but the idea put forth is a testable, scientific hypothesis that would resolve the conundrum of the anthropic principle. Cosmological evolution provides the explanation, yet CNS has been largely set aside by scientists because initial empirical study was looking for the wrong signature by analyzing stars in isolation. If cosmological evolution selected only for stars, we would expect the parameters defining the universe to lie within a certain range. However, if life and technology are included, we should expect the parameters to take values that compromise between the competing pulls of stars, life, and technology. A new study must be conducted that looks specifically for this compromise. Fortunately, this can be carried out with modest resources of computers for condensed matter simulations and a team’s salaries for a few years.

If correct, this is not just a resolution of the anthropic principle but also an explanation for how this complex universe emerged from a simpler initial vacuum fluctuation and why we find ourselves evolving here as biological beings and also liminal entities that lift technology into existence. At a moment in history marked by the roiling churn at the interface where biology and technology stir, [our purpose and our future can feel unmoored](https://iai.tv/video/solving-the-meaning-crisis-john-vervaeke). But from the perspective of cosmological natural selection, ushering technology into existence is a major part of our purpose. We and our machines are kin with a common function, collaborative participants grounded in an ancient cosmological mission. We humans are not the point of all of this, but we are a critical part—an elegant fiber, a gossamer yet load-bearing thread in the cosmological braid.
