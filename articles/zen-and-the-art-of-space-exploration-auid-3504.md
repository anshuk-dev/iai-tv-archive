# Zen and the art of space exploration

*Buddhism is made for the space age*

**By Ben Van Overmeire** | *24th February 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,449 words*

![Zen and the art of space exploration](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/2001-space-odyssey-still.webp)

---

*From* Star Wars *to* The Three-Body Problem*, science fiction repeatedly turns to Buddhism to imagine humanity’s future in space. Drawing on its doctrines of emptiness, interconnection, and cosmic plurality, **Ben Van Overmeire** argues that Buddhism offers a ready-made cosmology for thinking about alien life and a decentered universe – an alternative to Western narratives of conquest, colonisation, and divine centrality. This cross-cultural perspective offers vital insight into how we should approach outer space, which is an urgent question as commercial spaceflight expands and new powers enter the space race.*

In an iconic scene from the *Star Wars* movie [*The Empire Strikes Back*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0080684/) (1980), a young rebel named Luke Skywalker, having crashed his spacecraft into a swamp on a godforsaken planet, meets a tiny green alien. He isn’t looking for such an alien; he is looking for a great warrior named Yoda. He expresses as much to the alien, who immediately rebuts in his typical syntax: “Ohhh. Great warrior. Wars not make one great.” But of course Luke is being taken for a ride: this joyous little being, who rummages through Luke’s stuff, eats his food, and spars with his robot, is that same master Yoda. Luke is being tested and taught a lesson, which Yoda tries to teach him again and again: “You must unlearn what you have learned.”

*The Empire Strikes Back* was directed and produced by people [deeply interested in Asian religions](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14639947.2014.890348). Master Yoda himself, with his typical way of putting verbs at the end of sentences, was inspired by a living Zen Buddhist master, Shunryu Suzuki, whom the producer George Lucas met in California. Yoda’s urging for Luke to forget what he has learned is a version of the title of Suzuki’s most famous book, [*Zen Mind, Beginner’s Mind*](https://www.shambhala.com/zen-mind-beginner-s-mind-1796.html?srsltid=AfmBOoppJbyGgtrHgN1TcGATsfWnWItRqYNyIqI4BtxzA0Znr0qlCWOp) (1970), while his emphasis on ethical behavior (wars are not great) reflects Buddhism’s broader ethical core teachings that discourage killing. One of the most famous science fictions about humanity spread across outer space thus draws strongly on Buddhism for its cosmology and ethics.

\_\_\_

I’ve come to think of Buddhism as the global counterculture of space exploration.

\_\_\_

Yoda is not unique. Indeed, a walk through the science fiction halls of fame reveals that many visionary authors gave Buddhism a substantial place in their imagination of a space-borne humanity. Before *Star Wars*, Arthur C. Clarke, the so-called “prophet of the space age” and the driving imagination behind the epoch-changing film [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0062622/)(1968), [imagined Buddhism to be the only religion that survives humanity’s contact with a wiser alien species](https://www.amazon.com/Rendezvous-Arthur-Clarke-Centenary-Storyworlds-ebook/dp/B0BT857PD9/ref=sr_1_1?crid=2L7Q1R9H9BMMX&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.AygSyr1vW7qOjUA5rIMeloaEtbSYvN6R9AGOMjVlCaw0fkkqItLaoCcOUoXGKFCWuR45wQfvDJIsyG5M56sRhLdKLYpAu99suwl2f1C29yV6at2i0pga3wDAIoIDwMHt9lArCq6-dcYa38PmcfXlUq0G6TpuZUX-SieoP8A9JwBm7lWbPOBfNK9-mSh92GsBADKfqfn8_jTJqJYlBskkowxVc3E1g2txwr4IlE7_6M8._jOgZrXZHv9ZMZ8y3DOfClEs8ERG9kl1F3ZuuvcUUKw&dib_tag=se&keywords=Rendezvous+with+Arthur+C.+Clarke%3A+Centenary+Essays+%28SF+Storyworlds%3A+Critical+Studies+in+Science+Fiction%29&nsdOptOutParam=true&qid=1771847078&sprefix=rendezvous+with+arthur+c.+clarke+centenary+essays+sf+storyworlds+critical+studies+in+science+fiction+%2Caps%2C155&sr=8-1). Closer to our own time, Dan Simmons’s [*Hyperion*](https://www.orionbooks.co.uk/titles/dan-simmons/hyperion/9781399609500/#:~:text=Hyperion%20by%20Dan%20Simmons%20%7C%20Orion,Search%20magnifying%20glass%20graphic) (1989) sage repeatedly casts massive AI entities that regulate our outer space travel as Zen Buddhist masters. In East Asia the presence of Buddhism is even more pronounced: [as I have shown elsewhere](https://brill.com/view/journals/jcph/51/2-3/article-p137_5.xml), Liu Cixin’s [*The Three Body Problem*](https://torpublishinggroup.com/the-three-body-problem/#:~:text=The%20Three%2DBody%20Problem%20%2D%20Tor%20Publishing%20Group) cycle, the most popular Chinese science fiction series of all time recently turned into a Netflix TV series, draws strongly on Zen to elaborate its bleak, Hobbesian vision of outer space, while his colleague and second most influential Chinese science fiction author [Han Song](https://paper-republic.org/pers/han-song/) imagines a dystopian future where the Buddhist ethics of care penetrate every aspect of our existence.

These texts may be fiction, but they reflect on how we understand and imagine the vast unknown that is outer space, a place almost none of us have ever visited. And even those that have bring back stories that resonate with Buddhist ideas. Take astronaut Edgar Mitchell. In 1971, [he had a vision](https://archive.org/details/wayofexplorerapo0000edga). Seeing the Earth from space, he was overwhelmed by “a grand epiphany accompanied by exhilaration, an event I would later refer to in terms that could not be more foreign to my upbringing in West Texas, and later, New Mexico. From that moment on my life would take a radically different course.” Later on, Mitchell would classify his experience as spontaneous *samadhi*, the term Buddhists use for a state of complete meditative absorption.

As someone who has built his academic career on studying how Western literature incorporates Buddhist ideas, characters, and stories, I found myself asking: “Why Buddhism?” The book I’m currently writing tries to answer that exact question, drawing on Buddhist thinkers, astronaut autobiographical narratives, and science fiction to provide a history of the Buddhist imagination of outer space. It focuses on sources in two main languages, English and Chinese, to argue that Buddhism provides a framework for humanity’s relationship with outer space that can challenge Western paradigms of conquest and colonization, of positivism and materialism, of anthropocentric individualism and heroism. In short, I’ve come to think of Buddhism as the global counterculture of space exploration.

\_\_\_

More than any other word religion, Buddhism offers an imaginative vocabulary to understand the vastness of outer space and the existence of alien life.

\_\_\_

Like any interesting question, there is no straightforward answer to the question “Why does Buddhism emerge so prominently in these narratives?” One answer is historical: In America, interest in Buddhism surged exactly at the time the space race with the Soviet Union was at its most intense. This was obviously also the time of the counterculture, an unprecedented global questioning of authority that turned to the East for inspiration. Buddhism’s appearance in *Star Wars*, the work of Clarke, Frank Herbert’s [*Dune*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/352036/dune-movie-tie-in-by-frank-herbert/9780441005901/), and so forth can be seen as a reflection of Buddhism’s popularity in other fields as well. A second explanation is Buddhism’s [perceived proximity to science](https://iai.tv/video/east-meets-west-chris-impey). In the twentieth century, many depicted Buddhism as a basically secular philosophy that had obtained some religious elements during its long history. This version of Buddhism has deeply appealed to science fiction authors, and Buddhists such as the Chinese monk Taixu or the Dalai Lama have done their best to show that Buddhism is eminently compatible with science.

Though both of these answers make sense, I myself am drawn to a third, philosophical explanation: Buddhism figures so prominently in our collective imagination of a space-based future because, more than any other word religion, Buddhism offers an imaginative vocabulary to understand the vastness of outer space and the existence of alien life. These elements are present in its classic texts, which describe a multiverse that is both empty and teeming with life.

Buddhism’s philosophical alignment with such an empty-yet-full cosmos is particularly striking when contrasted with the twentieth-century reality of space exploration. Until recently, the primary space-faring nations have been rooted in Christianity, a faith tradition where both the vastness of space and the possibility of alien life present major challenges. The narrative of the Bible focuses on our world, Earth, and does not say much about other worlds. Did God create those as well? What is their role and their relationship to God? If Christ died for humanity’s sins, did he also redeem alien species (can aliens sin?)

Not few science fiction authors have engaged these types of theological questions. In the short story “[Omphalos](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/538034/exhalation-by-ted-chiang/),” the renowned Asian-American short story writer Ted Chiang portrays a world where creationism is correct, a world where archeological evidence of the first trees (without tree rings) and first humans (who have no navel) can be recovered. However, this world is horrified by the discovery of an Earthlike planet (one that almost certainly contains life) that does not revolve around a star, but instead has a star rotating around itself. This discovery of a geocentric system leads to the shocking realization that humankind is perhaps not central to God’s plan, that they are merely a byproduct of creation. In *Hyperion*, a Jesuit priest is first delighted by the discovery of cruciforms on another planet, which seems to indicate an intergalactic presence of Christianity. However, these cruciforms turn out to be merciless parasites that doom their bearers to mental degradation for eternity, a devastating literalization of the promise of eternal life through the cross. “Omphalos” and *Hyperion*, as many other science fictions, dramatize a deep theological anxiety caused by the discovery of alien life.

Buddhism, however, does not face the same theological hurdles as Christianity does. Instead of seeing the cosmos in terms of what *is*, the departure point of the Buddhist universe is *what is not*. This is why the Buddha claims that life is suffering: we suffer because we can find nothing that is permanent and enduring, no self and no world that remains unchanged throughout time. In Mahayana Buddhism, the type of Buddhism that gave rise to Zen, this understanding was conceptualized as “emptiness” (*shunyata* in Sanskrit, *kong* in Chinese): underlying the myriad phenomena there is nothingness, a creative void that gives rise to endless possibilities.

This realm of endless possibilities is forcefully expressed in Mahayana scriptures, which usually feature hosts of non-human beings interacting with the Buddha and his disciples. Often, these scriptures discuss Buddha-realms, other worlds with their own types of beings and their own Buddhas. The most popular of these realms is no doubt the realm of Amitabha Buddha (*Amituofo* in Chinese, *Amida* in Japanese), but there are countless other such realms, as numerous as grains of sand. The descriptions of these realms are “mind-bending,” as my colleague [James Shields has called it](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003088264-5/mahāyāna-mind-bending-james-mark-shields): everything that catches the eye is made out of gold, jewels, and other precious materials. As numerous as those realms are the types of beings that populate them. Some of these beings naturally (given the authorship of the sutras) resemble humans, but others are significantly different from us: we hear of beings that take nourishment from music or beings that enjoy sexuality without physicality. The Buddhist imagination in these texts is quite remarkable.

Another doctrinal consequence of emptiness is that all beings, across time and space, are interconnected. If nothing has an enduring essence, this necessarily means that everything is constantly affecting everything else. This might seem abstruse but is quite commonsensical: my mood today is affected by how others have behaved towards me today and in the past. How I feel is also affected by the air quality, the food I’ve eaten, changes in my microbiome, and so on. This means that if I look deeply enough into myself (usually through meditative concentration), I can see only others. The whole cosmos exists within me.

This is why, for most of their history, Buddhists did not need rockets to travel to outer space. All a Buddhist who wanted to travel to Mars needed to do was sit down. One— usually less highlighted—aspect of Shakyamuni Buddha’s enlightenment story is that, just before achieving awakening, he [sees other worlds](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Sūtra-on-the-foundation-of-the-Buddhist-order-Kloppenborg/9beced0fe24e05a2574589ad77f7acf343a33543). With his “divine ear [which is] pure and transcends human [ear], he heard both sounds, human as well as non-human and those [sounds] which are far as well as those which are near.” And with his “divine eye, [which is] pure and transcends human [eye], he saw the beings, dying as well as coming into existence, [beings] of good colour as well as of bad colour, inferior ones as well as superior ones, those who enter a good existence as well as those who enter a bad existence, living according to the [their] actions [karma], he truly knew the beings.” Upon the moment of his awakening, the Buddha is, in short, seeing all beings in the cosmos, and he is doing so without moving from his position under the bodhi tree.

As Luke trains with Yoda, he learns to travel like this as well: not only does he get visions of what goes on in other places of the galaxy, but eventually (in the more recent additions to the series), he learns to project an astral body (another Buddhist notion) to save the galactic rebellion in its time of greatest need. He begins to feel his connection to all other beings, and the ethical behavior that attends such an insight: to be kind, to minimize desires and strong emotions, to live a simpler life. He becomes a starborne Buddhist monk.

*Star Wars* is but one example of how Buddhist ideas can elaborate an alternative vision of outer space. A different way of doing so is constituted by Liu Cixin’s *The Three Body Problem*. One of the many Buddhist ideas elaborated in his fiction is that a nostalgic attachment to Earth as the only home of mankind is pointless: eventually, humankind, if it is to survive, will have to let go of this attachment and wander around the cosmos. The language he uses to elaborate this vision is that of “leaving home,” which is what Buddhist monks are said to do when they set out on the monastic path. In Buddhism, this is deemed a necessity because it is our attachments that cause us pain and suffering. In *The Three Body Problem*, it is exactly humankind’s reluctance to leave Earth, our nostalgic attachment to our home planet, that causes the near-extinction of the human race.

Historically, the continued state funding for space exploration despite its increasingly diminishing returns attests to the fact that [the space race is much more than a display of technology or even a symbol of military superiority](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14777622.2013.801720). The quest to conquer the skies speaks of a more deeply rooted desire to rise up and encounter the divine that was traditionally located up in the skies. Already, this quest is generating new (and often disturbing) gendered and millenarian religious narratives such as those proposed by billionaire space entrepreneurs, [who see our planet as a fallen place that we ought to escape from](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/A/bo184287883.html). At the same time, relative newcomers to [the space race](https://iai.tv/articles/science-fictions-guilty-conscience-auid-453), such as China and India, bring with them a way of seeing the human role in outer space that is different from the Christian narrative that has thus far dominated the rationale in going beyond our atmosphere. Buddhism lies [at the foundation of both these cultural traditions](https://iai.tv/video/after-buddhism), and in our historical moment, understanding the alternative vision of our cosmos that it elaborates seems to me most crucial.

I do not believe that we will ever be able to approach outer space without some religious framework: these frameworks are simply too deeply rooted in our cultural background. What we can do, however, is to become aware of [the frameworks within which we imagine the universe and our role within it](https://iai.tv/video/science-fiction-for-a-dystopian-present-cory-doctorow). It seems likely that we will soon see commercial spaceflight becoming affordable for those of us who aren’t billionaires. If this does happen, more of us than ever before will leave our atmosphere to glimpse, and eventually perhaps inhabit, a place that is unlike any other we’ve encountered on Earth. We do not have much time left, then, to study and understand the stories that we tell about outer space, and how these stories answer the most important questions of humankind: who are we, and why are we here?
