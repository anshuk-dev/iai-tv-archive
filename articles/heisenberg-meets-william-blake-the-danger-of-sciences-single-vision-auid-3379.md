# Heisenberg meets William Blake: The danger of science's "single vision"

*The need for poetic science*

**By Mark Vernon** | *7th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,574 words*

![Heisenberg meets William Blake: The danger of science's "single vision"](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Heisenberg-meets-William-Blake-copy.webp)

---

*William Blake and Werner Heisenberg, radical poet and pioneering quantum physicist, shared the same fear: that science, while enabling us to intervene in the world ever more powerfully, was at the same time cutting us off from reality itself. Science works by inferring general categories and laws from individual observations. This potent method, left unchecked, locks us into a generalizing model, which Blake calls a “single vision” and in which, Heisenberg says, “reality is lost.” For both poet and physicist, only through poetry and imagination can we reconnect with reality’s vivid particulars, argues psychotherapist and writer Mark Vernon.*

The poet William Blake is often thought to be anti-science. Consider one of his best-known phrases: “Single vision and Newton’s sleep.” That sounds like a criticism. “Reductive” would be the modern word: science produces a limited perception of things, an accusation firmly pinned to the standout genius of early-modern science, Isaac Newton.

But consider again, because Blake can also fulsomely affirm the sciences. In one place, he writes: “What is the Life of Man but Art & Science?” In another: “Arts & Sciences are the Destruction of Tyrannies or Bad Governments.”

So what is Blake’s complaint and why might it matter now?

An initially simplified view of his worry provides a starting point. Roughly speaking, Blake was against any account of things that gave exclusive place to the rational. Newton takes a hit because of [his application of rigid principles of cause and effect](https://iai.tv/video/newton-s-space-emily-thomas?ts=1618827126) as a dominant mode of explanation.

\_\_\_

Generalizations are deductions about the nature of things, derived from examples and instances. That is an inductive step, and the process steps back from reality into a model of reality. This attempt to understand things therefore takes us from them.

\_\_\_

So [remarkably powerful was this approach](https://iai.tv/articles/what-newton-really-meant-daniel-hoek-auid-2579) that Newton’s work inspired parallel efforts to understand other phenomena. Another key figure of opprobrium for Blake was, therefore, [John Locke](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke/), a friend of Newton who thought of himself as an “under-labourer” to the great scientist. Locke applied observation and reason to questions of human understanding and behaviour, producing his best-known theory that the mind is a blank slate, or *tabula rasa*. Born empty, the mind absorbs sensory experiences that are then shaped by inner processes of reasoning and remembering.

Blake resisted this rationalising turn for a range of reasons. But one stands out for its relevance to us today. The heart of Blake’s complaint is that the world around us is not best understood through generalizations. His logic is clear: generalizations are deductions about the nature of things, derived from examples and instances. That is an inductive step, and the process steps back from reality into a model of reality. This attempt to understand things therefore takes us from them.

\_\_\_

Through this process of idealization and precise definition the immediate connection with reality is lost.

\_\_\_

A similar critique has been made by others. From Blake’s own time, the best-known of these was the Scottish philosopher, [David Hume](iai-academy/courses/info?course=life-lessons-from-david-hume). But consider someone else: Werner Heisenberg, one of the [founding figures of quantum physics](https://iai.tv/articles/how-quantum-mechanics-changed-science-forever-auid-3144). Science requires a version of reality amenable to mathematical analysis, Heisenberg noted. “But through this process of idealization and precise definition the immediate connection with reality is lost,” [he continued](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/56983/physics-and-philosophy-by-werner-heisenberg-intro-paul-davies/9780141182155). Further, Heisenberg and Blake had an additional concern.

Generalizations have a seductive power. The appeal of so-called “[laws of nature](https://iai.tv/video/conjuring-the-universe)” is not just their ability to encapsulate certain events, but their utility, too. Newtonian science greatly extends possibilities for the development of technology—not least in the form of the industrial revolution that Newton and Locke did not see, but Blake did. He realised that “dark Satanic mills,” to quote another famous phrase, were not only burgeoning features of the cities and towns of industrial England, but were a kind of mentality: as if reality itself were “a mill with complicated wheels.”

This tendency to view reality through the lens of the technology that dominates the day is another facet of “Single vision and Newton’s Sleep.” In Blake’s time, a mechanical worldview was the result; in ours, an information age, [information processing provides the go-to metaphors](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02650185). If technology needs a mentality that prepares the way for it, the resulting technology can also colonize the mentality.

Heisenberg echoed the concern when he argued that people need to trust other kinds of knowledge, not only that of science, to stay in touch with reality. He feared that, unchecked, physics alone produces what he called a “rigid frame” that can demonstrate great power with its purchase on material things, but is inclined to reduce or dismiss other dimensions of existence remembered in our language—“the concepts of mind, of the human soul or of life,” he writes in his book, *Physics and Philosophy*. He himself found succour in his conversations with Rabindranath Tagore, the Indian philosopher and poet. [Poetry might matter more than physics](https://iai.tv/video/poetic-theories), intuitions more than theories. “They touch reality,” Heisenberg continues, in a way that concepts do not. Rationalising can seize the mind, can become a way of life, and so render other forms of perception, like intuitions, doubtful.

Blake himself placed his faith in the imagination. He lamented the downgrading of the imagination in his time—and would no doubt feel similarly in ours. Then and now, the imagination is treated as a private possession that, whilst adding colour to life and on occasion providing personal inspiration, must be disciplined by evidence and reason, [which are the determining guides to truth](https://iai.tv/articles/imagination-is-not-an-escape-from-reality-auid-3002). But Blake held that the imagination, well discerned, offers a much richer way of encountering the world because it can embrace the enormously diverse variety of living things and cosmic features without having to reduce them to types and categories. “Nature is imagination itself,” he cried, arguing that everything from trees to stars are manifestations of an imaginative outpouring that is rational, but in the premodern sense of demonstrating connections shaped by beauty and affinity, tension and care. Moreover, with imagination, we can align with the multifaceted and dynamic intelligences active in creatures and the cosmos because a similar intelligence is also active within ourselves. Things may look mechanical but really they are alive.

\_\_\_

A culture which denied this vitality and grew more in love with generalizing would fall out of love with the direct encounter. In time, the map would fascinate more than the landscape; the screen image more than the real thing.

\_\_\_

This expanded vision illuminates the way in which Blake valued science, for he was in a way something of a scientist himself. He devoted himself to what he calls “sweet science” that fosters love for “minute particulars,” meaning the individual, specific beings we encounter, be they animal, vegetable or mineral. Take his art. It is marked by his experimentation with different types of material, and his workshop would have been more akin to a chemistry lab than a studio. Know-how about metals and acids, pigments and oils, presses and paper was the essence of his work, and he viewed these materials as entities to befriend rather than commodities to manipulate.

He perceived, further, that a culture which denied this vitality and grew more in love with generalizing would fall out of love with the direct encounter. In time, the map would fascinate more than the landscape; the screen image more than the real thing: “Abstract Philosophy warring in enmity against Imagination,” he lamented. So, his critique of science is a warning: science by itself will take us from a buzzing, blooming immersion in life, even as it offers powerful ways of studying that life. “You accumulate Particulars, & murder by analyzing. But General Forms have their vitality in Particulars.”

What began in the sciences would, he feared, affect other forms of knowledge. He predicted that generalizations might reshape every part of life because of the “single vision” they impose upon our perception of it. That would not only distance human beings from the natural world but from each other and even from themselves. Consider the impact upon morality.

Ethical ideas were also changing fast in Blake’s time. Prior to the era of Newton and Locke, the main philosophical focus in ethics had been the virtues: the qualities or habits that a person has. Shape those well, live well within the wider community of being.

But near contemporaries of Blake, notably Jeremy Bentham and Immanuel Kant, were devising new moral theories, inspired by the Newtonian worldview. Bentham developed “felicific calculus,” with the promise that a rational calculation would produce the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people. Kant tried to derive rationally determined universal laws, which he called categorical imperatives.

Blake worried that this approach to morality would deepen the disconnection people felt from others and from themselves. [Moral laws would waste life](https://iai.tv/articles/alasdair-macintyre-the-man-who-declared-morality-a-fiction-auid-3182), he said, because calculations would undermine, even replace, the messy business of actual relationships—as if principles were a better guide than, say, feelings like love and hate. “Energy is the only life,” he countered. Better to be wrong than be right in the wrong way.

“What is the Life of Man but Art & Science?” he had written. “Arts & Sciences are the Destruction of Tyrannies or Bad Governments.” The arts and the sciences together will keep us free from a tyranny that is at once ideological and practical. And we might do well to heed that now. “Nations are Destroy’d or Flourish in proportion to Their Poetry, Painting, and Music are Destroy’d or Flourish!” Blake wrote. The thought is not a mere Romantic gesture. It is a clear epistemological observation.
