# The moral price tag of decency

*Why 1% Might Be Enough*

**By Michael Plant** | *5th June 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,455 words*

![The moral price tag of decency](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Aim-at-decency-not-sainthood.webp)

---

*In a world of vast inequality, morality demands more than good intentions. Is giving just 1% of your income to effective charities the minimum for being a decent person?*

Most of us like to see ourselves as good, morally decent individuals. What’s more, we largely agree on what it means to be a decent person. You don’t just have to pay your taxes and not harm others. You have to go beyond that by, for instance, being kind, treating others with respect, and supporting your friends, family and neighbours.

What our modern secular society lacks, however, is a clear idea of how a decent person should give to charity. We live in a world of staggering inequality and extreme need. There will always be more we could do. When have we given enough - not to be a saint, but to clear the bar of decency?

One extreme view comes from the philosopher [Peter Singer](http://iai.tv/articles/mapping-morality-peter-singer-vs-his-critics-auid-2245). In his [famous thought experiment](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/get-involved/videos-books-and-essays/famine-affluence-and-morality-peter-singer), you’re walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning. You could save the child—but it would ruin your expensive new shoes. Most people agree that it would be wrong not to wade in. Singer proposes the principle that if we can prevent something bad from happening, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do so.

Singer extends this principle to charity. He points out that, everyday, we’re like the person on the side of the pond. We could sacrifice some of our material wealth by giving to charities and this would be a great benefit to others at a small cost to us. If we’re required to sacrifice our shoes, why aren’t we also required to give?

Yet, if other people matter morally as much as we do ourselves, the terrifying implication is that we should keep giving until we’re left with nothing - besides that which we need to survive.

That is a crushing standard. Even Singer admits he doesn’t meet it. And, crucially for our purposes, giving away all your spare income is far more than just decent: it’s saintly. Clearly, you can be a [morally decent person](http://iai.tv/articles/hobbes-vs-rousseau-are-we-inherently-evil-or-good-auid-1221) without being a saint. We are asking what a decent person should do, not what a saint would do.

At the other extreme is the idea that we don’t need to give anything at all. That’s too lenient. Moral decency may not require everything, but it definitely requires something.

So, where’s the line?

Many people think giving should fall to “the rich.” But the rich include more people than you might think. If you earn the median UK income—around £37,000—you’re in the [top 2%](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2025/giving-to-others-how-to-convert-your-money-into-greater-happiness-for-others/#fn11) of the global income distribution. Those of us in this bracket often don’t *feel* rich because we live in wealthy societies and we engage in upwards comparisons, paying more attention to those better off than us that those worse off. Resultantly, we can suffer from a sort of ‘[money dysmorphia](https://www.independent.co.uk/bulletin/lifestyle/why-people-think-they-are-poor-money-dysmorphia-b2732780.html)’ - an analogue of body dysmorphia - where we see ourselves as poorer than we are.

But most readers of this article are *exceptionally* fortunate. It’s been estimated that 117 billion members of our species [have ever lived](https://www.prb.org/articles/how-many-people-have-ever-lived-on-earth/#:~:text=Still%2C%20with%20some%20assumptions%20about,ever%20been%20born%20on%20Earth.). That means that if you’re in the 2% richest today, you’re likely in the 0.1% richest people in history. Surely the richest 0.1% can afford to give a bit - even if they already help strangers in other ways.

In the Christian tradition, one would give a tithe, a full 10% of one’s earnings; this norm held in a medieval era where people were far poorer. Should we resurrect and adopt that as a modern secular norm? 10% is, frankly, a daunting sum. It’s far more that most people give today and few have the slack in their budget to suddenly give that much. 10% seems more than decent; it’s impressive.

Norms should evolve, so let me suggest one that is both less demanding on us and means we would have a greater impact: giving 1% of our income, but to the most cost-effective charities.

The ‘most cost-effective’ part may be surprising, but let me explain some new evidence on charity impact and how this takes us to 1%.

In recent research published in the [*World Happiness Report*](https://worldhappiness.report/ed/2025/giving-to-others-how-to-convert-your-money-into-greater-happiness-for-others/), my team and I at the [Happier Lives Institute](https://www.happierlivesinstitute.org/) conducted the first global comparison of charities. We looked at how much happiness different charities create per dollar. We quantified happiness in terms of wellbeing-years, or WELLBYs. The metric is simple: 1 WELLBY equals a one-point increase in wellbeing for 1 person for 1 year, measured on a zero to 10 scale. So, if your self-reported life satisfaction went from a 7/10 to a 8/10 for a year, that’s 1 WELLBY. The WELLBY was proposed in academia in [2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03229-5) and endorsed by the [UK Treasury](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-wellbeing) for comparing policies in 2021; it’s a new measure of social value.

We found the [best charities](https://www.happierlivesinstitute.org/research/charity-evaluations/)—which tend to work in the Global South on issues like lead poisoning, severe malnutrition, and depression—create 1 WELLBY for about £15. To put that in perspective, £15 is the cost of a meal out, and a doubling of income is associated with a 0.2 increase in life satisfaction. If you know a pizza place that gives you so much joy for £15, let me know.

What really astonished us was how much the cost-effectiveness of charities varied. The top NGOs created around 150 times more happiness per dollar than the UK-based charities we examined. The best charity we found was nearly 1,000 times more impactful than the least-good. To press the point, giving £1,000 to one charity could do as much good as giving £1m to another.

This finding is in stark contrast with people’s expectations. In [further research](https://www.happierlivesinstitute.org/2025/04/21/the-best-charities-to-donate-to-expectation-reality/), we found that people think the best charities are only 3 times more cost-effective than the average ones. This misperception has real consequences: people don’t attempt to be ‘effective givers’ - to support the most impactful charities - in part because they believe one charity is about as good as another. In fact, you can do a huge amount more good, at no extra cost to yourself, just by changing where you give.

This conclusion, that the best charities are much better than we expected, suggests a new norm for giving.

Imagine someone picked a charity at random and gave 30% of their income to it. We’d call them not merely decent, but heroic. But if you gave just 1% to the most effective organisations, our estimates mean you would have five times the impact—equivalent to giving an impossible 150% of your income to the average charity. If you have far more impact than a hero, surely that’s enough to clear the bar for moral decency. If you *can* have more impact than a hero at a small cost to yourself, there is perhaps a moral duty that you *should.*

Consequently, I’d argue that *where* you give is much more important than *how much* you give but I’m proposing giving 1% as a useful minimum.

Why 1%, not 5% or 10%? Because 1% is a challenging but attainable goal. I see it as the impact equivalent of eating five fruits and vegetables a day—or taking 10,000 steps. A small change with large benefits. Anything less than 1% feels tokenistic, whereas 10% is the moral equivalent of telling people to run a supermarathon. If you think you could easily give 10% without noticing it, you should be giving at least 1%.

If you’re not giving already, giving 1% to effective charities is the place to start. You don’t have to go straight to 1%. You could work towards it. [Singer himself has made arguments](https://www.thelifeyoucansave.org/book/) in favour of those new to charitable giving to start gradually with small amounts in the interest of it becoming a sustainable and widespread habit.

If you’re giving more, but haven’t given much thought to maximise your impact, consider using the research from a charity evaluator like my own, the [Happier Lives Institute](https://www.happierlivesinstitute.org/), who has done the work for you to find the most impactful organisations. We focus on the best ways to improve people’s wellbeing. But if you want your resources to go as far as possible towards, say, helping animals or the environment, you might prefer the recommendations of [Animal Charity Evaluators](https://animalcharityevaluators.org/) or [Giving Green](https://www.givinggreen.earth/), respectively.

If you already give more than 1% effectively, I applaud you. Please don’t stop.

What makes someone a decent person is not merely worrying, or complaining, about the state of the world, it’s actually [making a difference](http://iai.tv/video/the-self-and-the-selfless). Because some charities can do so much more good than others, giving 1% effectively seems the least a decent person should do. It means doing your bit and making a heroic difference without sacrificing too much. And perhaps, if this became a social norm, that would encourage those even richer to do their bit too.
