# Everything doesn't happen for a reason

*The myth of modern stoicism*

**By Tracy Llanera** | *7th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,350 words*

![Everything doesn't happen for a reason](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-myth-of-modern-stoicism2.webp)

---

*“Everything happens for a reason,” “it’s meant to be,” “it is what it is.” These cliches express an increasingly popular form of Stoic fatalism. The underlying idea is that “Reality” just is a certain way, determined by God or physics. This superficially tough realism comforts us by absolving us of responsibility: whatever happens was bound to happen. But this makes it dangerous, argues Tracy Llanera. It leads to resigned inaction in the face of geopolitical strife, injustice, and our personal lives. Instead, we must recognize that there is no higher being responsible for us: we must take responsibility for each other and the world we live in.*

“Everything happens for a reason” is a popular salve for life’s nasty surprises. Many cling to it for existential comfort, or, better yet, the promise of a good ending.

Your house catches fire*?* Your dog’s stomach twists, and he dies mid-surgery at the age of five? A plane combusts mid-air while flying over the Pacific Ocean? Someone hacks into your bank account and withdraws your life savings? Anew virus wipes out 7 million people in the twenty-first century? Two friends are diagnosed with cancer in less than two months? Your beloved wife of twenty years files for divorce? Trump is President of the United States of America? Again?

You know how it goes: everything happens for a reason.

When life takes a savage turn, people are desperate for an explanation. And when the cause of misfortune boils down to bad luck, horrid behavior, or things simply running their course, some people want a fuller answer. They hanker for more justificatory flesh to grip, a deeper meaning or greater purpose behind the ordeal. They want reassurance that something better will come along, making the pain worth whatever valuable lesson it yields in the future.

\_\_\_

The idea that every little thing is set in stone is a morally misguided philosophy.

\_\_\_

Along with the religious flex “God has a plan for you,” the notion that all our suffering is predetermined, etched in ink by the cosmos or by a supernatural Being and preparing us for something better to come, is an answer all too easily embraced by many. Because, the very Christian story goes, there’s a blueprint to all this, and give or take it’s a good one and it always ends well. In times of trouble, it is a soothing belief paradigm. This mindset works to bring order to life’s chaos, promising that despite all the signs signaling the opposite, the kids are going to be alright. And the belief in a grand design to human existence can have real and good effects. It can speed up the process of reconciling with our grief, enabling us to pick up the pieces of the life we have left. It can serve, in other words, as the official permission to move on (whether this is a genuine road to recovery is case-by-case and harder to answer).

When I was younger, hearing “everything happens for a reason” while lying face-down in the muck made me uncomfortable. Now, it sets me off. The reason is simple: the idea that every little thing is set in stone is a morally misguided philosophy*.* It suggests that all kinds of pain and suffering are unavoidable, that the injustices and cruelties plaguing our short lives are not only inescapable but in some ludicrous way even deserved, and that there’s nothing we can do differently as human beings to change our fate. These are the loaded claims behind “it’s meant to be” and “it is what it is,” colloquialisms of fatalist, unreflective stoicism tendered by well-meaning souls.

\_\_\_

We are not responsible to a higher being, and neither is this higher being responsible for us. We are responsible for each other.

\_\_\_

Richard Rorty, an American philosopher, is suspicious of the impulse to sublimate our tragedies to a higher, meaningful order. He is a pragmatist who sees himself as anti-metaphysical, anti-dogmatic, and anti-authoritarian all the way down, and most of his philosophical career has been dedicated to tearing down the notion of the mind as our “[glassy essence](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691178158/philosophy-and-the-mirror-of-nature?srsltid=AfmBOooebfBn4KEYG33w_2H7fmqM_MVCUxRFTPXQeBiY02yTl6dK5WFJ),” demolishing universalist myths, and “[discarding the image of the fierce father figure](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/truth-and-progress/322C08FB76AA9084F6BBA2DBD26ABAB0)” or any other [God-surrogates in human culture](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674248915). Rorty, to put it another way, is critical of theories and social practices whose authority is premised on having an answer or a blueprint to *The Way Things Really Are* and *The Way Things Should Be.* Instead, philosophical concepts, meaning, belief, and science are best judged and understood in terms of their function, that is, for purposes of solving problems and for guiding human action.

Rorty’s pragmatism asserts that that there is no distinction between theory and practice. What we theorize about – whether we are talking about sociological problems, or issues in the hard sciences – are all social practices governed by communal norms. There is no ivory tower, no magical place where intellectuals can escape from the real world and experiment with wholly theoretical problems. The projects we undertake always have a connection to our practical lives. We belong to a community of inquirers, and it is to ourselves, and to our community, that we should hold ourselves accountable.

\_\_\_

“Look, there isn’t any authority that we can appeal to settle the quarrels between us. We’re going to have to deal with them ourselves.”

\_\_\_

We are not responsible to a higher being, and neither is this higher being responsible for us. *We are responsible for each other.*This is Rorty’s blunt comeback to a millennia-old story of religion that has shaped the course of human history. So, when it comes to our human, all too human problems, the best answer according to Rorty is this: “Look, there isn’t any authority that we can appeal to settle the quarrels between us. We’re going to have to deal with them ourselves.”

Why is this Rortyan all-hands-on-deck view important to consider, especially in the face of natural and human catastrophes? It is true that some tragedies are a matter of chance and pure dumb luck, and that others bear the mark of human greed and evil. But most tragedies are a mixture of contingency and human fault, and none of them need to be attached to a grand plan to matter, to transform us, or to have unforeseen consequences. And, more importantly, there is no guarantee that things will be better in the long run. Sometimes, they get even worse.

This philosophical position comes to life when applied in the real world. I am Filipino. The Philippines, an archipelago in Southeast Asia, is no stranger to wreckage wrought by monstrous tropical storms. I have memories of wading in waist-deep water after my classes back home in Manila, wearing my school uniform and a pair of low-heeled Mary Janes, dodging drowning rats, fallen electric wires, and potholes. A decade ago, you could count each year’s worst storms on the palm of your hand; today, you need both hands, and every typhoon is more ferocious than the previous one. The dead number thousands every time.

On television, Filipinos are called a resilient people, the type to always bounce back, smiling on camera while picking up the remains of their tattered homes. On the ground, religious cant offers an explanation more plausible to the homeless and the distraught than climate change: “God won’t give you anything you can’t handle,” “God is testing you,” or, like a broken record, “everything happens for a reason.” In government, local politicians plaster their faces on food donation bags and first-aid kits to shore up votes for re-election, an exercise they need not have engaged in had they used their time in office to craft better laws for environmental protection or go after the country’s worst polluters.

It is a grave mistake to think that the suffering borne by nature’s cataclysm is pre-ordained. It is also wishful thinking to believe that something better will always come out of it. While some elements are fixed, a greater part of the disaster was the work of human hands. And no amount of it happening for some cosmic reason is going to miraculously clean up the mess.

That part, my friends, is up to us.
