# Consciousness is not an object we can observe

*Materialism misses the self-awareness built into consciousness*

**By Manfred Frank** | *26th June 2026* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,310 words*

![Consciousness is not an object we can observe](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/consciousness-is-not-an-object-we-can-observe.webp)

---

*Physicist Carlo Rovelli recently proclaimed that consciousness is just another physical thing, while materialist philosophers argue that consciousness is simply one brain state observing another. What such views miss, argues **Manfred Frank**, a leading figure in contemporary German philosophy, is that consciousness comes with a built-in awareness of itself—an awareness that exists prior to any introspection or observation. Consciousness does not observe itself in the way we observe objects; rather, it dissolves the distinction between observer and observed altogether. This is why consciousness cannot be simply another object in the world, but instead exists in an entirely different dimension.*

**I**

If we had no consciousness our lives would be devoid of meaning. We have been immersed in this medium since before we were born and remain in it even in our dreams. And we do so without any effort on our part. Apart from when it is interrupted by deep sleep or we are unconscious after fainting or during anaesthesia, we remain in this state or are enveloped in it throughout our entire lives. Nothing is more familiar to us than consciousness; it is so familiar that we rarely notice it or attend to it.

Consciousness must therefore be clearly distinguished from attention. As the philosopher [Ned Block observed](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/2111.003.0030), “one can be aware of what one is not attending to.” While it makes perfect sense to urge someone to pull themselves together, to concentrate, to pay attention, or to think carefully, it would make little sense simply to say to someone: “*Be conscious!*”To understandsuch a command you would have to be a being that *is* already conscious.

We must also distinguish consciousness from knowledge. This becomes especially clear when we consider that we may have experiences that present themselves with the greatest clarity, yet which we are unable to classify correctly. For example, I may see the shade Red-128 and yet take it to be Red-120. However, the fact that I am misclassifying it in no way prevents me from seeing precisely what I see.

Is this not the reason why something that appears to be the most obvious and familiar thing in the world can at the same time become our [greatest philosophical riddle](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-cosmology-and-the-collapse-of-common-sense-auid-3272)? The fact that we are so unconditionally certain of our own consciousness does not mean that we possess infallible knowledge of it. It is in fact difficult to imagine that philosophy—be it the intellectual traditions of Eastern Asia or those of the Western world—would ever have arisen without the puzzlement occasioned by the riddle of consciousness, not to mention its later derivatives, psychology and neuroscience.

**II**

We can also view the mystery of consciousness from another perspective. It has the remarkable characteristic of being informed of its own existence or presence without the mediation of a second instance. In other words, every conscious state implies a form of *self*-consciousness.

Since Descartes, philosophers have reserved the term “immediate” for this distinctive mode of self-awareness, meaning quite literally a form of awareness that is not mediated by a second instance such as “[introspection](https://iai.tv/articles/introspection-is-an-illusion-created-by-the-brain-auid-3573)” or an “inward glance.” We often use these two terms quite unreflectively in everyday talk when referring to processes that we call “inner.” Yet they are misleading metaphors. Strictly speaking, anyone who attempts to turn their gaze “inward” will find literally nothing there—nothing, at least, that could be described as an inner object of observation.

**III**

If you have followed me thus far, the next step won’t surprise you. If it is true that our familiarity with consciousness is a *necessary* condition for the mere occurrence of consciousness (for more on this see the next section)—and if this familiarity may properly be described as “immediate”—then the explanatory model that has dominated philosophical accounts of consciousness since Descartes right up to the present, i.e. the “*reflection model*,” must be abandoned.

The German philosopher Dieter Henrich attributed this decisive insight to Johann Gottlieb Fichte, and referred to it as “[Fichte’s original insight](https://phil880.colinmclear.net/materials/readings/henrich-fichte.pdf).” Henrich’s argument can be summarized as follows: if consciousness were not originally familiar with itself—if this familiarity were not immediate—it would only become aware of itself through a higher-order act that turns back upon it. He pointed out that this is precisely what is meant by *reflection*: the reflective turning of an act upon itself.

Knowledge is different from what is known. Modes of consciousness do not differ from their awareness. They are identical to it.

\_\_\_

However, if this model were correct, we would never become conscious of our mental states or acts at all, for according to the initial premise the higher-order act would also not yet be conscious of itself. It would therefore require a third act to make it conscious, which in turn would require a fourth, and so on. At the top of this infinite series of states, stacked up one on top of the other, there would remain an act that was not conscious of itself, and the entire chain beneath it would collapse into the unconscious. Yet consciousness evidently does exist. [Hence the model must be false](https://archive.org/details/introductionstow00fich).

Contemporary defenders of the reflection model, [such as Rocco Gennaro](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262016605/the-consciousness-paradox/), object that such an infinite regress does not in fact arise. In their view, the stacking up of acts stops at the third level. Why on the third? Because a second-order act renders the first-order act conscious, while the second-order act itself becomes conscious through a third-order act. The proponents of [*higher-order theories*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-higher/)call this third-level state *introspection*. At this point, they argue, the regress comes to an end: the second-order act—made conscious by the third—encounters a first-order act that it has itself already rendered conscious. Consciousness thus meets consciousness, and familiarity with consciousness is explained without regress.

What has replaced the regress, however, is actually a circle. A knowledge claim is made that has not previously been justified, but merely presumed or asserted. For Gennaro fails to show how the second-order act *comes to know*—or *how it acquires the knowledge*—that it is *identical* with the first-order act. Indeed, according to his theory, the acts on the different levels are “numerically distinct.” However, what the theory sought to explain was *self*-consciousness, which by definition cannot involve something *distinct* from the conscious act coming to know that act.

Thus far I have described the knowledge presupposed both by the reflection model and by higher-order theories as immediate or familiar. We can now be more precise and call it *pre-reflective* self-awareness. Why? Because it already exists prior to reflection. Reflection is a retrospective act and can only discover what was already present. A contemporary of Fichte, Novalis (Friedrich von Hardenberg), one of the early Romantics, captured the idea with admirable simplicity: “What reflection *finds seems already to have been there*” (S*chriften*, 1965, 112, Nr. 14).

**IV**

Fichte’s “original insight” has—unwittingly—been rediscovered by several contemporary philosophers, albeit in a very different conceptual framework. They argue that a conscious mental state like pain is *necessarily* informed of its own occurrence, yet without thereby becoming the object of knowledge. Awareness of consciousness is “*non-objectual*.”

Thus [Saul Kripke observes](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Naming_and_Necessity.html?id=9vvAlOBfq0kC&redir_esc=y) that while it is perfectly conceivable that we feel pain without knowing anything about the brain process that (probably) causes it, it is impossible to feel pain without it hurting, i.e. without our consciousness registering the occurrence. The relevant brain process may occur without my feeling pain (e.g. when I have been given an anaesthetic). But it is impossible for pain to occur without its hurting. Pain and the felt quality of pain are, in effect, identical, and feeling is a mode of consciousness.

As [David Chalmers puts it](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Conscious_Mind.html?id=0fZZQHOfdAAC&redir_esc=y), “pain’s feel is essential to it … You could not have something that felt like pain without it *being* pain.” The feeling of pain implies its existence. Or, in [the words of John Searle](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/The_Rediscovery_of_the_Mind.html?id=V64CnwEACAAJ&redir_esc=y): “We can’t make that sort of appearance–reality distinction for consciousness because consciousness *consists* in the appearances themselves.”

None of these philosophers would describe their view in terms of pre-reflective self-awareness. Yet that is precisely what their claims amount to. Reflection is an instance of knowledge. Knowledge is different from what is known. Modes of consciousness do not differ from their awareness. They are identical to it.

**V**

You may have noticed that at several points in my essay I have cheated a little with the terminology—or “fiddled around” with it. I could not characterize the awareness that consciousness has of itself as objectual. For that would abolish the immediacy that I am claiming and put us back in the court of the reflection model. Nevertheless, I have occasionally described the consciousness that is aware of itself as “noticing,” “being informed about itself,” or “registering the self”—and sometimes used the term “knowledge” (*Kenntnis*) in a deliberately vague or sloppy way.

All of these are object-related mental attitudes and thus instances of knowledge. Reflection is another such term. We can call all of these relationships “representational”: they distinguish what consciousness is about from consciousness itself. Yet if we assume that consciousness is pre-reflective this very distinction is ruled out. Recall Kripke’s and Chalmers’ claim that *pain and the awareness of pain are one and the same thing*. In German it is somewhat easier to draw this conceptual distinction. We differentiate between Kenntnis and Erkenntnis—in English both are rendered simply as “knowledge.” The former consists in simple awareness; the latter has a cognitive component.

We call both states of consciousness and states of knowledge mental. But not all mental states are conscious. The “switchboard of our thoughts,” as it were, operates largely outside of our awareness—which is why it can be [simulated so successfully by artificial intelligence](https://iai.tv/articles/the-dawkins-delusion-intelligence-and-language-dont-reveal-consciousness-auid-3566). Yet beyond a certain threshold of complexity we are no longer able to follow the subtle operations of such systems. Moreover, we know very well—and anyone who has ever written an article will recognize this—that our thoughts often refuse to obey our commands. Insights frequently pop up as sudden inspirations, for instance as we transition from sleep to wakefulness, without our experiencing ourselves as their authors.

Moreover, thoughts are subject to the true-false alternative. It would make no sense at all to say this of states of consciousness—of experiences. Their notorious, so-called “Cartesian certainty” consists precisely in the fact that they do not have to pass such a test.

This is related to the claim, advanced by philosophers [from Hume](https://archive.org/details/treatiseofhumann002393mbp) through [Husserl](https://archive.org/details/logischeuntersuc01hussuoft), [Sartre](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/La_transcendance_de_l_%C3%A9go.html?id=zecQ8YrHDYoC&redir_esc=y) and [Gurwitsch](https://doi.org/10.2307/2102762) and more [recent contributions](https://doi.org/10.5840/gps1999573), that the “I” is not an original inhabitant of consciousness (the “no-ownership thesis”), but that it is like an [opaque object that clouds its transparency](https://archive.org/details/ltreetlenantessa0000sart). How the “I” emerges within the stream of consciousness or in the course of phylogenesis is a complicated story, [many variations of which](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-creates-itself-out-of-nothing-auid-3424) have been told by philosophers and developmental psychologists alike. (However, this is not my topic here.)

Kant, too, held that elemental conscious experiences occur without an “I.” The ego as expressed in the proposition “I think,” he argued, is an agency of thinking—i.e. of cognition. He saw it as an inhabitant or subject or agent, not of what he termed “sensory intuitions,” but exclusively of thoughts. Indeed, he regarded it as the creator of what he called “categories,” by which he meant the basic conceptual structures that determine our rational being-in-the-world.

Freud, somewhat surprisingly, was of a similar view when he formulated the therapeutic maxim: “Where id was, there ego shall be.” He assumed that deeply seated or “repressed” emotions are concealed from consciousness. Yet this can hardly be so, as the suffering of people with mental health problems clearly demonstrates. What these emotions are concealed from is not consciousness but knowledge. It is from knowledge that they are obscured, presenting themselves instead in distorted or disguised forms that the psychotherapist is then tasked with translating into articulated knowledge. Freud should rather have said: “Hidden drive impulses that exert pressure must be brought into the light of cognition; they need—literally—to be elucidated.”

This would, of course, concede that we do in fact have deeply rooted thoughts and emotions that do not enter the light of our manifest minds. They are not occurrent, in the jargon, as feelings of pain are. Yet even these allegedly “deep” mental states or dispositions are by no means unconscious, as many believe. Rather, it is that consciousness left to itself lacks the key required to understand them and perhaps to break or weaken their dark surging pressure through interpretation. Consciousness is something event-like and passive, something that we have no control over. Many aspects of our ego-governed cognitive life and of our mental life remain hidden from it. Thus Raskolnikov, after murdering the pawnbroker, can exclaim in horror: “How could I have done that?!” For consciousness itself is egoless and inactive. This work of elucidation requires not consciousness, but rational effort. Freud called this “[a work of culture—not unlike the draining of the Zuider Zee](https://www.yorku.ca/dcarveth/Freud%20NIL%20L33%20Dissection.pdf).”

**VI**

I have come to the end of this brief introduction. Some readers would probably have wished me to be more explicit in regard to difficult details. In particular, you might have liked to know whether there is anything more specific that can be said about the enigmatic structure of non-objectual mental self-registering. Others might have liked to learn more about why “occurrent mental states” should, ipso facto, be conscious. Still others might have wanted to see a more elaborate justification of my claim that consciousness is egoless, given how closely “I”-statements are intertwined with statements about our conscious life. My analysis of the problem of the so-called unconscious is also far too superficial.

My only excuse is rather prosaic: the editors kindly asked me to explain, in an accessible manner, why I consider [consciousness to be pre-reflective](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-022-00619-z)—and this was the maximum amount of space available. I therefore refer the reader to the suggestions for further reading given in the links scattered throughout the essay.
