# Schrödinger’s blunder created the quantum measurement problem

*Quantum reality has both waves and particles*

**By Antony Valentini** | *16th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,732 words*

![Schrödinger’s blunder created the quantum measurement problem](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Schrodingers-blunder-created-the-quantum-measurement-problem.webp)

---

*Quantum mechanics is seen as challenging the idea that science provides objective descriptions of reality outside of our minds. The “measurement problem” seems to suggest that the act of observation creates the outcome that is observed. But physicist **Antony Valentini**, whose* Beyond the Quantum *was hailed by Lee Smolin as “the best book written this century” on quantum mechanics, argues that this picture rests on an enormous blunder by Erwin Schrödinger. After Louis de Broglie predicted the wave-like trajectories of particles, Schrödinger found the equation describing this. But whereas de Broglie envisaged particles riding on waves, Schrödinger kept the waves and threw away the particles. That single decision birthed the measurement problem—and it was completely unnecessary. Resurrect de Broglie’s original particle-based theory, Valentini argues, and we can dispel quantum mystery.*

**Reality and the quantum**

Across the sciences, realism reigns. Astrophysicists observe galaxies that are billions of years old; geologists study the Earth’s deep history; evolutionary biologists sequence ancient DNA from fossils. It is understood that galaxies and the Earth formed long before the rise of humans, and that now-extinct species lived and died millions of years ago. Only in the peculiar context of quantum physics do we find claims that physical reality somehow depends on the presence of human observers, even though humans did not evolve until long after the formation of galaxies, stars, and planets, and arrived quite late on the scene of life on Earth. Are such claims grounded in special evidence? Is there perhaps some remarkable laboratory phenomenon that demonstrates the power of human observers to “[create reality](https://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-and-our-part-in-creating-reality-auid-1881)”?

According to many physics textbooks, as well as popular accounts, the iconic two-slit experiment (Figure 1) demonstrates that there is [no objective reality of moving particles](https://iai.tv/video/the-substance-of-the-world). It is often argued that the appearance of a localised particle at the backstop is a random event triggered by the conscious intervention of “the observer” (causing the wave-like state to “collapse” to a particle-like state). [God plays dice](https://iai.tv/video/playing-dice-with-the-universe). Particles do not exist until they are measured. And similarly for cats, the Moon, and the wider universe.

![Schrodingers blunder Figure 1](assets/Uploads/Schrodingers-blunder-Figure-1.webp)

*Figure 1. The two-slit experiment.*

Such claims are belied by “pilot-wave theory,” first developed by French physicist Louis de Broglie in the 1920s, then revived by American physicist David Bohm in the 1950s. This is [a theory of particles in motion, guided by waves](https://iai.tv/video/tim-maudlin-in-depth-interview-quantum-theory), as illustrated in Figure 2. As a crude analogy, think of a floating bottle carried along by an ocean wave. The wave hits the barrier and passes through the slits, so two waves emerge on the far side. A single particle carried by the wave moves from source to backstop, along a definite path or trajectory, and through one hole only. Over many repetitions, the particle trajectories bunch into the supposedly inexplicable “interference pattern” predicted by quantum mechanics (Figure 3)—provided the positions of the incoming particles are distributed in a way that matches the incoming wave (technically, its squared-magnitude, the famous “Born probability rule”). In similar fashion, the theory gives the same predictions as quantum mechanics for other experiments too. Even so, the theory has been mostly swept under the rug by physicists and historians.

![Schrodingers blunder Figure 2](assets/Uploads/Schrodingers-blunder-Figure-2.webp)

*Figure 2. Pilot-wave theory for one particle.*

![Schrodingers blunder Figure 3](assets/Uploads/Schrodingers-blunder-Figure-3.webp)

*Figure 3. Pilot-wave theory for many particles (with the Born rule).*

[Arguments against realism](https://iai.tv/articles/quantum-realism-is-impossible-auid-3432), based on quantum physics, do not stand up to scrutiny. In the two-slit experiment, the usual argument against particle trajectories assumes that they would be unaffected by closing one of the holes. This is not true in pilot-wave theory, as is obvious from Figure 2 (if one hole were closed, there would no longer be two overlapping waves on the far side, drastically changing the trajectory of the particle). Despite its iconic status as a killer argument against quantum reality, the two-slit experiment has a straightforward realist explanation. And similarly with more elaborate arguments that appear to undermine realism. “Delayed-choice” experiments, in which present choices made by the observer appear to affect the past, [have straightforward explanations in terms of pilot-wave theory](https://www.nature.com/articles/315294a0). [The same is true for “Wigner’s friend” experiments](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-37535-1), which seem to show that quantum physics cannot apply to observers. [A recent high-profile “tunnelling” experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09099-4), seemingly disproving pilot-wave theory, [is flawed for elementary reasons](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10450-6). Time and again, the road from mundane experimental fact to stunning philosophical conclusion is paved with bad arguments and unjustified assumptions.

For a system of many particles, the pilot wave can be “entangled.” There is then only one wave, in a higher-dimensional space, guiding all the particles as if they were one. The particles are instantaneously connected. What happens to one can instantaneously affect the others, no matter how far apart. This “nonlocality” or “[spooky action at a distance](https://iai.tv/articles/einstein-god-and-spooky-action-at-a-distance-auid-2255)” conflicts with Einstein’s relativity, which forbids superluminal causation. Many physicists strongly object. It is as if no reality at all is preferable to a reality that conflicts with relativity. In fact, pilot-wave theory is consistent with relativity at the statistical level probed by current experiments, for which the Born rule holds. It is only at the deeper—currently inaccessible—level of individual trajectories that relativity is broken (technically, time is no longer relative). But then the objection is scientifically weak: we have no evidence that relativity must be true at a deeper level where it has never been tested.

**Ironies of quantum history**

Historical appraisals of pilot-wave theory are frequently erroneous and occasionally scientifically illogical. In 1923, de Broglie was the first to predict particle interference, which was eventually observed in 1927. And yet, for a century, such effects (Figure 1) were widely cited as evidence against the existence of particle trajectories. How can something observed in the laboratory count as evidence *against* the theory that first predicted it?

In 1926, Schrödinger found the correct equation for de Broglie’s waves, but removed the particle trajectories (for no obvious reason). Thus the notorious “[measurement problem](https://iai.tv/articles/100-years-of-quantum-mechanics-a-new-approach-is-needed-auid-3479)” was born: how can we account for observed particles if there are only waves? Ironically, later proponents of pilot-wave theory were accused of adding excess structure to quantum theory, when they were simply reinstating what should (arguably) have never been removed. Were it not for Schrödinger’s misstep, a century of confusion about the measurement problem could have been avoided.

Then there is the lingering mythology surrounding the 1927 Solvay conference, where de Broglie presented his theory. It is often said that he only had a simplified theory for one particle; in fact, he had the full theory for a system of many particles. According to one widely cited historian, his theory “[was hardly discussed at all](https://books.google.it/books/about/The_Philosophy_of_Quantum_Mechanics.html?id=X6fvAAAAMAAJ&redir_esc=y)”; in fact, it was extensively discussed, as much as the others (as is clear from the published proceedings). De Broglie is supposed to have been flummoxed by an objection from Pauli; in fact, de Broglie’s answer was essentially correct. The conference is mainly remembered for the famous but largely mythical “[Bohr-Einstein debate](https://iai.tv/articles/einstein-vs-bohr-quantum-reality-is-still-up-for-grabs-auid-3131)” about the uncertainty principle. There was no such debate, in the sense of a moderated public discussion, only semi-private conversations, whose true nature has been widely misunderstood ([Einstein’s target was not the uncertainty principle but nonlocality](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-8771-8_6)). How did major historians get it so wrong, when the conference proceedings (published in French in 1928) are available for all to see in any good university library? [A translation into English, with analysis and commentary, was published in 2009](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139194983), but the mythology lives on.

**Origins of quantum philosophy**

We may ask what motivated so many unsound arguments, as well as partisan misappraisals of basic historical facts. Some quantum physicists, notably Bohr and Heisenberg, were convinced that a new and more sophisticated age had dawned, in which physics would leave behind the objective reality of the nineteenth century. In their eyes, de Broglie and others like-minded (including Einstein) seemed hopelessly retrograde. But why? There is probably no one simple answer. However, much can be understood from the writings of the eighteenth-century German philosopher Immanuel Kant, [whose influence on Bohr and Heisenberg is well documented](https://books.google.it/books/about/Niels_Bohr_s_Philosophy_of_Physics.html?id=yDV9xf7WeG0C&redir_esc=y).

Kant strove to understand how Newton had arrived at final scientific truth (as was widely believed at the time), despite David Hume’s “problem of induction,” which suggested that such truth could never be reliably attained. How had Newton done it? Kant’s ingenious answer, developed in his 1781 *Critique of Pure Reason*, was that Newton had read the truth not from nature, but from the inherent structure of the human mind. [For Kant, human beings can understand the physical world *only* in terms of Newton’s physics.](https://iai.tv/articles/kant-vs-hume-can-we-access-reality-auid-3125) Kant argued similarly that geometry could only be Euclidean, a claim which by the early nineteenth century had been disproven. Even so, Kant’s views about Newtonian physics were taken seriously by Bohr and Heisenberg. This had a remarkable consequence. Since human beings could reason about the world only in terms of Newton’s physics, and since that physics had been found to fail at the atomic level, it followed that human beings could never understand atoms. We had reached [the ultimate limits of human knowledge](https://iai.tv/video/quantum-and-the-unknowable-universe).

Unsurprisingly, writing in the late eighteenth century, Kant did not foresee the failure of Newtonian physics in the early twentieth century, nor the development of a new theory of motion by de Broglie (just as he did not foresee the development of non-Euclidean geometry). We *can* reason about the physical world in terms of de Broglie’s non-Newtonian theory of motion, and we can use it to explain atomic phenomena in terms of objective physical processes.

Kant also tried to reconcile Newtonian determinism with an ethics grounded in free will. Again, Kant’s answer was ingenious: while we must understand the physical world by means of Newton’s laws, we should not view ourselves in the same way (as argued in his 1788 *Critique of Practical Reason*). In some sense, when acting as free moral agents, we are not part of the physical world as usually understood.

Kant’s work caused a sensation in Germany, triggering the remarkable movements known as [German idealism](https://iai.tv/articles/the-return-of-metaphysics-hegel-vs-kant-auid-2032) and German Romanticism. His new philosophy was widely regarded as having in a sense undone the Copernican revolution: humanity was once more at the centre of the universe. Some philosophers, such as Fichte, began to write as if [the human ego and its freedom were the ultimate reality](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-creates-itself-out-of-nothing-auid-3424).

Kant’s influence on Bohr and Heisenberg is well known. Less well known is Heisenberg’s admiration for Fichte’s radically subjectivist philosophy. In his 1958 book *Physics and Philosophy*, Heisenberg claimed that we had overcome the usual distinction between mind and matter, since quantum mechanics “makes the sharp separation between the world and the I impossible.” This remarkable claim inspired the Austrian high-energy physicist Fritjof Capra to write his bestselling 1975 book *The Tao of Physics*, which drew parallels between quantum physics and Eastern mysticism.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, in view of Heisenberg’s remarkable philosophical turn, in his same book of 1958 he was scathing about Bohm, who had revived de Broglie’s theory just a few years earlier. Heisenberg accused Bohm of adding an “ideological superstructure” to quantum mechanics. But, again, Bohm was arguably simply restoring what Schrödinger had (unfortunately) removed.

**The future**

Where does all this leave us? Physicists remain divided for and against realism. And quantum realists are divided mainly among three interpretations: [many worlds](https://iai.tv/articles/david-deutsch-there-is-only-one-interpretation-of-quantum-mechanics-auid-3139) (different measurement outcomes occur in parallel universes), [objective collapse](https://iai.tv/video/the-collapse-of-the-wave-function) (the wave randomly collapses to one outcome), and pilot-wave theory. The deadlock is likely to be broken only if we find experimental evidence for one interpretation, or by a breakthrough in theoretical physics that relies on one interpretation.

We have said that pilot-wave theory predicts the usual two-slit interference pattern (Figure 3), *provided* the positions of the incoming particles have a distribution matching the squared-magnitude of the incoming wave. If this “Born rule” holds at the initial time, the particle motions imply it will hold at all times, including at the backstop where the particles are observed. Hence we obtain the usual pattern. Similarly, for other kinds of experiments, we find the same predictions as quantum mechanics, if we assume the Born rule to hold at some initial time. Most workers in pilot-wave theory stop there, taking the Born rule as axiomatic, so the theory is experimentally indistinguishable from quantum theory.

[In my view, this is mistaken](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/beyond-the-quantum-9780198853749?cc=gb&lang=en). Pilot-wave theory also contains a radical extension of known physics. We may just as well consider initial particle distributions that *break* the Born rule, thereby violating quantum mechanics. For example, in the two-slit experiment, the incoming particles could be clumped only within certain regions, in which case parts of the usual interference pattern will be missing (Figure 4). The internal logic of pilot-wave theory tells us that quantum physics is only a special case of a wider physics in which the Born rule fails. This opens up a huge theoretical landscape, which we can only sketch here.

![Schrodingers blunder Figure 4](assets/Uploads/Schrodingers-blunder-Figure-4.webp)

*Figure 4. Pilot-wave theory without the Born rule.*

First, we must explain why all experiments to date have verified the Born rule (for example, the usual two-slit pattern). This has a straightforward answer. The erratically moving particles [tend to “relax” towards the Born rule](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.2004.1394), much as classical molecules bouncing around in a box tend to relax towards thermal equilibrium. The resulting state of quantum equilibrium—or “quantum death”—describes what we see in the laboratory. Thinking this through, we realise that quantum death will have already occurred during the early violence of the Big Bang. We can then understand why we now see the Born rule in our experiments. Quantum theory and the Born rule are only special cases, arising from past relaxation (Figure 5).

![Schrodingers blunder Figure 6](assets/Uploads/Schrodingers-blunder-Figure-6.webp)

*Figure 5. Relaxation to quantum death (where we are today).*

Second, we must ask where or how we could ever experience new physics beyond the Born rule. A natural place to look is in the early universe. Far enough back in time, we might see the universe in a pre-quantum state, before relaxation occurred. According to inflationary cosmology (currently the leading cosmological theory), quantum fluctuations from early times are imprinted on the cosmic microwave background (or CMB, a relic radiation from the early universe), as small variations of temperature across the sky. The observed variations have statistical properties which are usually calculated from the Born rule. [If the Born rule is violated at early times, there will be anomalies in the CMB](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.82.063513). Anomalies of the expected kinds have been reported, but they are controversial, and [data-fitting to relaxation models has yielded tantalising hints but no firm evidence](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.100.043506). More data from future satellite missions, and further modelling, may settle the matter.

Other avenues are also being pursued. Relic particles from the early universe (sought today in experiments hunting for dark matter) [could carry traces of early Born-rule violations](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.92.063531). Applying pilot-wave theory to quantum gravity suggests that [the Born rule can become unstable](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10701-022-00635-0): black-hole explosions (as theorised by Stephen Hawking) may produce a pulse of radiation that breaks the Born rule. Should such explosions be observed, we could test the resulting radiation for possible violations of quantum mechanics. The QUICK3 satellite, launched last year, is in fact already performing tests of the Born rule in space. Recent work also considers [testing the Born rule for particles created over short timescales at high-energy colliders](https://doi.org/10.1103/4llk-pt1j).

[If we discover particles that break the Born rule, a whole new physics will open up](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/beyond-the-quantum-9780198853749?cc=gb&lang=en). Circumventing the usual quantum noise associated with the Born rule, we could beat the uncertainty principle and perform measurements that are currently impossible. Entangled particles could be employed for practical superluminal signalling, and relativity as usually understood would be transcended. New technologies beyond quantum would certainly follow.

Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. It remains to be seen whether Born-rule violations will ever be definitively observed. In the meantime, regardless of whether this or another realist theory might be vindicated, there is the larger question of realism in science generally. Quantum physics has become the contemporary focal point of a perennial conflict between realists and anti-realists, which has taken diverse forms down the centuries, in both science and philosophy. [There are genuine philosophical questions concerning the relation between reality and our description of it](https://iai.tv/articles/introducing-the-realism-debate-auid-1763). But quantum theory has nothing special to teach us about such questions.

We might ask why so many quantum physicists have appealed to anti-realist arguments that fall apart under scrutiny, and why so many historians have done a disservice to a legitimate realist theory that was already proposed in its essentials in the 1920s. This is perhaps as much a question for cultural historians, and for sociologists of science, as it is for physicists and philosophers.
