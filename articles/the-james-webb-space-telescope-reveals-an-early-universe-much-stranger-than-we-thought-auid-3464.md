# The James Webb Space Telescope reveals an early universe much stranger than we thought

*New findings are stress-testing our cosmology*

**By Sandro Tacchella** | *12th January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,121 words*

![The James Webb Space Telescope reveals an early universe much stranger than we thought](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/JWT.webp)

---

*The James Webb Space Telescope's view of the infant universe has delivered genuine surprises: bright galaxies, rapid star formation, and evidence of accreting black holes only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. These findings have ignited debates about whether our best cosmological theories can survive new data. In this article, astrophysicist **Sandro Tacchella** argues the crisis lies in the simplified assumptions long used to model early galaxy and black hole growth. The tension exposed by the JWST, he suggests, is a productive one—forcing us to rethink how complexity emerged in the early universe. However, we shouldn't completely throw out our theories just yet!*

In its first four years of operation, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has transformed our view of the early universe. With unprecedented infrared sensitivity and spectroscopic capability, it allows us to observe galaxies and black holes as they were when the cosmos was a few hundred million years old. These observations have revealed unexpected richness and complexity: bright galaxies, vigorous star formation, and evidence for actively accreting black holes at times earlier than many theoretical models anticipated. But while these findings are surprising in detail, they do not necessarily indicate that cosmology itself is in crisis. Rather, JWST is exposing the limits of our [models of galaxy formation](https://iai.tv/articles/the-challenge-to-dark-matter-mond-is-wrong-auid-2676) and giving us new clues about the physics of galactic birth, growth, and the wider cosmic context in which those processes unfold.

**The foundations of modern cosmology**

Our understanding of the cosmos rests on a conceptual framework that has been extraordinarily successful at describing the large-scale evolution of the universe. General relativity links the geometry of spacetime to its energy content, and when combined with the *cosmological principle*—the assumption that the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales—it leads to a simple set of equations governing cosmic expansion. The resulting picture is the so-called ΛCDM model, where Λ represents dark energy, the mysterious driver of accelerated expansion, and CDM denotes cold dark matter, an invisible form of matter that seeds structure formation.

ΛCDM is supported by a wealth of independent observations. The detailed pattern of fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, emitted when the universe was just 380,000 years old, provides a precise snapshot of primordial density variations. Measurements of the large-scale distribution of galaxies, gravitational lensing, and the abundances of light elements produced in the early universe all align remarkably well with the ΛCDM prediction.

These successes demonstrate that the architecture of ΛCDM is sound, even if its foundations remain incomplete. ΛCDM should be understood as [a phenomenological model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01760): it encodes, with remarkable accuracy, how the universe behaves on large scales, without yet specifying the fundamental nature of dark matter or dark energy. This is not a weakness but a familiar stage in scientific progress. History offers many examples in which effective descriptions preceded deeper understanding. When the microphysics of dark matter and dark energy are eventually uncovered, ΛCDM can be upgraded into a fully physical theory rather than replaced. A true crisis would arise only if observations forced us to abandon its core predictions about cosmic expansion or structure formation; for example through a contradiction between the cosmic microwave background and the late-time universe that could not be resolved by any plausible extension of the model. That situation has not emerged.

However, tension does exist at a *quantitative* level: different methods of measuring the current expansion rate (the Hubble constant, H₀) yield values that differ at a statistically significant level. This [“Hubble tension”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01183) is a real puzzle, but it does not, on its own, imply a breakdown of the Big Bang or ΛCDM. Understanding its origin is one of the most active areas of research in cosmology today. Much of the current effort is focused on disentangling potential systematic effects in the data from the possibility that the tension signals a genuine departure from the simplest cosmological model.

**Galaxy formation: complex physics in a simple framework**

While cosmology sets the overall canvas, the emergence of structures within it, such as galaxies, stars, and black holes, involves [messy, nonlinear physics](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.06891). Gas must cool and collapse inside dark matter halos; stars form, live, and die; and feedback from stellar winds, supernovae, and accretion disks and jets around black holes can heat or eject gas. These processes operate across scales from sub-parsecs to megaparsecs, and our ability to model them is still developing.

A useful baseline is the [*constant star–formation efficiency* model](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.03299): the star formation rate in a galaxy is assumed to be proportional to the growth rate of its host dark matter halo. This simple picture captures many broad trends in the universe’s star formation history, especially when averaged over cosmic volumes. But it is an approximation—one that begins to strain under the unprecedented detail revealed by JWST.

**JWST and the first galaxies**

[JWST](https://esawebb.org/)’s near-infrared imaging and spectroscopy have opened a window into the first few hundred million years of cosmic history. Galaxies at redshifts z ≈ 10–15—meaning they existed when the universe was only about 200–500 million years old—have been [identified and studied in detail](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.18485). These systems are relatively small, yet they are surprisingly bright and efficient at forming stars relative to expectations from simpler models.

Taking just the rest-frame ultraviolet light as a proxy for star formation, many of these galaxies seem to be doubling their stellar masses on timescales much [shorter than expected](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04480). In some cases, inferred stellar masses based on simplistic assumptions were initially interpreted as [challenging standard cosmology](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12446). But deeper spectroscopic analyses have shown that assumptions about stellar populations, dust content, and the contribution of non–stellar light sources like black holes must be carefully accounted for; otherwise, inferred masses and luminosities can be significantly biased.

**Black holes in the early universe**

One of the most intriguing surprises from JWST has been evidence for actively accreting black holes in the early universe. Some compact objects at high redshift show spectral signatures that cannot be explained purely by starlight and instead indicate that gas falling into black holes may be a significant contributor to their luminosity. In at least some early galaxies, the radiation from accreting black holes (also called active galactic nuclei) may contribute substantially to their observed brightness and red colours.

A particularly intriguing manifestation of this early black hole activity is the emergence of a new class of objects in JWST images, often referred to as “[little red dots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05448).” These are extremely compact, red sources whose light is difficult to interpret using standard stellar population models. One promising [explanation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07805) is that they host black holes embedded in very dense, optically thick gas. In such environments, radiation from the accreting black hole is heavily reprocessed by the surrounding material, producing a smooth, star-like spectrum rather than the familiar signatures of classical active galactic nuclei. This has led to the notion of a “[black-hole star](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16596)”: a system in which the observed light does not come directly from stars, but from gas heated by a growing black hole and radiated in a way that mimics stellar emission. Importantly, while these objects provide compelling evidence that black hole growth is already underway at cosmic dawn, the masses of the black holes involved remain highly uncertain. Inferring black hole masses in this regime relies on assumptions about the geometry, density, and radiative properties of the surrounding gas—assumptions that are poorly constrained for such extreme, early systems. As a result, current mass estimates should be regarded as rough estimates, highlighting how much remains to be learned about black hole accretion and radiation in the young universe.

Taken together, these discoveries, including the emerging evidence for black-hole-powered systems at early cosmic times, indicate that black holes with masses of millions to tens of millions of solar masses were already growing within the first few hundred million years. While this rapid growth is striking, it does not contradict cosmology; rather, it reveals that black hole formation and accretion in the early universe are more efficient and diverse than many theoretical models had anticipated.

The presence of accreting black holes complicates how we infer galaxy properties: if a significant fraction of a galaxy’s light originates from an active galactic nucleus rather than from stars, then stellar masses derived under purely stellar assumptions will be systematically overestimated. This insight has been central to understanding why some early JWST observations initially appeared to challenge standard galaxy formation models. Far from signalling a breakdown, black holes at high redshifts offer a natural explanation for the unexpectedly red colours of some early galaxies, while also introducing feedback processes—through the heating and expulsion of gas—that likely play an important role in regulating early star formation.

**The challenge of bright early galaxies**

Despite successes like accounting for black hole contributions, one persistent finding remains: the abundance of luminous galaxies at very early cosmic times (redshift z > 10) is higher than many galaxy formation models predicted before JWST’s observations. The simple constant star-formation efficiency models mentioned above underpredict how many bright, UV-luminous galaxies we observe at such early times.

This has prompted consideration of a range of physical possibilities: star formation may have been more bursty or [efficient](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04827); the [initial mass function of stars](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06872) could differ at low metallicity; or [dust obscuration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00720) may be lower. Disentangling these possibilities is challenging because they often mimic each other’s effects observationally.

Another exciting possibility is that new physics in the very early universe might help explain both the abundance of early galaxies and the Hubble tension. In this case, the apparent discrepancies would not signal a failure of ΛCDM, but rather indicate that it is an effective, phenomenological description that may require an extension at early times, while retaining its remarkable success at later epochs. One such idea gaining attention is early dark energy.

**Early dark energy**

Dark energy, as usually conceived in ΛCDM, is a constant energy density pervading space that drives the accelerated expansion of the universe today. But some theoretical models posit that a similar form of energy may have been briefly significant in the very early universe: a hypothesis known as [early dark energy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.09032).

In this scenario, a component of energy behaves like dark energy for a short period at early times, briefly accelerating the expansion before fading away. Physicists have explored such models because they can, in principle, address two pressing cosmological puzzles. First, they can help reconcile the [Hubble tension](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04492)—the discrepancy between local measurements of the current expansion rate and values inferred from the CMB—by altering the physics of the universe’s early expansion history in a way that reduces this mismatch. Second, such an early phase could [enhance the formation of structure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15548) in the infant universe, leading to more massive dark matter halos and hence also bright galaxies at high redshift than in standard ΛCDM predictions.

In a [recent study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19427), we modelled galaxy formation in the presence of early dark energy and found that this brief extra component can make the early universe more conducive to the rapid growth of galaxies, thereby potentially explaining both the high abundance of early bright galaxies and the Hubble tension within a single unified framework. While the model does not yet have decisive empirical support, it is a valuable example of how cosmologists are exploring extensions to ΛCDM that *augment* rather than *undermine* the standard picture.

Even the strongest versions of early dark energy still operate within the context of an overall Big Bang framework; they do not imply a [qualitatively different cosmological](https://iai.tv/articles/cosmologys-crisis-challenges-scientific-realism-david-merritt-auid-2651) paradigm but rather a modification to the early energy budget that can help reconcile tensions between different observational probes.

**Data, models, and the future of cosmology**

JWST’s ongoing mission promises even more discoveries. As larger, more representative samples of early galaxies and black holes become available, and as spectroscopic characterizations expand, our understanding of these systems will sharpen. Simultaneously, efforts to resolve the Hubble tension, whether through improved observations, refined distance ladder measurements, or better theoretical models like early dark energy, will continue to drive progress in cosmology.

None of this [suggests that the Big Bang or ΛCDM is in jeopardy](https://iai.tv/articles/cosmologys-crisis-challenges-scientific-realism-david-merritt-auid-2651). Far from it: the concordance model remains the best description we have of cosmic history on large scales. The “surprises” revealed by JWST are largely at the complex intersection of astrophysics and cosmology, where the physics of stars, gas, black holes, and feedback meet the larger context of cosmic expansion and structure growth.

What JWST is demonstrating most vividly is the richness of the early universe. The discoveries of bright galaxies, black holes in infancy, and potential hints from early dark energy all underscore how much we still have to learn.
