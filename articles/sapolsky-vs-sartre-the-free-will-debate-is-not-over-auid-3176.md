# Sapolsky vs Sartre: The free will debate is not over

*HowTheLightGetsIn debates this weekend*

**By Omari Edwards** | *23rd May 2025* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,872 words*

![Sapolsky vs Sartre: The free will debate is not over](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Sapolsky-vs-Sartre-The-free-will-debate-is-not-over.webp)

*Are your thoughts truly your own, or the product of forces beyond your control? In this article, Omari Edwards, Contributing Editor of the IAI, uncovers the arguments behind on* [*the Freedom and Fate debate at this year’s HowTheLightGetsIn Hay 2025*](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/festival-passes) *ideas and music festival**, tracing arguments from neuroscience, philosophy, and psychology. From Robert Sapolsky’s determinism to Paul Bloom’s compatibilism and Lucy Allais’s Kantian dualism, he explores what’s really at stake in the free will debate: not just metaphysics, but how we live, judge, and take responsibility. Whether free will is fact or fiction, the question remains urgent. And in Hay-on-Wye, these three minds will be asked if they were ever free to answer otherwise.*

So much of the way we think about the free will debate is coloured not by science or even culture, a rare thing for any modern debate, but rather by something far more intimate: our own sense of self. “Well, but I feel free,” is the classic fallback. It comes dressed up in thought experiments, in dinner-party provocations. “How can you explain that I can raise my hand whenever I want?” The kind of trite argument reminiscent of kicking a stone to prove the materiality of the world comes to mind. But the question is serious. And the consequences of this debate for culture are more profound than we often admit.  
   
 Moral responsibility is usually the next concern for those bold enough to wade into the debate. So much of philosophy relies on the idea that we have agency, [the will to choose this or that](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/agency/), to be one kind of person rather than another. Yet science, and particularly neuroscience, increasingly calls that premise into question.  
   
 Consider the [famous experiments by Benjamin Libet](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article-abstract/106/3/623/271932), and the more recent refinements of his work. In these studies, researchers could detect a readiness potential, a specific pattern of neural activity, detectable before the subject could even consciously decide to act. In some cases, decisions could be predicted up to seven seconds in advance of a person’s conscious awareness. That is, your brain makes the choice before you do. Or more precisely, before the “you” that narrates experience steps in to claim authorship.

\_\_\_

If your neurons fire a certain way, you act a certain way. That’s it.

\_\_\_

[These studies have not gone unchallenged,](https://www.faraday.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/resources/Faraday%20Papers/Faraday%20Paper%2017%20Clarke_EN.pdf) but the broader trend in neuroscience is unambiguous. It pushes us away from the comforting myth of the autonomous self and toward an understanding of human action as fully explicable in causal terms. Enter [Robert Sapolsky](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/the-big-ideas/speakers-2/robert-sapolsky).  
   
 Sapolsky does not hedge his bets. In his view, free will is an illusion, full stop. [In his book Determined](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Determined-Life-Without-Free-Will/dp/1847925537), he argues that our actions are the consequence of an intricate web of neural firings, genetic predispositions, hormonal influences, and environmental pressures, none of which we choose. There is no ghost in the machine, no mysterious homunculus that escapes causation. Sapolsky’s position is not merely that we are influenced by these forces, but that we are entirely determined by them. And crucially, he believes that science can, in principle, resolve this question. There is no space for mystery here. If your neurons fire a certain way, you act a certain way. That’s it.

\_\_\_

Compatibilism may be logically coherent, I would say even philosophically elegant. But it’s also emotionally unsatisfying.

\_\_\_

Set against this hard determinism are three other positions, one of whose authors could not unfortunately commune\* with us to convey at [HowTheLightGetsIn](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay). Paul Bloom, the psychologist and, to those paying attention, a philosopher, offers what many consider the middle ground: a kind of compatibilism. Bloom agrees that the universe is deterministic, or at best, governed by quantum indeterminacy, which he rightly says [has little relevance for human choice](http://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-and-the-return-of-free-will-tim-andersen-auid-2475). “[So, you know, the determinists are right,”](https://www.chronicle.com/article/free-will-does-not-exist-so-what/) he has implied. But he resists the nihilism that often follows. For Bloom, moral responsibility doesn’t depend on metaphysical freedom. If my actions arise from my character, my values, my desires, even if all of those are determined, then they still reflect me. And that, he argues, is enough to ground praise, blame, and everything in between.  
   
 This is attractive: it gives us a place for moral responsibility. You may have always done the wrong thing, but [punishment still has a place to regulate your behaviour](http://iai.tv/articles/free-will-or-no-free-will-punishment-is-justified-auid-2141). I find this compelling. It allows me to accept that we can’t overcome our biology or environment but leaves room for the responsibility and outrage we feel toward those who have wronged us, or others.  
   
 The problem is that for many of us, this feels like sleight of hand. This sort of Compatibilism may be logically coherent, I would say even philosophically elegant. But it’s also [emotionally unsatisfying.](http://iai.tv/articles/moral-responsibility-without-free-will-auid-2115) It seems to say: yes, you’re just a machine, but since the machine has your name on it, we’ll call it free. It’s a satisfying myth, one designed to keep the structures of society, law, punishment, and meritocracy, intact while allowing science to keep marching forward. Perhaps a fairer society, one focused on equality, from each according to their ability, to each according to their needs, would be a better world for such a view.

\_\_\_

We are, after all, animals who think about thinking, beings who live inside the causal order and yet experience ourselves as something more.

\_\_\_

And then there is Lucy Allais, who draws on the [Kantian tradition to offer a more radical solution](https://stias.ac.za/2020/10/understanding-the-complexities-of-human-freedom-fellows-seminar-by-lucy-allais). Allais argues that the contradiction between freedom and determinism only arises if we assume that all events are part of a single, causally closed physical order. But Kant didn’t think that, and anyone who wishes (at least by me) to be taken seriously must engage with his ideas. He distinguished between [the phenomenal world, the world of appearances, where causality reigns, and the noumenal world, the domain of freedom](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/). For Kant, and for Allais, human beings occupy both domains. Insofar as we are physical beings, we are subject to deterministic laws. But insofar as we are rational agents, capable of moral deliberation, we are free.  
   
 It’s a strange duality, one that seems to deny the unity of the self. But it may also be the most honest reckoning with our divided nature. We are, after all, animals who think about thinking, beings who live inside the causal order and yet experience ourselves as something more.  
   
 This is where Sartre enters the frame, not with a solution, but with a provocation. “[Man is condemned to be free,](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/works/exist/sartre.htm)” he wrote. For Sartre, there is no essence, no underlying nature, [no deterministic anchor to who we are](http://iai.tv/articles/free-will-vs-determinism-can-you-escape-your-fate-auid-1861). We are not what happens to us, but what we make of it. And in this sense, Sartre represents the polar opposite of Sapolsky. Where Sapolsky sees neural inevitability, Sartre sees existential possibility. Where Sapolsky strips us of authorship, Sartre insists we are nothing but authors.  
   
 Yet neither view fully convinces. Sartre’s vision of absolute freedom may inspire defiance, but it also feels disconnected from the realities of trauma, coercion, addiction, the countless ways we are shaped by forces beyond our control. Sapolsky’s determinism, meanwhile, offers clarity, but at the cost of agency, dignity, and hope. I don’t think his views are misguided, in fact, they are a much-needed challenge to the lazy assumptions of everyday moralism. But the concept of free will is itself confused, one in desperate need of clarification, a clarification that will require both the analytic philosopher’s scalpel, to cut away the confusion between choice and causation, and the existentialist’s lens, to keep alive the idea that life has meaning even in the absence of metaphysical certainty.

So where does that leave us? Perhaps the most important insight is that free will, whatever it is, isn’t just a metaphysical idea. It is an existential posture, a cultural cornerstone, and a psychological necessity. It shapes how we raise children, how we love, how we judge, how we create.  
   
 And yet the cracks are showing. The rise of neuroscience, the spread of behavioural psychology, the language of “triggers” and “programming”, all of it suggests that agency is under revision. Whether that revision leads to a deeper, more compassionate understanding of the human condition, or to a new fatalism, remains to be seen.   
   
 Perhaps the deepest challenge in all this is that freedom, however we try to define it, contains its own contradictions. We say we want freedom, to choose, to act, to live on our own terms. But the experience of freedom is often inseparable from anxiety. If we are truly free, then the burden of choice is ours alone. The safety net of fate, of God, biology, upbringing, even luck, is pulled away. In this sense, determinism can be oddly comforting. If everything is caused, maybe nothing is truly our fault. But if we are free, we are to blame, not just for our wrongs, but for our failures to become what we might have been.

Perhaps the deepest challenge in all this is that freedom, however we try to define it, contains its own contradictions.

\_\_\_

At the same time, total freedom begins to look indistinguishable from chaos. If nothing determines us, not our past, not our nature, not even our rational commitments, then what anchors our decisions? What gives them weight? Sartre’s freedom can quickly turn to vertigo: a dizzying awareness that, if everything is possible, then nothing is necessary.

There is also the strange reality that freedom, in practice, often requires constraint. Rules, routines, even forms of obligation, these give shape to our lives and allow us to flourish. A jazz musician improvises freely, but only because they’ve internalised structure. A writer finds their voice not in endless possibility, but in learning to work within form. This is the paradox: freedom does not always come from removing limits, but from engaging with them meaningfully.

These tensions suggest that freedom is not a condition we either have or lack. It is not a metaphysical toggle switch. It is something we negotiate, inhabit, and interpret. And perhaps this is [why the debate remains so alive](http://iai.tv/articles/free-will-vs-determinism-can-you-escape-your-fate-auid-1861), because it speaks not just to the mechanics of mind and matter, but to the core of what it means to live a human life. The question of free will isn’t just about what’s true. It’s about how we live. And in that sense, the stakes couldn’t be higher.

Of course, these are only my thoughts, most likely predetermined before I wrote them, on a debate whose arguments and conclusions will themselves be shaped by the nature and environment in which they unfold. For a further exchange, join three of the thinkers I find most compelling as they take on the question of [Freedom and Fate in the fields of Hay-on-Wye](https://howthelightgetsin.org/festivals/hay/programme), where they’ll ask whether any of us are truly free to have done otherwise.

Uncertainty, after all, isn’t just a metaphysical puzzle, it’s embedded in our tools, our systems, and our attention. And nowhere is this more apparent than in our relationship with ourselves.
