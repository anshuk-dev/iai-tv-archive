# The self creates itself out of nothing

*Fichte and overcoming the paradox of the self*

**By Gabriel Gottlieb** | *21st November 2025* | *Read time: approx. 17 mins* | *3,342 words*

![The self creates itself out of nothing](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/The-self-creates-itself-out-of-nothing.webp)

---

*Debates about the self tend to fall into two opposing camps. Many today argue that the self is an illusion. Conversely, critics of this view argue the self is undeniable, claiming that the self is the one thing that cannot be an illusion. Here, philosopher **Gabriel Gottlieb**, drawing on the philosophy of Fichte, argues that the self posits itself into existence. In fact, the self is the very act of its own self-creation.*

It has become fashionable to think that, at a fundamental level, there is simply no “self.” The self is merely [an illusion of the brain’s activity](https://iai.tv/articles/the-demystification-of-consciousness-auid-1381), the narrative center of gravity, as [Daniel Dennett](https://iai.tv/articles/daniel-dennett-the-man-who-saw-realitys-patterns-auid-2816) has argued, or, if you are a Humean, a mere bundle of sensations, perceptions, or impressions. There is no *substance* to the self, and certainly no ghostly immaterial thinking substance, as Descartes would have it. [In the words of the contemporary German philosopher, Thomas Metzinger, “we are processes.”](https://deconstructingyourself.com/what-is-the-self-metzinger.html) According to Metzinger, “there is no metaphysical entity such as the self which could exist independently of the brain.” I think Metzinger is right about a key point.

The “self” is a process, or, as I would rather put it, an *activity*. This, at least, is how another German philosopher, [Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762-1814)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/johann-fichte/), thinks of the self. Fichte, like both Hume and Metzinger, denies that the self is some kind of metaphysical entity, a substance of some variety. “In order not to suggest the idea of a substratum,” Fichte once wrote, “I do not even want to call the I an *acting something*.” To be a self is just to *do* “selfy” things, to engage in selfy activities, if I can put it this way. In contrast to Metzinger’s view that the self is illusory, Fichte held that there is, nevertheless, one essential selfy activity that makes one a self, or “an I,” to use Fichte’s preferred terminology. What is that essential activity? Fichte calls it “self-positing.”

Johann Gottlieb Fichte was posited by his pious parents, Christian and Maria, in 1762. Born in the small town of Rammenau, Germany, just east of Dresden, he came from humble origins. With the support of a noble patron who recognized his prodigious mind at the young age of nine, Fichte eventually attained an elite education. Like many young educated men at the time, Fichte first served as a tutor to the children of wealthy families. During the summer of 1790, while working as a tutor, he began a systematic reading of Immanuel Kant’s philosophical works. He was particularly moved by Kant’s *Critique of Practical Reason*, a work that outlines the role and limits of our reasoning about morality and freedom.Fichte’s reading of Kant was a transformative experience that led him to reject his previously held dogmatic views about the impossibility of human freedom. Kant, for Fichte, had provided an entirely new way to think about the freedom of the self, both in moral and theoretical terms.

\_\_\_

The principle Fichte arrived at is the principle of the self-positing I: “The I originally posits its own being purely and simply.”

\_\_\_

In his magnum opus, *The Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant argues that our knowledge depends on 1) our capacity to receive a “manifold” of sensations through the faculty of sensibility, 2) our capacity to order that manifold of sensations as in space and time, and 3) by exercising universal and necessary concepts that give a unity and order (e.g., a causal order) to the manifold. Kant referred to this view as transcendental idealism:  the doctrine that we only know things as they appear to us, not as they are in themselves, and that space and time do not consist in the properties of things or relation of things to each other independently of our minds, our of our capacity to intuit objects; [rather, space and time are the forms our sensible intuitions of objects.](https://iai.tv/articles/kant-vs-hume-can-we-access-reality-auid-3125) It is in virtue of the exercise of these capacities and concepts, what Kant refers to as categories, that we are capable of having the kind of experience we happen to have, that is, an experience of a causally ordered world of unified and stable objects. No doubt these are complicated matters, and debate about how we should understand Kant’s transcendental idealism [rages on](https://iai.tv/articles/the-world-is-both-subjective-and-real-paul-franks-auid-2789).

Nevertheless, with regard to the self, or I, Kant additionally argues that if I am able to think about the objects of my experience, if they are to mean something to me, to be the kinds of things I might reason or deliberate about, then my experiences of the objects, my representations of them, [must be representations that I am capable of being conscious of as my representations.](https://iai.tv/articles/the-self-exists-and-it-is-an-illusion-auid-3347) The way he puts this is by saying “the I think must be able to accompany all my representations.” In brief, Kant’s point is that awareness of the representations as my representations is required for them to play a role in my experience and knowledge of the world.

Kant’s views were immediately controversial (they remain so today), and they initially launched a series of objections and defenses. Fichte eventually emerged not only as one of Kant’s great defenders, but as one of the most innovative philosophers working within Kant’s wake, even though Kant eventually distanced his philosophy from Fichte’s in a damning open letter from 1799.

Fichte became particularly interested in the foundations of Kant’s philosophical system. He was especially invested in showing that Kant’s philosophy could be based on an *a priori* first principle, a self-evident principle that relied on absolutely no considerations drawn from experience. He also wanted this principle to rely on no presuppositions, not even the principles of logic. The principle Fichte arrived at is the principle of the self-positing I: “The I originally posits its own being purely and simply.” From this first principle, Fichte hypothesized that he could erect the Kantian philosophical system, including sensibility, the manifold, time, space, the universal and necessary concepts required for experience, as well as the principles of morality. An ambitious philosophical project, to say the least.

I am particularly interested in Fichte’s conception of the I as self-positing, but not because of its role as a first principle in his system. What I think is more philosophically interesting are his views about the self as an activity and whether his views have anything to offer us as we continue to investigate the nature of the self today. The key point I take Fichte to be making in his considerations of the I as an activity is that the essential activity of being a self consists in a peculiar act of constituting itself as an I, or self. To be a self is to be a self-constituting activity.

Consider this basic point. To be a self is to be aware of oneself as oneself. A “self” that has no relation to itself is not a self, yet this self-relation does not seem sufficient for being a self. It is important to stress the ‘as’ in the formulation “aware of oneself *as* oneself.” In other words, the nature of the relation to oneself involved in being a self, or in being a selfy activity, *matters*.

\_\_\_

As Fichte sees it, the activity (subject) is directed toward itself (as object) and it is in virtue of this dual activity that the I constitutes itself as an I, or is a self-reverting activity.

\_\_\_

To see this point, I’ll provide a scenario inspired by the philosopher John Perry. Imagine from a distance that you notice a car in the parking lot has a flat tire and you think, 1) “That poor guy has a flat tire.” But, as you come closer to the car you realize, 2) “Wait, I have a flat tire.” Of course, what you’ve realized is that the car with the flat tire is your car. There are two distinct types of self-relations at work here. In 1), I am related to the “poor guy” (myself) as a discrete object, and in this relation I in no way identify the object with myself. We might call this an irreflexive self-relation since there is a self-relation but the self-relation does not include a reference back to oneself *as* oneself; instead, it is a relation toward an object that just happens to be oneself. In 2), the self-relation includes an identification—one identifies oneself as the one with the flat tire—and so the self-relation does refer back to oneself, which means it is a reflexive self-relation. The point of this example is to illustrate the difference between an irreflexive self-relation (a relation to oneself) and a reflexive self-relation (a relation to oneself *as* oneself). For Fichte, to be a selfy activity entails that one relates to oneself as oneself, or reflexively.

The first point I want to make about the self-positing of the I is that Fichte views it as consisting in a reflexive self-relation. To capture this aspect of the self, Fichte sometimes refers to the I as a self-reverting activity, or an activity that returns back into itself.

The second point I want to make about self-positing is that Fichte holds that the reflexive self-relation is a constituting activity; it produces something, brings something into being. But what, exactly, does it produce, or constitute? Fichte holds that it doesn’t produce or constitute a subject, but a subject-object.  Fichte’s claim is that the reflexive self-relation constitutes itself as a subject-object. The one passage in all of his work that I think best expresses this idea is found in *Foundations of the Entire Wissenschaftslehre* (1794-95), where the term *Wissenschaftslehre* could be translated as the “Doctrine of Knowledge.” Fichte writes:

The I posits itself, and, by virtue of this mere positing through itself, it is; and conversely, by virtue of its mere being, the I is, and it posits its being. — The I is at the same time the acting subject and the product of this action, what is active and what is brought about by means of this activity. Action [*Handlung*] and deed [*Tat*] are one and the same, and this is why [the proposition] “I am” expresses a *Tathandlung*.

The I, according to Fichte, is a *Tathandlung*, an activity that through its own activity constitutes itself. There is no “doer” behind the activity, as Nietzsche once put it, no metaphysical substance or entity that expresses itself in the activity. Instead, the I has the peculiar status of being both the activity and the result of its activity at one and the same time. This is what the German is meant to convey: action (*Handlung*) and deed *(Tat)*, the product of acting, are one and the same.

It is a challenge to capture just what kind of selfy “thing” is meant by this description, but in some works, Fichte refers to the I as a subject-object, neither subject nor object, but subject-object, the identity of subject and object. A subject-object is not a metaphysical entity, but a self-reflexive activity.

It is natural to wonder why Fichte thinks we need such novel way of looking at the I, subject, or self. Can’t the I simply be a subject? The main reason Fichte rejects this suggestion has to do with the nature of the I’s activity: the activity of the I is directed towards itself, not simply toward an object separate from itself. In being directed toward itself, the activity is relating to itself as an object. To refer to the I as merely a subject would make it difficult to appreciate that an aspect of its self-relation involves an awareness of itself as an object.

If this is a satisfying response, one might then suggest that the I, since it is directed toward itself as object, must just be an object of some sort. Fichte clearly rejects this idea as well.  While it is true that the activity of the I is directed toward itself as an object, it is not the case that the activity lacks a subjective aspect. The subjective aspect is subjective since it involves an awareness of oneself.

As Fichte sees it, the activity (subject) is directed toward itself (as object) and it is in virtue of this dual activity that the I constitutes itself as an I, or is a self-reverting activity. For Fichte, whatever activity is enacted in precisely this manner is a self-constituting activity, or what he calls a self-positing activity. To say that the I self-posits means that it is a self-constituting, self-reflexive activity.

So, [Fichte holds that the self is not a metaphysical entity](https://iai.tv/video/the-divided-self-sam-harris-roger-penrose), but the activity involved in being a “subject-object.” Since the self is an activity for Fichte, it is not entirely right to say that it is an illusion, as Metzinger claims. Something can fail to be a metaphysical entity or thing, but in virtue of being an activity or process it is, in some fundamental sense, real (and real beyond a phenomenal sense of self). In his writings and lectures about the I, Fichte does, however, refer to the I as an idea, and one might think that this entails that the self-positing activity of the I is illusory.

However, this is not what Fichte has in mind. He has in mind something very specific when he refers to the I as an idea. By an idea, he does not mean the self-constituting activity of the I as a subject-object is illusory, made up, or imagined. Instead, he believes that in order to explain how it is possible for us to be rational, deliberative beings, we must posit that the I is a self-positing activity, a reflexive, self-reverting activity, or a subject-object. He agrees with Hume that we cannot have a sensible impression of such an I, but the I is *explanatorily ineliminable*. Because the I is explanatorily ineliminable he considers it a necessary posit, a necessary idea. Its necessity is what licenses Fichte to consider the activity of the I as not simply illusory, but, in some fundamental sense, real.

\_\_\_

The two activities are not two discrete parts of a subsystem, but two modalities of a single activity that together constitute the I as subject-object.

\_\_\_

It is worth noting that in [*Being No One* (MIT 2003)](https://www.amazon.com/Being-No-One-Self-Model-Subjectivity/dp/0262633086), Metzinger recognizes similarities between his view of the self and Fichte’s and, for this reason, he deems it necessary to distance his own account from Fichte’s self-positing model. As for Fichte’s characterization of the self as a “subject-object,” Metzinger writes that such a “beautiful but too metaphorical way of speaking is dangerous.” I actually don’t think it’s too beautiful, too metaphorical, or too dangerous. If we think of the I as a system, an aspect of the system is aware of another aspect of the system, but the two aspects of the system are said to be one and the same, or identical. We refer to the first aspect as the subjective aspect which is aware of itself (the objective aspect). The way Fichte sometimes puts this is to break the two aspects into two types of activities, real activity and ideal activity. The real activity (object) might be an activity aimed at accomplishing a cognitive goal; the ideal activity (subject) is the activity aware of the real activity. The I as subject-object is to be an activity that consists in these two aspects. One posits oneself as an I if and only if one’s activity takes on the form of being a subject-object activity.

Metzinger, however, is not satisfied. In fact, he takes issue with how Fichte (and others) frame the reflexive self-relation as consisting in an identity. Metzinger writes: “The cardinal problem for classical models of ‘reflexive’ self-consciousness, for example, in Fichte, was the problem of the identity of the subject versus its epistemicity: How could something that was strictly identical to itself be separated into a knowledge relation?” This does, indeed, seem to be a problem. The thought here seems to be that a “knowledge relation,” to use his phrase, consists in a subject (the knower) having a relationship to an object (the known). That seems like a perfectly fine assumption. Metzinger also holds that in every knowledge relation the knower and the known are, in some sense, distinct and not strictly identical. For instance, if the self is a simple, but a knowledge relation requires a separation between knower (subject) and known (object), then a simple, something incapable of being divided, cannot make the requisite separation within itself to have a knowledge relation to itself.

Metzinger, however, introduces an assumption about reflexive models of self-consciousness that allows him to reject them on the grounds that they cannot allow for the separation required for a knowledge relation. The key assumption is that all such views involve a reflexive relation in which identity means strict identity. If there is something wrong about this assumption, then Metzinger’s objection will have lost much of its appeal. It is worth noting in brief that Metzinger resolves this problem in his own model of the “self” by suggesting that two parts of the system have a mereological relation where one is related to the other in a unidirectional, intransitive manner. There’s no need for a reflexive self-relation, though other issues might arise for Metzinger. For instance, one might think the “mineness” of one’s states is possible if and only if a reflexive self-relation accompanies that state.

As for the assumption about “strict identity,” I do not think that this assumption correctly characterizes how Fichte thinks of the selfy activity of the I when he considers its self-reflexive relation in terms of a subject-object. The identity between subject and object such that they are a subject-object should be viewed in terms of the reciprocal relationship between two aspects of the selfy activity. Fichte gives expression to this thought when he writes of these activities as conditioning and determining one another. The first aspect is the aspect of a real activity in which the activity is directed outside itself, toward the representation of an object or the realization of an action. The second aspect is the ideal aspect directed at the real activity. The ideal activity consists in an intuitive awareness of the real activity. It is in virtue of the ideal activity that the subject-object activity is a self-reverting activity. No real activity, no ideal activity; no ideal activity, no real activity. The two activities are not two discrete parts of a subsystem, but two modalities of a single activity that together constitute the I as subject-object. Perhaps Metzinger’s model is not so far from Fichte’s when one appreciates that the knowledge relationship involved in Fichte’s model of the reflexive self-relation of the subject-object does not assume a strict identity, but only a reciprocal relationship in which the two activities constitute the core activity of the I as subject-object.

Let me attempt to bring this all back to the key idea: Fichte does not think of the self, or I, as a metaphysical substance, object, or entity. Instead, like Metzinger, Fichte considers the self to be a process or activity. However, Fichte holds that the activity of the I must be a self-positing activity, one in which the I, in virtue of consisting in two discernible activities, a real activity and an ideal activity, constitutes itself as an I. This model of the self considers the self to involve a reflexive self-relation in which the I posits itself as an I. Fichte is not claiming that the self or I is illusory, but that a necessary condition of explaining how it is possible for rational beings like us to have the kind of self-conscious experiences we do have, we must come to see that this conception of the self or I is a required and fundamental posit we must make in our explanation. On Fichte’s view, the self-positing activity of the I is ineliminable.

While there is much more one might say in defense of Fichte’s model, particularly with regard to some of its apparently paradoxical elements, by comparing Fichte’s claim that the self is a self-positing activity to the contemporary work of Thomas Metzinger, I hope to have shown that even if Fichte’s views sound wild, they might still be a live option.
