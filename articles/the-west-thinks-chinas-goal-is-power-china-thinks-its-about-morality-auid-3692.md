# The West thinks China's goal is power, China thinks it's about morality

*Chinese hegemony would look nothing like America's*

**By Astrid Nordin** | *2nd October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,063 words*

![The West thinks China's goal is power, China thinks it's about morality](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-West-thinks-Chinas-goal-is-power-China-thinks-its-about-morality.webp)

*President Xi Jinping has repeatedly said China will "never seek hegemony, expansion, or a sphere of influence." Nonetheless, Western academics like John Mearsheimer continue to argue China will seek to become a regional hegemon. Chinese International Relations Professor **Astrid Nordin** argues that in fact, both are true. When China says it rejects hegemony, it means the illegitimate form of bullying and coercive power it associates with the US. It’s not rejecting a hierarchical world order with China at the top, moral and competent, rewarding those who follow, and disciplining those who don’t.*

When Chinese President Xi Jinping met US President Donald Trump on his visit to the US last week, he spoke of the two countries’ historic responsibility for human progress and their shared responsibility for addressing global challenges. Few familiar with China will have been surprised by this rhetoric, which drew on his longstanding insistence that “[no matter how developed it could become, China will never seek hegemony or engage in expansion.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/zy/jj/2015zt/xjpdmgjxgsfwbcxlhgcl70znxlfh/202406/t20240606_11381543.html)”

Xi’s repeated assertions that China will never seek hegemony push back against the longstanding proposition in much Anglophone academic and political debate that China is bound to do exactly that: seek to replace the US as global, or at least regional, hegemon. One doesn’t have to look far to find explicit remarks to this effect. [Academic superstar John Mearsheimer](https://iai.tv/video/mearshimer-and-the-death-of-ideology)’s “offensive realism” famously dictates that “[China is likely to imitate the US and attempt to become a regional hegemon](https://www.mearsheimer.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/The-Australian-November-18-2005.pdf),” out of structural necessity. Former Biden-official-cum-scholar Rush Doshi has drawn on Chinese strategic rhetoric to make a similar argument: [If there are two paths to hegemony—a regional one and a global one—China is now pursuing both](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-long-game-chinas-grand-strategy-to-displace-american-order/).

There appear, then, to be two directly contradictory claims in circulation. China will seek hegemony. China will never seek hegemony. Which claim is right? A closer look at the understandings of hegemony on which these interlocutors draw suggests a different question: when Xi rejects hegemony, is he necessarily rejecting the kind of international position that scholars like Doshi and Mearsheimer call hegemonic?

**What does it mean to reject hegemony?**

To Mearsheimer and his “defensive realist” followers, hegemony means domination of a regional or global system through overwhelming relative power. To Doshi, it denotes a hierarchical order in which the pre-eminent state secures others’ deference through some combination of coercion, inducement, and legitimacy. If to “never seek hegemony” means never seeking to be significantly more powerful than your neighbors, or never seeking others’ deference, Xi’s statement would be an extraordinarily restrictive promise. However, neither [official PRC rhetoric](https://iai.tv/video/make-china-great-again-steve-tsang-on-xi-jinping) nor important Chinese theories of international order necessarily understand anti-hegemony that way.

In Chinese political rhetoric, hegemony is commonly rendered as *baquan* (霸权), and hegemonism as *baquan zhuyi* (霸权主义), [a term with deep Maoist roots](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315724300-18/hegemony-chinese-astrid-nordin) that generally identifies an illegitimate form of bullying and coercive power. It is often used to describe US hegemony specifically, and contrasted with a Chinese and more harmonious form of leadership. The upshot is that a Chinese statement that “China will never seek hegemony” is not necessarily a statement that China will never seek to build a hierarchical order with itself at the top, or to induce others’ deference with both carrots and sticks. It may instead be saying that China will never pursue pre-eminence in ways that it considers unnecessarily aggressive or despotic. This is not simply a question of semantics or mistranslation. The question is not whether China seeks superior power, but what kinds of power it considers legitimate, and on whose behalf.

A key interlocutor who has developed this distinction is the prominent Tsinghua international relations scholar Yan Xuetong, who has long advocated what he terms “moral realism.” In an article revisiting the formulation of this theory earlier this year, [Yan distinguishes leadership according to two dimensions: morality and competence](https://academic.oup.com/cjip/article/19/2/194/8687181).

Yan measures competence by the ability to achieve political objectives through diplomacy rather than military coercion. He then distinguishes two ideal types of competent leadership, one moral and the other immoral. He considers decision-makers immoral when they pursue policies that both harm national interests and violate international norms, often privileging their personal or regime interests instead. This diplomatically competent but immoral leadership is what Yan calls “hegemony.” Its much rarer competent and moral counterpart is what he calls “humane authority.” His latest formulation defines moral leadership relatively permissively, as pursuing national interests or international norms, ideally both. Both forms of leadership are powerful and commanding. What distinguishes them is morality, rather than the extent of their power over others.

For Yan, these forms of leadership produce different kinds of international order. Whereas hegemony produces a “big stick” order, in which the hegemon uses its superior military power to compel compliance without respecting others’ sovereignty, a dominant state that leads through humane authority produces a “norms-adoption” order. Crucially, however, Yan’s alternative to hegemony is strikingly hierarchical. Humane authority establishes norms and encourages others to adopt them through its own example, but also possesses “superior comprehensive power” that enables it to reinforce, and perhaps enforce, its norms through “a series of rewards and punishments.” Granted, moral realism does something different from Mearsheimerian offensive realism, in that it prizes the ability to lead by exemplary moral conduct and inspire followership. However, it is by no means a toothless form of leadership; attraction and admiration are combined with material asymmetry and disciplinary capacity, to be deployed when the leader deems it necessary.

If we look, as Yan has often done, to pre-Westernized Chinese traditions, we find an alternative way of approaching this distinction in thinking through the qualitative contrasts of *yin-yang*—which readers may recognize in the *Taiji* diagram where a circle consists of a dark and a light half, with a dark dot in the light, and a light dot in the dark. In this tradition, opposites are understood as relational and interdependent, rather than mutually exclusive; each contains the potential for transformation into the other. Attraction does not preclude coercion; hierarchy does not preclude restraint; the exercise of superior power may be both welcomed and resisted by those subject to it. Rather than placing hegemony and humane authority in entirely separate boxes, we might consider how elements associated with each coexist within particular forms of leadership, and how the balance between them changes. This is not quite Yan’s argument, which distinguishes between types of leadership according to morality and competence. Instead, it raises a question that his classification leaves open: who gets to determine when the exercise of superior power has ceased to be humane?

**Whose hegemony?**

Attention to Yan’s theorization shows that common American understandings of hegemony overlap significantly with the alternative he advocates. Chinese leaders might seek hegemony in the sense that Mearsheimer or Doshi mean it, while rejecting it in Yan’s immoral sense.

Of course, Yan is not alone in distinguishing between different forms of hierarchical leadership. [Darren Lim and G. John Ikenberry take this argument directly to China](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09636412.2023.2178963), noting the Chinese leadership’s repeated insistence that it will never pursue hegemony, but nevertheless theorizing what a China-led hegemonic order might look like. Like American hegemony, they argue, it would depend on more than coercion: China would need to attract other states through bargains, partnerships, and material benefits. Its organizing principles might nevertheless differ from those of the American liberal order, prioritizing sovereign autonomy, economic development, and relatively informal partnerships. They suggest that such an order could acquire illiberal characteristics, particularly where bilateral negotiations give considerable weight to China’s superior power.

In a published response, [Amy King challenges Lim and Ikenberry’s, and by extension Yan’s, tendency to derive the character of international order from the dominant state’s own preferences](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09636412.2023.2253148). International orders, she argues, have collective foundations: weaker states participate in negotiating, contesting, and reshaping them. Nor should pluralism be treated as the exclusive preserve of a US-led “liberal international order”—an order, one might add, that many struggle to see as either liberal or orderly. China’s emphasis on sovereignty and development already resonates with ideas shared across much of the developing world. Read alongside Yan, King’s argument raises a further question: can [the morality of international leadership](https://iai.tv/video/a-world-without-morality) be established by reference to the leading state’s own conduct and intentions, or must those subject to its authority also have a say in what constitutes legitimate leadership?

Sovereignty is particularly important here. Respect for other states’ sovereignty is central to Yan’s distinction between “big stick” and “norms-adoption” orders, and equally prominent in Xi’s articulation of legitimate international leadership. It offers weaker states protection against the interference of stronger ones, but is not a politically neutral criterion. A conception of equality centred on recognized sovereign states conveniently marginalizes competing claims to self-determination, including by groups in Taiwan, Tibet, and Xinjiang, whose interests and political aspirations differ from those of the Chinese Party-State. And of course, respect for sovereignty alone does not settle how much influence less powerful states have over the rules they are expected to follow.

In practice, Xi’s successive Global Initiatives, covering Development (2021), Security (2022), Civilization (2023), and Governance (2025), progressively extend China’s proposed principles for international order into new domains, while retaining rhetorical opposition to hegemonism and a strong emphasis on sovereign equality. The [Global Security Initiative](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/zy/gb/202405/t20240531_11367484.html) explicitly rejects hegemonism and power politics, while the [Global Civilization Initiative](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/wjb/zzjg_663340/xws_665282/xgxw_665284/202303/t20230317_11043656.html) rejects the proposition that becoming powerful necessarily entails seeking hegemony (*guoqiang biba*, 国强必霸). Most recently, the [Global Governance Initiative](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/xw/wjbxw/202509/t20250901_11699912.html), launched in September 2025, proposes five principles: sovereign equality, international rule of law, multilateralism, a people-centred approach, and practical results. Its concept paper calls for greater representation and decision-making power for developing countries. This increasingly comprehensive agenda for international order is presented precisely as an alternative to hegemonism and rules imposed by a small number of powerful states, whilst marginalizing non-sovereign actors.

The Party-state is thus becoming more ambitious about China’s role in shaping international order while remaining emphatic that this project is anti-hegemonic. The question is ultimately not simply whether China will exercise unequal power, but who gets to decide when leadership, hierarchy, attraction, norm-setting, reward, and punishment have crossed the line into immoral domination, and therefore into “hegemony” in Yan’s sense of the term.

**Who decides what counts as hegemony?**

China will seek hegemony. China will never seek hegemony. Both propositions can be true, insofar as they draw on different understandings of what hegemony entails. This is not simply a linguistic distinction, nor is it a reason to accept Chinese claims to moral leadership at face value. Hegemony is not merely a description of a particular distribution of power, but also a judgement about the legitimacy of how that power is exercised.

A *yin-yang* reading of this apparent contradiction suggests something further. Apparently opposing tendencies do not merely coexist; they are mutually constitutive, each taking shape through its relationship with the other. Attraction and coercion, hierarchy and equality, power and restraint are not necessarily separate features of international order. The exercise of superior power can generate both willing followership and resistance, and what is experienced as legitimate leadership by some may simultaneously constitute domination for others.

The emphasis on sovereignty in China's proposed order brings home the high stakes involved. Sovereign equality offers an important protection against the domination of weaker states by stronger ones. Yet a vision of equality centred on sovereign states also privileges governments as the legitimate representatives of their populations. It leaves considerably less room for solidarity with peoples whose claims challenge those governments, including those denied recognition as legitimate political actors in their own right. The question is not simply whether a Chinese-led order would be more or less coercive than an American-led one, but whose claims to equality, security, and political agency would be recognized within it.

If moral leadership is defined principally by the intentions and conduct of the leading power and the individuals in charge of it, [there is a danger that morality becomes no more than a judgement the powerful make about themselves](https://academic.oup.com/cjip/article/4/4/349/353513)—and as we know, few leaders call their own actions immoral. Yet [international order is not constituted by its leader alone](https://iai.tv/articles/francis-fukuyama-warns-trump-is-not-a-realist-auid-3128). Its legitimacy also depends on the participation of those who live within it, including their ability to disagree with the rules and challenge the authority of those who set them.

A hegemon and a humane authority might, after all, look rather similar from below, particularly when those subject to their authority disagree with the rules they are expected to follow.
