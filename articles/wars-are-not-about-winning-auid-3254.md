# Wars are not about winning

*The game theory behind the new world order*

**By Andy Owen** | *11th July 2025* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *2,941 words*

![Wars are not about winning](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Wars-are-not-about-winning.webp)

---

*In war, it is often said, there are no winners. But, in fact, wars are often not even about winning and losing at all. They are about reaching an equilibrium. Author and former intelligence officer **Andy Owen** argues that what has changed in the world today – in part due to the chaos of Trump's second term, but also due to events well preceding him – is that the world has been thrown out of this equilibrium. And if geopolitics is indeed a kind of game, the referee – the US – and the rules of the game – international law – have come unstuck. The result is a dangerous free for all, where might equals right. Owen argues, in order to survive in such a world, smaller nations need to build coalitions to have any chance of repelling the power of larger nations – and they need to be smart in playing the game.*

Playing games is a universal part of human experience. The British Museum has a board from the third millennium BC, from the Mesopotamian strategy board game The Royal Game of Ur. There are many parts of our experience that we can easily recognise but become slippery when we are asked to define them. [Games are one of these](http://iai.tv/video/the-game-of-life). Philosopher [Ludwig Wittgenstein](http://iai.tv/articles/wittgenstein-vs-wittgenstein-lee-braver-auid-2615) believed the elements of games, such as play, rules, and competition, all fail to adequately define what games are. He concluded that people apply “game” to a range of disparate human activities that only share “family resemblances”. Some of those disparate human activities are far from play. Most however can be analysed through Game theory. When applied to our currently fragmenting geopolitical system, and the most fractious relationships within it, game theory can provide insight into what moves key players may make next, and what state the board may be left in.

[Game theory](http://iai.tv/articles/war-is-not-a-game-but-we-should-play-it-like-one-frank-zagare-auid-2448) analyzes strategic decision-making. It assumes that participants are rational actors and will act to maximize their own outcomes. It is a framework for analyzing situations where multiple decision-makers interact, and the outcome for each depends on the choices of all others. It can be applied to the simple board games like the Royal Game of Ur and the most complex of human activities. I used a form of game theory as a soldier, without realising. Patrolling through Basra in 2006 we were engaged in a more deadly Mesopotamian game. Local improvised explosive device (IED) teams would disguise their lethal projectiles in piles of rubbish or dig them into the side of the road on routes they thought troops would take. Despite my team gathering intelligence on the IED teams, including sometimes the locations of the IEDs themselves, and the training we received on how to spot signs of their presence, it mostly felt like we were playing a game of chance rather than a game of skill. Every decision about which turn to take was a play in the game that could decide who was the winner and who was the loser.

In the early days of the war in Iraq, IEDs were constructed out of old artillery shells. By 2006 though, the IEDs we feared were constructed out of a shaped charge that used a concave liner to create a superplastic metal projectile which could penetrate our strongest armour. These were known as EFPs (Explosively Formed Penetrators) and were detonated by PIR (Passive Infrared) devices which detected a vehicle’s heat signature, triggering the detonation. A cat and mouse game within the wider game developed. US soldiers started to strap empty ammunition boxes with warm liquid to a pole projecting from a patrol’s lead vehicle. The box would trigger the blast but in front of the patrol. The IED teams then started to angle the bombs so they would still hit the patrol.

These PIR EFPs were supplied by Iran. Iran established itself as the dominant Shia regional power out of the chaos of the Iraq invasion, filling the void left by the dismantling of Saddam Hussien’s Baathist regime with militias that took their orders from Tehran. Over the last few decades, Iran and Sunni Saudi Arabia have used the sectarian divide in the region to further their ambitions and act as fragile regional counter-balances to each other. The foundations of that order are now being shaken in the wars Israel has been waging in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran. US president [Donald Trump](http://iai.tv/articles/francis-fukuyama-warns-trump-is-not-a-realist-auid-3128) has described the [current ceasefire between Iran](http://iai.tv/articles/iran-is-the-enemy-the-west-created-auid-3149) and Israel as “complete and total.” Game theory would suggest it is likely to be fragile and temporary.

It is becoming harder to predict the actions of the key players in the game, the rules of the game are changing, and the referee has left the field of play.

\_\_\_

The US and Israel do not know with any certainty the status of Iran’s official stock of 400kg of highly enriched uranium (60% purity). Iran could still use this to relatively quickly make enough weapons-grade fissile material (usually 90% purity) for ten nuclear bombs, if it has active centrifuges. The head of Iran’s Atomic Energy Organization, claims that Iran has another secret, hardened enrichment facility. Israel can kill more scientists, but you cannot bomb knowledge. Nuclear bombs are based on 80-year-old technology. At the same time, Israel has proved to itself that it can launch an attack on Iran and manage the consequences. It will now continue to do so every time it believes Iran is close to building a nuclear weapon. The Iranian regime, who claimed they were not trying to build a bomb when Israel attacked this time, may conclude that doing so is the only strategy that can prevent future attacks. It will also have noted the recent performance of Chinese air defence systems used by Pakistan against India bombers, and will likely look to deepen its military relationship with China. The Israeli strikes have resulted in both sides now seeing the benefit of new strategies that increase the risk of regional instability.

In game theory there is the concept of [the Nash Equilibrium](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0308738101), named after mathematician John Nash. This is the state where, having analyzed what each other player does after they have taken into account what they expect the others to do, none of the players can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their strategy. The Nash Equilibrium creates a stable system as long as one's choices are consistent. When reached, no player will change their strategy given their assumption about the strategies the others will continue to pursue. The conflict between Iran and Israel cannot be said to be in such an equilibrium.

There are factors outside of the changing strategies in Tel Aviv and Tehran, that have contributed to this situation. The new unpredictability of America’s foreign policy, the reticence by Trump to enforce international laws and norms in place since the end of the Second World War, and the eroding of these laws and norms that began before Trump, are together creating global instability. It is becoming harder to predict the actions of the key players in the game, the rules of the game are changing, and the referee has left the field of play. When the laws of the game are clearly understood and an effective law-enforcing power exists, the balance of power is measured in terms other than force. When it does not, the ability to use force becomes the only measure that matters. “Might is right” used to be how the system worked, and is becoming the case again.

Before the twentieth century, in the absence of global mechanisms to adjudicate international conflicts, states primarily asserted their rights or resolved disputes by going to war. This was the international system that led to the colonisation of most of the planet, where strong states invaded and occupied weaker states, weaving them into a global economic system on unfavourable terms. Competition between these colonial empires culminated in the First World War. Many current territorial disputes are still the legacy of straight lines drawn by colonial cartographers.

The rules of war

After the industrialized carnage of the First World War, US Secretary of State Frank Kellogg and French Prime Minister Aristide Briand put forward the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928 which looked to establish the principle that aggressive war was illegal. Fifty-eight signatories (representing the majority of the then most powerful states) agreed to “condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies, and renounce it, as an instrument of national policy.” While the pact did not stop the Second World War it set in motion a process that led to today’s international order.

In 1945, the UN Charter prohibited states from using force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any other state and made leaders criminally accountable for launching aggressive wars. The first count in the indictment at the Nuremberg war crime trials, charged the Nazi hierarchy with initiating “aggressive war” in violation “of the terms of the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928.” States went on to design further frameworks and institutions, leading to the establishment of an order that elevated economic tools over military might to ensure peace. The apogee of this process was the signing of The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (ICC) that established the ICC in 1998. In the 65 years after 1945, the amount of territory seized by belligerent states per year dropped to less than six percent of what it had been a century before 1928.

While the amount of “hot wars” between states reduced, these frameworks, charters and organisations did not prevent the Cold War and an Iron Curtain descending across Europe, behind which many peoples lost their right to self-determination. Some analysts believe what prevented the Cold War turning hot was not frameworks of international law, but the use of game theory by the leaders of the US and USSR. Both adopted a strategy which became known as Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) based on game theory, and particularly the Nash equilibrium. MAD described the situation where, if either side used nuclear weapons, both sides would be annihilated, therefore discouraging either from initiating a nuclear war.

Others analysts have credited the spread of democracy to limiting wars. That the US is a democracy did not prevent it from attempting to covertly engineer regime change in Latin America, its war in Vietnam, its invasions of Iraq and Afghanistan in a coalition of other democracies, and its fighting a multi-decade counterterrorism campaign across the Middle East. For some of these interventions, justifications under international law were sought using somewhat questionable claims of preemptive self-defense, in others they were not. Democracies have more checks and balances on the use of force than autocracies, but as history shows in the case of Iraq, mass protests do not prevent them, they can maybe only restrict the scope of conflicts and how they are fought. Of note, the US did not seek to permanently hold territory in any of these campaigns.

The benefits of increased global trade and the risk of losing access to markets is also often cited as a reason for the decline in state on state violence. In 1945, imports and exports accounted for only around ten percent of the world’s GDP; by 2023, they made up 58 percent. The threat of being excluded from international cooperation, and more specifically US economic cooperation (due to its large share of global GDP and the dollar’s status as the world’s reserve currency) weighed heavily against any positive outcome initiating war would provide. However, when a country’s security is threatened economic considerations tend to take second billing. Both Israel and Russia have demonstrated this.

Those who believe economic interdependence has kept us peaceful should be worried.

[The economic growth of China](http://iai.tv/articles/understanding-china-means-understanding-xi-steve-tsang-auid-2736) has offered states an alternative to Western markets and financing, which can soften the blow of Western sanctions. Since 2008 we have also been undeniably aware of the fragile foundations of the Western economies. American growth is now either a bet on new technologies we are yet to monetise (and could create significant unemployment) or protectionism that hastens the collapse of existing international trade structures. The reason imports and exports were so low in the 1940s was in part due to the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which raised tariffs by about 20% on imports, leading to retaliatory tariffs and a steep decline in global trade (by over 65% by 1934). These are comparable to Trump’s Liberation Day tariffs. Those who believe economic interdependence has kept us peaceful should be worried.

Trump is an undoer of the ties that bind us. The US is not a signatory of the Rome Statute, however in 2025, Trump picked back up his undermining of the ICC and international law. This February he issued an Executive Order authorizing sanctions on the ICC and its Chief Prosecutor. UN experts called the move “an attack on global rule of law.” As well as making semi-serious threats to invade Canada, Panama and Greenland, Trump has signalled the US will do little to defend other states’ right not to be conquered unless it directly benefits the US. In April, after threatening to pull military assistance, Trump warned Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelensky that if he did not consider his peace plan that would cede twenty percent of Ukraine’s territory to Russia, he would face “losing [his] whole country.”

The post-1945 international order was more likely kept in place by the threat of American violence and the promise of access to wealth generating free markets, rather than a shared sense of global morality or justice. This was more obvious from the Global South, who saw the Western countries punish weaker states for breaking the rules they themselves regularly broke. Regardless of the hypocrisy, most states when gaming their options, saw they would maximise their outcomes by playing by the rules. If the threat is no longer there and the promise is less than it once was, more states will develop strategies that will fracture and fragment the old order.

We are potentially already seeing the order that will succeed our current one. It is one where strong regional powers carve the world into spheres of influence. These states will be free to exercise their influence within their regional spheres, forcing less powerful states into unfavourable economic relationships and even exercising their territorial rights, in exchange for protection. China is also already engaging more intensely with the organizations and institutions (including both the UN and the ICC) positioning itself to fill the American void. A Chinese influenced system of international law would be focused on territorial integrity and less on human rights, but with the expectation that China would, when it suited, be exempt to any rules that it chose, as with the previous guarantor of the old order.

Writing on the tragedies, poet Anne Carson asks and answers, “Why does tragedy exist? Because you are full of rage. Why are you full of rage? Because you are full of grief.” There are few better descriptions of the motivations for war in the Middle East.

Those outside the great powers, including those in the Global South, should form broad coalitions and institutions to share the responsibility of upkeeping and shaping legal norms, making them more legitimate, resilient to internal politics of any single nation and representative of all of the different ways we humans choose to live and be governed. These coalitions should experiment with new forms of organization, learning from protest groups who developed direct action in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis. They will not be able to fully replace the threat of American violence, but together, pose a regional defensive threat, that when coupled with the promise of economic collaboration, change the balance of outcomes, such as the growing coalition of Baltic and Scandinavian states who are attempting to mitigate the threat of Russian aggression.

These coalitions will need to recognize that war is likely to become a family resemblance term as states use hybrid warfare (including cyberwarfare, economic espionage, sabotage and covert interference in democratic processes) in attempts to seize information and influence as much as a territory. New international laws will be needed.

We should also remember when international justice seems a quixotic task, that international rulings even when unenforceable internationally can provide justification, support and a source of inspiration for national opposition and protest groups within the countries the international courts rule against.

As much as [playing games is part of human experience](http://iai.tv/articles/zizek-and-the-game-of-life-auid-2508), so is waging war. Game theory must use its rational analysis to analyse irrational human motivations like revenge, humiliation, pride, and the desire for legacy. With the current wars, as much as analyzing rational choices, we may gain insight from explorations of timeless human motivations, such as the Greek tragedies. Writing on the tragedies, poet Anne Carson asks and answers, “Why does tragedy exist? Because you are full of rage. Why are you full of rage? Because you are full of grief.” There are few better descriptions of the motivations for war in the Middle East.

We are distinguished from other animals by our tremendous capability for rational thinking, but we are also alone among the animals in our readiness to kill and be killed for nonsensical ideas in an attempt to give our lives meaning. Game theory can provide a rational analysis of our irrationality if we recognise it in ourselves and in others, and take into account what they, rationally or not, expect the others to do. It should at least, at a time of transition and unpredictability, push us to examine how we move to new equilibriums that can, if only temporarily, halt the current spirals of violence.
