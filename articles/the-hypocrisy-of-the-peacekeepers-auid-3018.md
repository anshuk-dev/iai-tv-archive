# The hypocrisy of the peacekeepers

*When peacekeeping looks a lot like war*

**By Flemming Splidsboel Hansen** | *9th December 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,026 words*

![The hypocrisy of the peacekeepers](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/peacekeeping-piece.webp)

---

*Peacekeeping, as we imagine it, is under threat. As we witness the decline of the rules based international order, this system to ensure peace and stability after conflicts is under threat from all sides. But the inherently political nature of peacekeeping threatens to undermine this vital process. Flemming Splidsboel Hansen argues that as Russia sends mercenary forces into Africa under the guise of 'peacekeeping', we need to define the idea before it just becomes a friendlier synonym for war.*

As Russian troops were already revving up the engines to launch their unprovoked and illegal aggression against Ukraine on 24 February 2022, Russian President Vladimir Putin issued an order for the Russian military to prepare so-called peacekeepers to move across the border. A few days later, the speaker of the Russian Duma, Vyacheslav Volodin, insisted on upholding the political charade initiated by the Kremlin by urging Russians to support “our soldiers and officers, who take part in the peacekeeping operation”. By then, Russian troops had fired barrage after barrage of missiles against targets, including civilian, across Ukraine, and their first war crimes on Ukrainian soil had already been recorded.

\_\_\_

“When troops of one country enter the territory of another country without its consent, they are not impartial peacekeepers. They are not peacekeepers at all”

\_\_\_

This flagrant misuse of the term “peacekeeping” led United Nations [UN] Secretary-General Antonio Guterres to voice his concern. “I am concerned about the perversion of the concept of peacekeeping”, [he noted](https://x.com/antonioguterres/status/1496290209151000581), adding that “when troops of one country enter the territory of another country without its consent, they are not impartial peacekeepers. They are not peacekeepers at all”. However, both political and conceptual developments in Russia suggest that the country will seek to exploit still further the notion of peacekeeping in an attempt to shape international outcomes to match its own interests. Starting a war under the guise of peacekeeping may sound counter intuitive, but it speaks to something fundamental about Russia’s foreign policy.

These developments essentially include taking a much more cynical and instrumental approach to the big peacekeeping questions – when to intervene, where to intervene, how to intervene and who to send to intervene?

**Is this a joke?**

As I have been researching the Russian approach to peacekeeping, many colleagues have asked me if this was a joke? Such is the general understanding that Russia is incapable of undertaking the kind of impartial action which is associated with peacekeeping. Most Western experts do indeed share this sentiment, typically using the ironic “peacekeeping” to de-legitimize operations claimed by the Russian authorities to be proper peacekeeping.

However, Russian-led “peacekeeping” operations – numbering six in total – have played a very important role in Russian foreign policy following the break-up of the Soviet Union, these are South Ossetia (Georgia), Abkhazia (Georgia), Transnistria (Moldova), Tajikistan, Kazakhstan, and Nagorno-Karabakh (Azerbaijan). It is of course impossible to say anything certain about how the post-Soviet space would look today without these operations, but it is highly likely that Russian influence in this space would be much reduced relative to where it stands now, and that several of the other former Soviet republics by contrast would enjoy a much higher degree of freedom to define their own development paths.

The Russian record of peacekeeping – or “peacekeeping” – in the post-Soviet space has been presented by critics as a modern-day “[Monroe Doctrine](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287543705_Russia%27s_Monroe_Doctrine_peacekeeping_peacemaking_or_imperial_outreach)” designed to simply restore “[[the country’s] great power status and [its] dominant role in its historical geostrategic space”.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3095759)

\_\_\_

\_\_\_

Even this legacy has been accompanied by the development, within political and expert communities, of doctrines and conceptual thinking designed to legitimize the Russian approach to peacekeeping.

A dominant line behind this development has insisted that the collective West, led by the United States of America [USA], has been opposed to Russian peacekeeping, working actively to prevent Russian operations in the post-Soviet space, so that Moscow would be less successful in stabilizing this important space characterized by conflict and disintegration.

A more extreme take on the same line argues that Western states have *deliberately* injected additional conflict and disintegration into this space with the same aim of preventing the stabilization of it. The ultimate ambition of this strategy, as argued by Russian sources, is to interrupt the (inevitable) rise of Russia as a challenger in the international system. From the Russian perspective, keeping the peace means keeping the former Soviet states within Russia’s sphere of influence. To this end, invading neighbors is a logical extension of peacekeeping.

**Conscripts as future peacekeepers**

Russia does not have a doctrine for peacekeeping. The country’s operational guidelines instead have to be pieced together from other doctrines, that regulate foreign and defence policies, but even these tell us very little. They speak about the importance of peacekeeping as a foreign policy tool and emphasize the ambition of Russia to take part in more peacekeeping operations, but they remain silent on the main modalities. The lack of a designated doctrine for peacekeeping offers policy-makers a high degree of flexibility when designing and executing operations. Recent policy developments suggest that Russia is preparing to allow itself even greater flexibility in the future. The country’s previous foreign policy doctrine, dating back to 2016, emphasized the importance for all peacekeeping actors of relying on “[realistic and concise mandates, which do not allow any arbitrary interpretation, especially in relation to the use of force”,](https://interkomitet.com/foreign-policy/basic-documents/foreign-policy-concept-of-the-russian-federation-approved-by-president-of-the-russian-federation-vladimir-putin-on-november-30-2016/) but a new foreign policy doctrine, revised and signed in 2023, does not refer to mandates at all.

\_\_\_

A strong sensitivity remains regarding the deployment of especially teenage conscripts

\_\_\_

In a highly significant step, the Russian Duma in 2023 passed a law preparing for mobilized troops and conscripts to be deployed to peacekeeping operations. Prior to this, only contract soldiers could be deployed. Any future decision to send mobilized troops and conscripts to peacekeeping operations will, in principle, require the consent of the individual soldier. This provision should be seen as an indication of the controversial nature of the law – a strong sensitivity remains regarding the deployment of especially teenage conscripts – rather than a firm guarantee that these troops will *not* be deployed against their will. Russian conscripts have, after all, been pushed by their military commanders across the border into occupied Ukraine, where some of them have been killed.

The 2023 law on the deployment of peacekeepers adds to the traditional pool of available troops in a dramatic fashion. This in turn suggests that Russia is looking to increase its deployment to peacekeeping – or “peacekeeping” – operations. This may happen, for instance, through a re-designation of various armed conflicts, in which Russia is already involved, to peacekeeping operations. Russia’s war in Ukraine is a likely candidate for this. This would bring the Russian authorities to do a full circle – opening the illegal and unprovoked aggression against Ukraine with the term “peacekeeping operation” and attempting to close it with the use of the same caption. One scenario announced by Russian politicians would be the introduction of a demilitarized zone between the Russian-occupied regions of Eastern and Southern Ukraine and the “rest” of Ukraine. Russian “peacekeepers” could then be deployed to this zone. The term “peace-keeping” would fall victim to Russia’s acute operational demands.

**The elements of the Russian approach**

Looking at greater detail at the big peacekeeping questions - when to intervene, where to intervene, how to intervene and who to send to intervene? -  the political and conceptual debate in Russia points to a number of important and far-reaching changes.

Firstly, Russia is set to lower even further the threshold for when to intervene. A leading Russian expert, and staff member at the Russian Military Academy, points already now to a controversial “Russian peacekeeping model”, which, so he explains, “in a number of cases has allowed, under critical military-political conditions, direct armed intervention in a conflict, without a mandate from the UN or another authorized organization, with the aim of localizing and swiftly resolving it”.[[1]](#_edn1) We should expect Russia to launch more “peacekeeping” interventions when it sees a need to protect its national, or perhaps rather *regime*, interests, applying the “peacekeeping” sticker in the process to win broader acceptance of its policies. As already noted, the 2023 foreign policy doctrine does not refer to mandates.

\_\_\_

Secondly, the post-Soviet space remains of critical importance to Russia, and the country is clearly willing to go very far to maintain its position of power in this area. Just witness Ukraine, where Russia is fighting a war with exceptional brutality and a complete disregard for the laws of war as well as for its own troops. However, as Russia finds itself isolated from the West and under pressure in Ukraine, the country has an acute need to cultivate new allies. The African continent stands out here. Already a high priority for Russian leaders before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 – as demonstrated for instance by the 2019 Russia-Africa Summit in Sochi – it is highly likely that Russia will offer more military interventions to support African leaders facing political pressure or even open protest. Russian troops have intervened in so-called peacekeeping operations to help save political allies in the post-Soviet space, most notably in Tajikistan and Kazakhstan, and these operations may easily be replicated, on a smaller scale, in Africa.

Thirdly, Russia will rely still more on “peacekeeping” operations spanning the physical, cyber and cognitive domains. This is full cross-domain approach. The Russian literature on peacekeeping offers still more perspectives on the alleged need to introduce more advanced [Information Operations](https://www.rand.org/topics/information-operations.html) or [Psychological Operations](https://www.rand.org/topics/psychological-warfare.html) into the Russian toolkit. The aim will be to influence target populations in areas of intervention, for instance in various African countries. This is obviously highly controversial. The Putin regime is a strictly authoritarian regime, which suppresses and controls its own population in ways not seen since the Soviet Union of the early 1980s. By using the full range of the opportunities provided by the cyber domain, digital surveillance and regime propaganda delivered directly to people’s screens, the Russian authorities go even further than their Soviet counterparts four decades ago. A full cross-domain approach to peacekeeping will include advanced tools for suppressing and controlling local populations, and it will spread messages of conflict, hate and the need to “protect” society from hostile forces, domestic and foreign.

Finally, Russia will deploy more Private Military Companies (PMCs) to its “peacekeeping” operations. It is important to understand that Russian PMCs are *not* private; they are state-run and state-financed, and they serve as an extended arm of the Russian state. They act upon political orders. The PMCs offer many advantages for the Russian state. Most critically, perhaps, they offer the Russian state deniability. As Russia does not officially allow PMCs to exist within its borders – yet rely on several, most notably the Wagner Group – the authorities simply deny any ties, arguing that since PMCs have not been legalized in Russia, there cannot be any PMCs. “Peacekeeping” operations executed by Russian PMCs would be recognized by the Russian state, but the Russian state would insist that these PMCs operate independently and without political instructions. Additional advantages include the fact that PMCs represent a flexible tool, deployable at short notice and fully detached from the Russian public. Questions, including those relating to atrocities and war crimes, are not asked, and *if* they were, the Russian authorities would deny any knowledge and responsibility.

This is a disturbing development for everyone hoping to save peacekeeping from the worst excesses. A major power in the international system and a permanent member of the UN Security Council, Russia obviously plays an important role in setting the standards by which peacekeeping may be done. A lowering of the threshold for intervention, a cynical application of the sticker “peacekeeping” to operations designed to prop up authoritarian leaders, an increased use of cyber and information operations Russian-style and the deployment of state-led contractors operating outside any kind of legal control is the scenario we will be confronting. Much will then be lost.
