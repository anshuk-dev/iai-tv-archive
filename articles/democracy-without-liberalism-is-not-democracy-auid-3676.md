# Democracy without liberalism is not democracy

*Not about elections alone*

**By Philip Pettit** | *10th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,776 words*

![Democracy without liberalism is not democracy](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/democracy-without-liberalism-is-not-democracy.webp)

---

*Chinese philosopher Tongdong Bai recently argued in IAI News that democracy and liberalism are fundamentally at odds. Electoral majorities often support illiberal policies, and this, he thinks, will lead to the demise of Western “liberal democracies.” Today, Princeton political philosopher **Philip Pettit** responds, arguing that liberalism is essential to democracy. Tongdong’s mistake, he argues, is to equate democracy solely with elections. The point of democracy is to give people power over government, but elections alone are not enough to do this. Ordinary people must also be able to contest those in government through a free media, in independent courts, and without fear of persecution. Only liberal values and institutions make this possible.*

Tongdong Bai’s article, “[Democracy and liberalism are incompatible](https://iai.tv/articles/democracy-and-liberalism-are-incompatible-auid-3662),” like all his work, offers an intriguing Confucian perspective on democracy. He begins from the assumption that democracy is about “the rule of the majority,” and that it is built around the idea that “each individual should have an equal say in the political decision-making process”: that is, around “the convention of one-person-one-vote.” On this assumption, government is “by the people” and that, he suggests, raises a problem. For there is no guarantee within a majoritarian electoral system that it will be “for the people”; more specifically, that it will provide for the “equal opportunities and equality before the law” that he associates with liberalism. On the contrary, he says, recent experience has taught us that it is liable to give rise to a right-wing populism which threatens to erode liberal rights of that kind. Among a range of baneful effects that he lists, “it often encourages the majority to silence minorities,” perhaps even “leading to ethnic cleansing in fledgling democracies.”

I agree with Tongdong that [a purely electoral system of the kind he imagines is certainly liable to generate devastating problems](https://iai.tv/video/to-save-democracy-we-need-fewer-elections-with-helene-landemore). But the question I want to raise is whether it is fair, in theory or in practice, to equate democracy with such a system, distinguishing it from liberalism. I think of democracy as a system under which the collective people exercise a checking power over government and thereby guard individuals against state abuse, and I believe that such a system needs to have a liberal or constitutional aspect as well as an electoral one. More on that later. First, however, a little history.

The idea of an electoral democracy—a system where the suffrage ceases to be significantly restricted—entered public thinking over the last few hundred years under a different name: that of a representative system, which was described by James Mill (father of John Stuart Mill) in 1819 as “the grand invention of modern times.” And as the representative system evolved within Britain, the United States, France, Australia and other countries, it was taken to be just one element in a wider cultural and legal setting. In that setting, a written or unwritten constitution laid down the rules on how legislative, executive and judicial authorities should be organized, on how different authorities should be required to perform individually and in tandem, and on what protections should be available to ordinary citizens under the authority of the state; the citizenry gradually expanded but remained limited in early days on grounds of gender and property.

The word “democracy” came to be used of such a system as a whole—that is, of a representative government operating within a constitutionally constrained state—only in the early-to-mid-nineteenth century. Up until then it was used for an arrangement in which the citizenry as a whole would rule on a participatory, majoritarian basis. Such an arrangement was rejected on almost all sides down to the nineteenth century—Rousseau is a complex exception—as in the Federalist Papers in the United States and in the works of British utilitarians like James Mill. This is not surprising since it was figures like Jean Bodin and Thomas Hobbes who had given that participatory, majoritarian meaning to “democracy,” hoping to make the idea of a popular government look less appealing than the absolutist monarchy that they favored.

On this account of how the notion of democracy emerged over the nineteenth century, it referred to a system where the rule of the state was disciplined by constitutional constraints on the one side and on the other by recourse to the election, by an increasing number of citizens, of national legislators and, in the American case, of the head of the executive. In this development, the word “democracy” came to be used, innovatively, for a system that embodied rule-of-law constraints constitutionally and extended the suffrage beyond a traditional elite to an increasing number of citizens. Tongdong describes such constraints as liberal, in deference to contemporary usage—the word “liberal” gained its current sense in the nineteenth century—although they would have been seen earlier as republican constraints embedded in the idea of the mixed constitution: the idea, as it was represented after Montesquieu, of separating and dividing political power and imposing checks and balances on its employment.

To make these points is to find fault on historical grounds with equating democracy, as Tongdong does, with electoral politics and contrasting it with constitutionalism or liberalism. To be fair to him, he is not alone in that move as political scientists have often thought of the system they explore as entirely electoral in character, leaving constitutional matters to legal and philosophical theorists. Moreover, the line he takes would also be supported by populists themselves insofar as they wish to decry constitutional constraints as undemocratic. But these approaches all fail to reflect the fact that this is not how democracy was conceived in its emergence over the last two centuries and that most of us, influenced by that history, would reject the idea that a regime should count as a democracy if, for example, it didn’t institutionalize the independence of the judiciary. That idea is also rejected, for the record, in standard audits of democracy such as the well-respected [Economist Index of Democracy](https://www.eiu.com/n/campaigns/democracy-index-2025/); these invariably rate the democratic performance of states on a much richer basis than that provided by their electoral record.

Moving beyond historical considerations, however, I think that there are theoretical and practical reasons to insist on combining constitutional and electoral elements in our notion of democracy. In the original Greek sense, democracy simply meant a system in which ordinary people had significant but not necessarily electoral control over government: Athens, the exemplar of Greek democracy, operated under an arrangement where most offices were filled on [a lottery basis](https://iai.tv/articles/how-to-solve-democracys-polarisation-problem-auid-2996). In that original, appealing sense of the term, democracy requires that citizens should share equally in a system of control over state authorities that protects each against discrimination and abuse. But how might a democracy empower and protect individuals in that way? Not just by enabling them to vote and campaign and stand in the election of some authorities, though that will certainly be important. But also by being able under the constitution to [contest those in government before an independent judiciary](https://iai.tv/video/how-to-sue-your-government-with-jolyon-maugham), in a free and fair media system, or, without fear of persecution, on the streets.

In support of that theoretical reason for equating democracy with a system that embodies constitutional as well as electoral elements, there is also a practical consideration to put on the table. This is that the constitutional and electoral elements are needed in practice to support one another. Let parties compete under an arrangement where victory goes to those who command majority support, and they will each have to vote with their feet in favor of the freedom of information, the freedom of speech and the freedom of association that they must enjoy if they are to compete with any chance of success; otherwise they might just pay lip service to such ideals. And it is only if such ideals are thereby embedded in the expectations and actions of citizens—it is only if they are secured as habits of the heart—that the integrity of the electoral process will be robust and reliable. Constitutional ideals are likely to be stabilized in the presence of electoral politics, and electoral politics is likely to operate smoothly and dependably only if those ideals are stable.

I reject Tongdong’s idea, then, that we should give up on electoral democracy and look for a system where constitutional or liberal constraints are satisfied without exposure to the pressures of popular politics. I especially resist his proposal that by “limiting the voice of the people, the Confucian mixed regime may address … problems” like the tyranny of the majority. While he describes his proposal as a “constitutionalism-based Confucian hybrid,” the only guarantees on offer—for example, the only guarantee of “the right to free speech”—seem to be that they are would be “endorsed” in the polity he envisages, as they were endorsed in “traditional Chinese regimes.”

It has long been a principle of institutional design, in Hume’s words, that when we design institutions “every man ought to be supposed a knave, and to have no other end, in all his actions, than private interest.” The problem with Tongdong’s Confucian proposal, as I understand it, is that it assumes that those in charge within his ideal regime will be wise and virtuous. Perhaps the institutions of the state ought not to be designed on Hume’s pessimistic assumption that those living under them, particularly those in office, may be knaves. But they certainly should not be designed on the assumption that they will be saints. We need to be able to rely on our institutions to deliver the goods we expect of the state—Tongdong and I may not differ much on what those goods are—and that requires that we should be able to rely on them regardless of the merits and virtues of those who happen at any time to be in charge.

In conclusion, a concession. I agree strongly that in recent years electoral democracy has given rise to a [form of populism](https://iai.tv/video/the-politics-of-populism) that would threaten the benefits that Tongdong casts as liberal rights. I trace this, however, not to an inherent weakness in the notion of a constitutional democracy, but to failures in the design of democratic institutions that are in principle remediable. These include the failure, highly salient in the United States, to [keep money out of politics](https://iai.tv/video/the-dream-of-democracy), the failure in a country like Hungary to give sufficient protection to the constitution itself, and the failure almost everywhere to ensure [the integrity of the informational sphere](https://iai.tv/video/lies-damned-lies-and-post-truth). I would urge democrats to think about how to guard against such problems rather than resorting to Confucian alternatives that may have guided ancient regimes in China but remain untested under modern conditions.
