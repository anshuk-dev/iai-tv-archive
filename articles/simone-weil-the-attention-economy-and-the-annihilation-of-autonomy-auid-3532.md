# Simone Weil, the attention economy, and the annihilation of autonomy

*Real attention recognises the self in the other*

**By Kathryn Lawson** | *27th March 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,775 words*

*The attention economy isn’t just stealing our focus; it’s reshaping our reality. Drawing on Simone Weil, philosopher **Kathryn Lawson** argues that social media algorithms don’t just distract us but commit a deeper form of violence, reshaping our minds and stripping away genuine human connection. Each scroll surrenders our thinking to machines designed solely to keep us engaged, replacing what Weil saw as true attention—essential for ethics and truth—with a hollow substitute. Now, as a California jury holds Meta and Google liable for fuelling a young woman’s childhood addiction to their platforms, the solution isn't simply logging off, but relearning how to genuinely attend to others and reclaim our mental autonomy.*

“Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity.”

—Simone Weil

A theorist of radical social justice, Simone Weil’s philosophy can offer a startling look at the ontological violence inherent in the human being’s contemporary relationship to technology. The monetization of our internal world through what has been labelled the “[attention economy](live/iai-live-may-consciousness-attention-and-the-age-of-distraction)” has no end of Orwellian consequences, but Weil’s philosophy can help us to understand the impact of these issues on our very being. At this point, most of us know the situation well: as of the early 2000s, social media websites shifted to maximize time spent on the platforms and created profit by selling access to their users’ attention rather than through subscriptions or one-time purchases. We no longer pay for a product; lending out [our internal world to the whims of an algorithm](https://iai.tv/articles/plato-vs-the-algorithm-beauty-is-the-path-to-virtue-auid-3426) *is* the product. Across the hours, days, and months, these whims become systems increasingly bespoke and fine-tuned to our own preferences and desires. In an attention economy, the key objective of the algorithms guiding us is to manufacture the necessity to keep scrolling, to hold our attention on the next meme, reel, picture, or song just beyond our grasp.

This careful manufacturing of a surface-level attention to the next thing creates an *ersatz* attention, the German word for “substitute*,*” popularized during the Second World War. A cup of roasted chicory root, barley, acorns, rye, or dandelion root would stand in as an *ersatz* coffee when coffee bean rations were unavailable. While these substitutes could in some ways stand in, they were ultimately what Nabokov would call a *pale fire* comparison to the true article. Writing on his imprisonment in Auschwitz, Primo Levi makes note of the *ersatz* coffee, which adds yet another layer of the surreal simulacra of existence in the camps. This is precisely the type of attention produced by the attention economy. This *ersatz* attention is the shadow-self of true attention. It appears as if we are attending to our screens, but, in fact, it is the mirror-darkly of attention.

In contradistinction to the *ersatz* attention economy, true attention is the keystone of [Simone Weil’s philosophy](https://iai.tv/articles/why-attention-matters-for-morality-auid-2711). It is through attention that one can experience the highest possible level of truth in reality. Weil equates attention to prayer, to genius, and argues that it is the only avenue to living ethically. She offers her reader a pathway to reality and divine love through genuine attention, a path that has become less and less travelled and at times even barred altogether by the violent domination of the attention economy.

The cost of the attention economy is not merely the minutes and hours of a person’s life; it is the violent transformation of reality. There are two levels upon which the complex algorithms we follow inflict ontological violence or violence to the very being of ourselves and our world.

\_\_\_

The autonomy of our minds is the collateral damage of the attention economy.

\_\_\_

The first and most foundational form of ontological violence in the attention economy is violence against ourselves. Without even realizing it, or consciously deciding, we have handed over our critical thinking and rational engagement with the world to algorithms. The indexing, sorting, deciding, and listing of facts and information that make up much of our [critical thinking abilities](https://iai.tv/articles/escaping-the-cult-of-rationality-auid-2383) and world-creation have been transferred to computers that judiciously curate what we will experience through our stock trading, encryption, traffic modelling, social media, search engines, and even dating apps. These algorithms are not themselves *thinking*, and [by no means should we assume they are *capable* of thinking](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-is-a-marketing-trick-auid-3450), at least not until the leap to singularity, which leads to a radically unique host of issues. At this point, algorithms are simply collecting data and presenting it to us with the sole goal of monopolizing our attention for as long as possible. But in this process, we have offloaded our thinking onto them. We have handed over the construction of our worlds and have allowed ourselves to be guided by these machine-curated lists.

The small and seemingly inconsequential act of doomscrolling while we sit in a waiting room or stand in line at the grocery store is a violent rending of our autonomy and thought to an algorithm fixated on possessing our attention. We are guided into what we see, how we see it, what to feel, and how to feel it. We are shocked, appalled, tickled, chuffed, and then ushered forward to the next image or sound just beyond…and we need not ever consider feelings, experiences, or ideas beyond the scope of the content we receive. The fixation on the screen is numbing or disassociating, yes, but it also [reduces our reality](https://iai.tv/articles/idealism-is-realism-jeremy-dunham-auid-2815) beyond the screen to the dimensions of our curated content. We set down the phone, and the world reflects the opinions of our algorithm. The autonomy of our minds is the collateral damage of the attention economy.

To hold our attention requires that we are infuriated and/or enticed. A polarized world, divisive ideologies, dog whistles, and ignorance are certainly contenders for holding our minds captive. But so too are simplistic narratives of connection, beauty, and truth in things like [a loyal pet](https://iai.tv/articles/the-unjust-power-dynamics-of-pet-ownership-auid-3518), a good friend, a love-filled home, or an epic journey, catered to the algorithm of choice. This polarized, simplistic content promotes what Simone Weil saw as the problems of collectivity, namely, giving up our ability to think in order to slide into a group’s ideology. But this is a particularly Orwellian variation of this problem in that it is not to a religious institution, political party, or social movement that we hand over our thought, but rather to a machine whose sole purpose is to ransom our interior life for as long as possible. The violence of this act is made all the more offensive by our sheer passivity, lack of consent, and lack of recognition about what has been harvested and stolen from our innermost selves.

\_\_\_

The only way to overcome force is through true attention to the other, the miracle that transforms object into subject.

\_\_\_

The second form of ontological violence rendered in our engagement with algorithms is a violence inflicted upon those people suffering whose stories flash by our eyes as we scroll. If we draw on the philosophy of Simone Weil, this happens in two ways. First, Weil argues that to truly be moral to another, we must attend to them fully in their affliction and pain. She suggests that we must ransom ourselves to the other in such a way that we give up our totalizing conception of the world and attempt to see from their perspective. We release our conceptualization of reality and ask the other person: “What are you going through?” Only then can we step back into a world that is shared and act ethically with and for the other person. This, of course, is an impossibility as we scroll because we have already given our autonomous thought and our world in ransom to our algorithm. Far from allowing the other to exist, we have given [creative control to a machine](https://iai.tv/articles/ai-does-not-have-copyright-auid-3476) and as such, the world is no longer ours to give. But further, the algorithms of the attention economy are designed to keep us scrolling. The ways in which we receive news, stories of atrocities and oppression, both locally and globally, are meant to hold us only for a moment until we quickly carry on, either to find out more about the situation or to escape into a numbing alternative realm of content. With this built-in feature, nay, *foundation* of our algorithms, true attention to the afflicted person becomes an impossibility.

For Weil, it is our attention, prolonged and steadfast, that can turn the afflicted person from an object into a subject: “[It is a recognition that the sufferer exists, not only as a unit in a collection, or a specimen from the social category labeled ‘unfortunate,’ but as a [hu]man, exactly like us, who was one day stamped with a special mark by affliction.](https://archive.org/stream/Weil1951ReflectionsOnTheRightUseOfSchoolStudiesWithAViewToTheLoveOfGod/Weil%20-%201951%20-%20Reflections%20on%20the%20Right%20Use%20of%20School%20Studies%20With%20a%20View%20To%20the%20Love%20of%20God_djvu.txt)” Weil claims that from at least the time of Homer’s *Iliad*, humans have been drawn into this objectification of others in order to hold power over them. [The violent and polarized political climate](https://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-ideology) of our own time probably would not shock Weil, but would be yet another example of the sway of force dominating and objectifying all those in its path. The only way to overcome force is through true attention to the other, the miracle that transforms object into subject. Miraculous, though not beyond hope, attention has been rendered nearly impossible in our own time through the ontological annihilation of the mind’s autonomy and the cultivated surface-level attention to the afflicted other. We hand over the creation of the world to an algorithm as we attend to a shadowed existence, racing from image to image, allowing those in affliction to remain objects of our *ersatz* attention. This level of mechanization is, in some senses, beyond Weil’s conception of the world, but she did understand the mechanization of industrial labor both in theory and in practice during her time, carefully studying Marx and working in automobile factories. She noted that this form of mechanical labor rendered the laborer an object unworthy of subjectivity. Marked as she was by this work, she advocated the cultivation of attention as a means to [push back against the force that objectifies human beings](https://iai.tv/articles/creativity-is-about-rebellion-not-productivity-auid-3486). As such, the contemporary violent technological encounter is a manifestation of force that is different in its particular form but is not different in kind from other exhibits of force. This means that this ontological violence is one which we can miraculously overcome…but not as long as we continue to ransom ourselves to technology that produces an *ersatz* attention, overwhelms our autonomy, manifests a polarized and simplistic world, and violently objectifies the marginalized other, barring our attention from engaging with them.
