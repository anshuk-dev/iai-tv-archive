# There is no such thing as a country

*The world map is a political fiction*

**By Ian Hurd** | *15th April 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,524 words*

![There is no such thing as a country](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/there-is-no-such-thing-as-a-country-new-website-image3.webp)

---

*What counts as a "country" is far from an objective fact. In spite of measures such as borders, governments, and people, political scientist **Ian Hurd** argues that statehood is a fragile social fiction. From the UK’s sudden recognition of Palestine to Israel’s strategic deals in Africa, nations grant or withhold recognition not based on a territory's merit, but on cold strategic interest. Ultimately, international law is used as a tool to justify these political whims, and the desperate search for official status signals a state's fundamental vulnerability.*

I often begin my class on International Politics by presenting a map of the world and asking students how many countries there are. Many offer 193 as the answer, which is a decent starting point: it is the membership of the [United Nations](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/member-states). A few will then point out that [Palestine,](https://www.un.org/unispal/) a country by most standards, has been kept out of the UN for political reasons, but may tick the number up by one. Others bring up [Kosovo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kosovo), or [Taiwan](https://iai.tv/video/the-taiwan-tripwire), or [Tibet](http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-pacific-16689779), each a non-UN member with a plausible claim to statehood that might bring the number even higher. A few wonder whether [Tuvalu](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tuvalu) (pop. 10,000) or [Monaco](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-europe-17615784) (<1 sq mi.) should be taken seriously as countries, and if not, then the number should perhaps be smaller.

The conversation circles until we land somewhere that many find unsatisfactory: no one knows how to count the number of countries because no one knows what counts as a country. It isn’t a math question—it’s a window into the politics of recognition.

Countries come into being when they are recognized by others—we call this practice diplomatic recognition. Recognition might refer to recognizing the state itself, or the government of the state, or of certain boundaries. The history of recognition is full of [norms and rules and customs](https://iai.tv/articles/to-defend-international-order-we-must-paradoxically-pursue-the-national-interest-auid-3468), and also of ambiguities and contradictions and oddities. The fact that recognition doesn’t follow well-behaved conventions makes it all the more interesting: it is a space of politics and history, not of consistency or patterns.

The common-sense view is that a country deserves recognition when it has a coherent government, a set of borders, and a population inside it. A distinct language and sense of culture help too. These are the criteria set out in an international agreement from 1933 called the [Montevideo Convention](https://treaties.un.org/pages/showdetails.aspx?objid=0800000280166aef), which helped to standardize the modern legal idea of statehood: a country is a thing that has territorial frontiers, a population, a government, and relations with others.

It is easy to see that the UK, Canada, and Tuvalu have these properties. But so too does my house: it has boundaries (property lines, plus a hedgerow for good measure), inhabitants who have relations with the neighbors, and a way of making household decisions. What makes countries unique as a political form is that they are recognized by others *as countries*. They are [social facts](https://iai.tv/articles/forget-facts-and-values-everything-is-a-judgement-auid-3078) rather than brute facts; their existence depends on what others say and do about them. This is where things get interesting.

Recognition decisions are made by governments according to their own desires and judgments. Neither Montevideo nor any other piece of international law obligates countries to recognize those who meet the criteria. Recognition decisions typically have more to do with the interests of the government making the decision than they do with the objective features of the target.

The UK recognized Palestine in 2025 because doing so suited the desires of the UK government at the time, whereas it hadn’t before: not in 1917 with the [Balfour Declaration](https://www.britannica.com/event/Balfour-Declaration), nor in 1948 with the [State of Israel](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/creation-israel), nor any other time. The objective features of the Palestinian state were not obviously stronger than before—indeed, they were markedly poor at the time due to the invasion by Israel—but the interests of the British government had changed.

Non-recognition is often even more interesting than recognition. When we see that 156 countries have recognized Palestine, we wonder [what’s going on](https://iai.tv/articles/only-states-can-wage-war-and-why-it-matters-auid-2923) for those that haven’t. Turkey refuses to recognize [Cyprus](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-europe-17217956), and North Korea doesn’t recognize either South Korea or Japan. For students of world politics, each refusal is an invitation to look further into the relationships among the parties, which often means thinking about [symbolism, history, and contested meanings](https://iai.tv/articles/a-world-without-certainty-needs-its-own-grand-narratives-auid-3542).

The history of state recognition shows all manner of reasons behind recognition and non-recognition. A list of the most common would include access to resources, to favor a friend or spite an enemy, and to signal one’s strength. When Israel recognized the country of [Somaliland](https://theconversation.com/israels-recognition-of-somaliland-the-strategic-calculations-at-play-273817) last year (a wannabe state, broken from Somalia), the decision was apparently made with an eye on access to Red Sea ports and to a destination for forcibly displaced Palestinians.

Thus, the common-sense view of recognition doesn’t get us very far in understanding the real world. A territory that meets the Montevideo criteria may or may not be recognized as a country, and the uncertainty lies with judgments made by other governments.

But this is not to say that international law is insignificant on the issue. In fact, the power of international law is evident throughout the practice of recognition: a state has rights and privileges that are denied to others, including embassies, UN membership, rights to [self-defense and sovereignty,](https://legal.un.org/repertory/art51.shtml) and immunity from many laws. These are all *legal* objects, and while they may well be violated with some frequency, the fact that territories seem eager to be recognized as states is good evidence that they are seen as having [value](https://iai.tv/video/a-world-without-borders).

The key is to understand that the power of international law comes when it is put to use in the pursuit of some goal. Governments and activists bring law to life when they use legal rationales for their choices, and the availability of legal justification makes it easier to explain one’s choices. The Montevideo criteria are thus useful to a state that wants to explain why it recognizes Palestine (for instance) but not Somaliland as a state, by defining the language and concepts that are relevant to the judgment. They are equally useful to activists who want to criticize the decision by providing resources for showing that the two should be treated the same or for claiming hypocrisy. Most people take legal arguments seriously most of the time—this is the heart of the [ideology of the rule of law](https://worldjusticeproject.org/about-us/overview/what-rule-law). And this gives [international law its power](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691170114/how-to-do-things-with-international-law) in international politics.

Thus, the discursive power of international law is neither [apology nor utopia](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/from-apology-to-utopia/146C2D8967B109E959E651A5583EED54) for governments—that is, neither flimsy window-dressing nor a decisive policy guide. It is a tool that empowers some acts and some actors while constraining others.

There are two lessons here. First, the world of politics is not a domain of cause-and-effect. There is no reason to expect that the same set of inputs will produce a consistent result. This is evident from the fact that there is no mechanical relationship between meeting the Montevideo criteria and state recognition. Countries take different positions on whether Taiwan, Palestine, or Somaliland should be recognized as states—the “objective facts” of the candidate produce different choices on recognition from outside. Mechanical expectations of regularity are appropriate among engineers and scientists, but they mislead when applied to the study of social life.

Second, recognition is a game of power politics, and it comes with interesting nuance. Countries want to be recognized because it promises status, security, and access to organizations like the United Nations, but the need for recognition is a signal of weakness. This is a paradox that is familiar in interpersonal relations: people who are desperate for status are advertising their dependence on those whose approval they crave. Joshua Freedman has noted this phenomenon in international politics: the need for [recognition is a kind of vulnerability](https://academic.oup.com/isq/article-abstract/65/1/122/6071834).

States are desperate to feel validated—and thus a little safer—and they often look for recognition wherever they can get it. Kosovo launched a [National Olympic Committee](https://www.lawinsport.com/topics/news/item/ioc-grants-provisional-recognition-to-kosovo-olympic-committee) in 1992, seeking recognition as a nation within the International Olympic Committee, more than 15 years before it became an independent country and joined the United Nations.

The search for recognition and status suggests that countries are so insecure about their own status that they rely on the approval of others. It is a degree of insecurity that cannot be alleviated by a stronger military—it is deeper, more psychological, perhaps more pathological. Jennifer Mitzen named it the “[ontological insecurity](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1354066106067346)” of states.

When I ask my students how many countries there are, I offer a seemingly simple question which, as we look further, turns out to have no answer. As we wallow in the unanswerable puzzle for a while, we begin to see that the absence of patterns or a formula of rules is not a problem at all—instead, it reflects the [inescapably *political* nature](https://iai.tv/articles/world-politics-is-a-shared-myth-one-we-can-change-from-the-bottom-up-auid-3487) of international affairs and international law, where self-serving motives in a socially constructed world are all there is. Recognition is like a living laboratory where students can study the choices and motivations—the good, the bad, and the confounding—that make up international politics.
