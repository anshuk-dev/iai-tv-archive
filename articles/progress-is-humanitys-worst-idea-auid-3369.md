# Progress is humanity's worst idea

*We need to abandon progress*

**By Samuel Miller McDonald** | *23rd September 2025* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,673 words*

![Progress is humanity's worst idea](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Progress-is-humanitys-worst-idea.webp)

---

*The normal history of humanity is a relatively slow upward crawl in mortality, population and wealth, and with the advent of capitalism, and enormous upward spike in all measures of progress. But what if capitalism did not produce this spike in growth, and instead we've got the causality wrong? Samuel Miller McDonald argues that far from a driver of growth, capitalism used the veil of progress to justify the ravaging of the earth and corruption of anything done for anything apart from the all consuming growth imperative. Until we interrogate the history of growth and the turmoil it has spread along the way, we will be forever chasing an illusion that holds back societies and misses the true drivers of social progress.*

*Growth!* That compounding increase in surpluses. The tasty profit icing that accrues glacially, layer by layer. The heavy fruit hanging off a neatly ordered orchard. The hectares cordoned off by state or corporate bureaucracies. Ledger numbers… “Growth” is a fundamental principle of capitalism, the blood on which capital feasts, the engine pumping ceaselessly at the core of the global economy. In countries where there is a particular fixation on growth, we must also mention “growth*ism*.” That suffix denotes what we might call a meta-ideology, an orienting imperative for individuals, institutions, nations, and up, all the way to international organizations: it’s the unwavering faith that the most vital mission of any group of people is, must ever be, above all else, the delivery of new returns higher than the last. The figures must ever swell, the line infinitely ascend.

The conventional story of capitalism tells how this one invention—capital—and its pathways of distribution, forged by the wily hands of Early Modern merchants in the Mediterranean, and stirred into being in the alchemical caldrons of European Enlightenment thought in the half millennium between the 14th and 19th centuries, brought about the birth of economic growth and ideological growthism, and all the fruits, nourishing or rotten, that have followed this political-economic process and cultural imperative. And now, in the end of history, capitalism remains the magical, invisible hand that sustains perpetual annual economic growth.

This story is false. Capitalism did not birth growth and growthism. The tellers have got the narrative causation backward. Growth came first, and from it came this particular sort of accounting logic we now call, for the sake of argument, [*capitalism*](https://iai.tv/video/yanis-varoufakis-on-the-end-of-capitalism).

\_\_\_

The first city-states and empires in history, like Uruk and Babylon, built factories of textile manufacturing, mined voraciously, deforested to exhaustion for lumber, and stole spoils in conquest, which they traded in long supply chains throughout the region.

\_\_\_

During the Viking Age, between 793 and around 1050 CE, Norse settlers went about the world plundering whatever places they could find within marching distance of a seacoast or riverbank. Their far-reaching raids are legend. One of their farthest-sailed settlements was in Greenland, and one thing that sent them there was lust for market-derived profit. In the 10th century, the Norse were important merchants in a broad-spanning transcontinental economy. They were [particularly interested](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277379119305736?via%3Dihub) in walrus ivory, a highly sought-after commodity in this economy. They were so effective at extracting it that they collapsed walrus populations around Iceland and so moved on to Greenland to take more, before they collapsed populations there as well. After initiating bloody conflict with their Inuit neighbors, the Greenland Norse themselves faced collapse, due in part to their overexploitation of local resources for the sake of profit and growth.

Several centuries before them, Rome built a different economy, equally—or even more—[infatuated](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=260995) with trade and commerce, markets and profit. Historians agree that Rome was never “capitalist.” Still, Rome built roads, some of which still stretch across the continent, to carry market goods into its lupine metabolism, even as they plied trade on the Silk Road through Asia, all the way to China. Rome was profit-oriented to its core, [accommodating](https://academic.oup.com/book/40381/chapter-abstract/347114858?redirectedFrom=fulltext&login=false) small business associations engaged in mutual investments, and [hosting](https://academic.oup.com/book/9684/chapter-abstract/156822388?redirectedFrom=fulltext) ubiquitous networks of shopping centers and markets, both permanent and sporadic. They had banks and debts. The words merchant, market, trade, commerce, and money, [all are](https://engelsbergideas.com/essays/going-with-the-grain-the-rise-and-fall-of-the-roman-market/) Latin in origin. The word “profit” derives from the Latin [*prōfectus*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/profectus#Latin "profectus"), meaning “advance, progress, growth.” The Roman obsession with growth was as brutal and eliminatory as the Vikings’. Alongside denuding lands of wildlife—“making deserts and calling it peace,” in the famed words of Tacitus—they killed people by the millions across Europe, Southwest Asia, and North Africa, or secured their fate in the market, buying and selling human beings next to lions and tigers in this huge, fetid economy.

Millennia before them, ancient to Cicero and Caesar, Mesopotamians inaugurated (probably) this particular lineage of profit lust. The first city-states and empires in history, like Uruk and Babylon, built factories of textile manufacturing, mined voraciously, deforested to exhaustion for lumber, and stole spoils in conquest, which they traded in long supply chains throughout the region. Assyrians in northern Mesopotamia, [in the words](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2010/sep/what-makes-civilization) of archaeologist David Wengrow, made

*substantial profits by using relatively small amounts of Anatolian silver to purchase bulk quantities of tin and textiles from production centers further south. These commodities were then traded back over the Taurus mountains into central Turkey, where they were in high demand, in return for large shipments of silver (acquired at a highly favorable rate of exchange), which could be reinvested in the purchase of new merchandise to support further commercial ventures of the same kind.*

The Mesopotamians, too, killed off large populations, sacrificed souls uncounted to their god Marduk, whom they called “Creator of Fulness and Abundance” and “the Founder of Plenteousness.”

In all of these cases, growth and growthism are the gravitational force at the center of these empires, businesses, and raiding parties. Empire itself is, by definition, a political-economic form predicated on expansion, the compounding growth of territory or assets. But even in non-imperial modes, like decentralized Viking settler-colonialism or the wars of aggression waged by the Roman Republic, profit growth remained paramount. And in all cases, these societies predate the rise of capitalism by centuries or millennia.

\_\_\_

Suddenly, growth/ism needed a new program of codification to accommodate all the wealth coming into European economies.

\_\_\_

Capitalism is traditionally understood to have emerged, at the earliest, in the 14th century. It was hammered together as a tool of growthism. Or rather, it was a weapon honed to achieve growth: large landowners throughout Europe all came to the same forge and pulled the same blades they then used to increase yields, cajoled out of the soils and waters by the peasants and serfs who did their bidding, or to evict those tenants if that was more profitable, as they did in the Highland Clearances. They took this weapon to the Americas, and there used it to subdue or annihilate Native Americans, enslave Africans, and build a new society on the promise of perpetual growth and progress.

“Capitalism” was a loose arrangement of administrative tools, accounting practices, property laws, and social relations that were eventually pulled into a theoretically cohesive phenomenon by thinkers like [Adam Smith](https://iai.tv/articles/adam-smith-was-a-champion-of-the-working-classes-alexandra-digby-auid-2583), David Ricardo, and [Karl Marx](https://iai.tv/articles/the-most-important-book-marx-never-wrote-auid-2083). These many tools, eventually called capitalism, came together at a time when trade was beginning to accelerate with the construction of maritime economies driven largely by the Iberian, Dutch, French, and British empires. Suddenly, growth/ism needed a new program of codification to accommodate all the wealth coming into European economies, both from increasingly tortured serfs and from the huge expanses of land, labor, and commodities from outside the continent, often resulting from conquest in the Americas and Asia.

\_\_\_

Between 1928 and 1970, the USSR enjoyed an average growth rate near 5%, higher than the US average of the time or at any time in the 21st century.

\_\_\_

Capitalism offered a way to not only maintain growth, but to maintain and solidify the hierarchies of extraction, production, and distribution that growthism originally creates, across many miles and cultures. Growthism is a militarization of economies, regimenting an economic chain of command via class structure, like the royalty and nobility, the patricians and religious leaders, businessmen and artisans, to the conscripted soldiers, the bonded peasants or serfs, the enslaved, engaging all hands to increase surpluses.

This weapon of growth was not only material; it was psychological, and came in the form of liberalism, an invention of Enlightenment thinkers like John Locke, Nicolas de Condorcet, and Turgot, and preceded, to some extent, by the work of Islamic thinkers like Ibn Khaldun. This psychological weapon morally justified the systems glued together in the capitalist whole, and did so primarily by using progress narratives and a progressive historiography, furthering the lineage of such ideas that stretched back to Ancient Mesopotamia.

While these progress narratives could calm the nervous system of the rare high-conscientiousness elite, and forestall the agitation of a restive public, they were only a thin coat of paint smeared over an endless series of catastrophes, whether the catastrophe of the millions of people and animals wiped out by Mesopotamian growth, the tens or hundreds of millions wiped out by Roman growth, or the literally trillions of beings wiped out by twentieth and twenty-first century growth. And these deaths, often violent, don’t even account for the living misery of those billions of people who have been born, lived, and died in the endless torment of the most fear-inducing and tedious forms of servitude.

But to understand that growth is separate from capitalism, exists prior to it, and is not even dependent on it, we don’t need to limit ourselves to examples from hundreds or thousands of years ago. The USSR, for instance, was not capitalist, yet it was more effective at achieving economic growth than its capitalist rivals during much of the 20th century. Between 1928 and 1970, the USSR [enjoyed](https://content.csbs.utah.edu/~mli/economics%207004/allen-103.pdf) an average growth rate near 5%, higher than the US average of the time or at any time in the 21st century. This state-communist growthism led to many of the same ills as those that capitalist growthism imposed on the Americas: dispossession and eradication of Native people and cultures, [for one](http://www.newyorker.com/news/dispatch/when-the-soviet-union-freed-the-arctic-%20from-capitalist-slavery), and exploitation of labor and poisoning of land, air, and water, [for others](http://socialist-alliance.org/alliance-%20voices/ecological-disaster-was-ussr-0).

All of these aims have suffered significantly with the reorientation of the university around finance and growth.

\_\_\_

Sidestepping the debate about the extent to which China has adopted “capitalist” or economically liberal policies and practices, there is no doubt that it is less capitalist and liberal than the United States. Even so, China has been much more successful at achieving economic growth than its Western counterpart. Between 1990 and 2024—what some reasonably consider one of the United States’ hypercapitalist phases—the US [experienced](https://www.statista.com/statistics/188165/annual-gdp-growth-of-the-united-states-since-1990/) an average of 2.5% growth. During the same period, China [experienced](https://www.macrotrends.net/global-metrics/countries/chn/china/gdp-growth-rate) [as much](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG?locations=CN) as 8.9% growth on average. China is using some of that growth toward positive ends for the Chinese people, particularly with regard to improving energy and public infrastructure. But, capitalist or not, China is also doing great harm: polluting the environment, pumping out billionaires, and supercharging its military, all while establishing extractive relations to rival its capitalist competitors in other parts of the world.

We can also see this dynamic, the independence of growthism from capitalism, in institutions that are not commercial and not meant to be “capitalist” in any sense, but that have been captured by [the growth fetish](http://articles/the-new-utopianism-auid-763). Take universities. In the US, universities are almost entirely organized as non-profit organizations. That is to say, they are explicitly, legally prohibited from seeking profit. And yet, most major universities have done just that, reorienting their operations around growth: growth of their endowments through stock market investments, growth of property holdings via buying up real estate, growth of student enrollments and tuition fees, relying on their students taking on unstable, usurious, non-dischargeable debts. Universities cut jobs for researchers and teachers as they expand jobs for financial analysts.

\_\_\_

We must abandon growth and demand those things now, and not continue allowing hoarders and grifters to trick us into giving those things up on the promise of their delivery in some future frontier of growth.

\_\_\_

The core mission of the academy is—or has been and should be—the creation and dissemination of new knowledge, hosting funds and platforms for open inquiry and debate, and providing a safe haven for the espousal of ideas that challenge power. All of these aims have suffered significantly with the reorientation of the university around finance and growth. They are not only firing professors and closing down departments, they’re cracking down on students and staff who challenge power, surveilling and even allowing the arrest of people whom they should be protecting, and welcoming technologies like generative AI that have been proven to harm learning outcomes, steal intellectual property, spread misinformation, and pervert research data. As a result, not only has the central mission of academia suffered, but the legitimacy of the institution and its role as a public good have both come under serious scrutiny.

It is, of course, not just universities’ obsession with growth that has done widespread harm. It has become common wisdom to expect commercial entities to orient their operations around compounding profit growth. And yet even when *for*-profit entities fixate on growth above all else—something often encoded in their bylaws and transmitted through their rapidly overturning generations of managers—they, too, lose sight of their original missions of providing socially useful goods and services to the best of their abilities. Instead, in their quest for constant growth, they cut corners, build in product obsolescence, cheat and deceive customers and regulators, corrupt the government, and ultimately break the law, poison communities, and kill.

\_\_\_

Today, when we fix our attention solely on capitalism as the great Satan (or messiah) of civilization, we are like the dog barking at the stone that hit us rather than biting the hand that cast it.

\_\_\_

“Growth” has always been a bribe, an often-empty promise made by rulers to populations so as to defer the necessity of emancipation, of wealth redistribution, clean air and water, of the delivery of genuine economic and political democracy, of fairness and liberty. We must abandon growth and demand those things now, and not continue allowing hoarders and grifters to trick us into giving those things up on the promise of their delivery in some future frontier of growth.

What that means practically is forcing institutions to return to their original missions: for governments to provide a safe haven for citizens, protecting them from threats of both predatory officials and parasitic elites. For universities to provide a safe haven for the production and dissemination of knowledge, even the “dangerous” kind. For commercial entities or state-owned productive entities to deliver socially and environmentally beneficial goods and services, without the externalities of extreme wealth hoarding, labor exploitation, and ecological harm. Whether we call this a degrowth society, steady-state or circular economy, Indigenous or ecosocialist politics, or something else, a society that focuses on well-being and the delivery of meaningful pursuits instead of this mindless zombie march of growth is the only kind that has a future.

This obsession with growth, in both commercial and non-commercial entities, in both capitalist and non-capitalist contexts, has done great harm for thousands of years. Whatever good it has delivered has been far outpaced and undone by the harms that it has rendered. Today, when we fix our attention solely on capitalism as the great Satan (or messiah) of civilization, we are like the dog barking at the stone that hit us rather than biting the hand that cast it. The hand that needs biting today is the hand of all those individuals enamored with their one god, *growth*. While it remains a dire, urgent aim to curtail the harms of capitalism where and however we can, that is not enough. It is growthism, the more ancient and supreme malignancy, that must be eradicated if we are to maintain a habitable planet, and a society that deserves its existence.

Samuel Miller McDonald’s recent book, ***Progress: A History of Humanity’s Worst Idea**,* tells the five- thousand-year history of how the phenomenon of growing economies became entwined with ideas of progress—as well as their relationship to the development of capitalism—and how those forces have wreaked such havoc on the living beings of the world.
