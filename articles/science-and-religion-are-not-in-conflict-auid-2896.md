# Science and religion are not in conflict

*Can science and religion co-exist?*

**By Samuel McKee** | *19th July 2024* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,638 words*

![Science and religion are not in conflict](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Can-science-and-religion-co-exist-2.webp)

---

*The divide between science and religion is often seen to be irreconcilable. The two rarely meet, and most scientists fear being seen as having religious beliefs, fearing their reputation and career. This was no clearer the case than in the 1920s. Conflict between science and religious fundamentalism overshadowed nuance, argues Samual McKee. Scientists such as Arthur Eddington, who maintained science and religion could coexist, have been ignored. We should reject religious fundamentalism and scientific fundamentalism alike.*

Perhaps the most underappreciated period in the history of the dialogue between science and religion is the interwar era of 1918-1939. This is all the more perplexing due to it being the most revolutionary era in modern physics. The figures, books and disputes were as impactful as any time since Newton, yet they rarely feature outside of deep, academic articles.

When considering the relationship between science and religion during this period, the first thought of many centers on the 1925 Scopes Monkey Trial. This infamous chapter has stolen the thunder of the 1920s by skewing our focus towards the newborn fundamentalism. Pentecostal charismatic Christianity was born in Los Angeles at the Asuza Street Revival in 1906 and its corresponding awakening in Wales across the Atlantic. Shortly following this, the Assemblies of God and Elim Pentecostal denominations began, and a new era of Christianity was underway. Biographies of Sir Arthur Eddington, Edward Arthur Milne and other scientists, as well as the writings of CS Lewis suggest that this was no easy time to be a Christian scientist. But thing captures the tension like the infamous Monkey Trial. A schoolteacher in Tennessee dared to teach Darwin’s theory of Evolution in a high school science class and triggered one of the great trials of the century. Clarence Darrow, defending, and William Jennings Bryan, prosecuting, squared off over live radio broadcast, pitting established science against the Bible. Although Bryan’s side technically “won”, ordering a $100 fine be paid, Darrow’s defense wanted to push further up to the high court and make an even grander statement. For religious scientists, this was as ugly as it could get.

For many young scientists with a religious faith, the new fundamentalism forced them to choose between a scientific career and their doctrine.

\_\_\_

Matthew Stanley, in his biography of Eddington, [*Practical Mystic*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/P/bo5485363.html), notes that for many young scientists with a religious faith, the new fundamentalism forced them to choose between a scientific career and their doctrine. Like the fundamentalists, those pushing the conflict thesis of science versus religion had also grown. Ever since John William Draper’s *History of the Conflict between Science and Religion* was published in 1900, the Humanists had drawn their own battle lines. The champions of the early 20th century, such as H.G. Wells and George Bernard Shaw, were castigating the irrationality of religion in the new age by print and radio. Andrew Dickson White, also in 1900, published his own 800-page conflict thesis and by World War One the popular reader was under the impression that science and religion were entirely incompatible and always had been. The fundamentalists were only too happy to agree, as if Newton, Faraday, Clerk-Maxwell, Pascal and Boyle had never written anything about their faith.

However, those studying postgraduate science and religion (as offered at Oxford and Edinburgh) take none of this very seriously. White and Draper’s works are no more than historical novelties, full of errors and bad polemics. The Monkey Trial was a black eye, obscuring our vision of the nuanced dialogue between science and religion that took place from 1921–31.

The first reason this dialogue should command the attention of critics on both sides is the powerful figures on the religious side: [Sir Arthur Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington), [Sir James Jeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Jeans) and [Edward Arthur Milne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Arthur_Milne) are colossal figures in astronomy and astrophysics that do not get their fair due because they are dwarfed by Einstein, Schrodinger, Dirac, Heisenberg et al. However, they each wrote best-selling works on science and philosophy/religion that were immensely impactful at the time.

Each had their own unique perspective. Eddington was a Quaker, with a mystical bent that enamored and confounded those expecting a revisiting of the 19th century’s natural theology. Jeans may not have been truly religious: his personal convictions are a cause of dispute. But his popular contribution, *The Mysterious Universe*,was a masterpiece of reflection on the spiritual questions raised by the new physics.

Both men incurred the wrath of Bertrand Russell. His hit piece, *The Scientific Outlook*, took direct aim at both for popularizing religion out of the recent discoveries of physics. He had recently published his atheist treatise *Why I am not a Christian.* Eddington, perhaps in jest, published his own short book *Why I Believe in God: Science and Religion as a Scientist Sees It* in response. I am sure this made their Cambridge greetings even more colorful. By the time Milne had thrown his hat into the ring, the 1920s and 1930s were full of bestsellers (most of all, Eddington’s *The Nature of the Physical World*) that would be comparable to the best of today’s numbers. This period also saw Georges Lemaitre and Werner Heisenberg burst onto the scene, both of whom had their own strong religious convictions later expressed in their writing.

The second reason for consideration is the forums that were built. Joseph Needham’s seminal collection, funded by the chemist and philanthropist Mihajlo Pupin, *Science, Religion and Reality* in 1925, moved the dialogue forward significantly. This gathering of articles from Cambridge professors was a stunning collection on science and religion. It set the table for the individual astronomers mentioned to write their own works which sold out internationally. Eddington joined Pupin and other dialogue partners in what was a giant academic contribution to the dialogue. Released the same year as the Scopes trial, it is ironic that 1925 was a trigger for academic symposiums on science and religion. Needham went on to write a further volume in 1931: [*The Great Amphibium: Four Lectures on the Position of Religion in a World Dominated by Science*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy/article/abs/great-amphibium-four-lectures-on-the-position-of-religion-in-a-world-dominated-by-science-by-joseph-needham-london-student-christian-movement-press-1931-pp-180-price-6s-net/F759128D5BE5F3CC530DB9851D0BE4EB) as Cambridge ended up becoming the hub for interdisciplinary writing on the subject.

1931 also saw the BBC’s first ever symposium on science and religion. Published in book collection after the broadcast, it is a treasure trove of work by great thinkers of the time, such as Julian Huxley, the son of [Thomas Henry Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Henry_Huxley) – “Darwin’s bulldog”. Sir Arthur Eddington was the headline speaker, having published his immensely popular work *Science and the Unseen World* in 1929. Keeping his competition with Eddington alive, Jeans added further philosophical reflection in *The Universe Around Us,* also released in 1929. With Lemaitre’s 1927 paper on the Big Bang launching cosmology into the discipline it is today, people were asking more questions than ever about belief in God and the new science. Eddington translated this paper into English and added his own discourse on the topic in *The Expanding Universe*, published in 1933.

The 1920s and 1930s were a period of fierce publishing, particularly out of Cambridge, on integrating the new science with philosophical and religious reflections as the physics boom continued. This was anything but a period of displacement of religion by science. As Needham and the BBC endeavoured to do, symposiums were created to investigate the growing questions raised as humanity faced a new world. As the appetite for this grew, so did the prestige of gatherings such as The Gifford Lectures (established in 1887 in Scotland to “promote and diffuse the study of natural theology”). William Inge addressed the conference in 1919, Arthur Balfour spoke there in 1922, Arthur Eddington spoke at the Edinburgh gathering in 1927, JBS Haldane in 1928: all of these featured in Needham’s collection and/or the BBC symposium.

\_\_\_

History has not been kind to the conflict thesis on science and religion. Perhaps the most exciting period in the history of modern science is rife with intense, academic dialogue on the matter from as glorious a collection of physicists and astronomers as one could find.

\_\_\_

Both Neils Bohr and Werner Heisenberg would give [Gifford Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Lectures) after the Second World War. Heisenberg’s *Physics and Philosophy* published later was his own philosophical and religious reflection, as he became bolder in speaking about his own faith later in life.

The reason that this inter-war legacy matters so much is not only because it is compelling evidence against the conflict thesis on science and religion, but also because it set the table for the middle of the century when the dialogue continued to grow in nuance despite the impact of fundamentalism. When Eddington died in 1944, Cambridge University and the Royal Astronomical Society decided that the best way to honour his memory was to establish an annual memorial lectureship focused on the interdisciplinary dialogue between science, philosophy and religion. It still runs to the present and has included Nobel Laureates and prize winners such as Maurice Wilkins, Max Perutz and Sir Roger Penrose.

History has not been kind to the conflict thesis on science and religion. Perhaps the most exciting period in the history of modern science is rife with intense, academic dialogue on the matter from as glorious a collection of physicists and astronomers as one could find. Fundamentalism did not triumph, even at its hottest period. Stanley has written that Eddington alone was responsible for saving the faith of large numbers of scientists on both sides of the Atlantic who were being told to choose a side. One wishes that today we could enjoy symposiums and nuanced gatherings of the quality that was enjoyed by both academics and the general public in this explosive period of scientific breakthrough.
