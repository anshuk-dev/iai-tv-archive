# Freud vs Jung: Trauma extends beyond the self

*The self is political*

**By Andrew Samuels** | *10th February 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,021 words*

![Freud vs Jung: Trauma extends beyond the self](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/10.02.25.jung-freud-thumbnail-2-copy.webp)

---

*Freud and Jung offered two distinctive ideas on the development of the self: one shaped by early experience and the other by the forces of nature imprinted in the archetypes of the collective unconscious. For Freud, trauma can be explained by childhood experience, while Jung looked to the wider collective. But, Andrew Samuels argues, we are political animals. The political world affects the psyche much more than is usually given credit for, and while we should use the ideas of both Freud and Jung, we must also move beyond them.*

Where does psychological distress come from? Why do so many people have ‘emotional issues’? What should be done, and by whom? These contemporary questions are themselves the end results of a century and a half of systematic enquiry embracing – but not culminating in – the work of Freud and Jung.

Those two patriarchs of psychology and psychotherapy represent two poles of the nature-nurture debate, though it is not really as neatly bifurcated as that. Freud’s work on the aetiology of neurosis in family dynamics, in which reconstructing the past history is central, is epistemologically different from that of Jung. Where Freud looked for causal chains in personal history, Jung looked to universal patterns in what we could call the constitutional personality; what is inborn, the acorn from which the tree develops.

The archetypes of the collective unconscious – the layer of the unconscious mind containing shared psychological patterns, or archetypes – are, assuming one agrees that they exist at all, rooted in the body.  In many ways, Jung was the first neuropsychologist. His insight that these patterns that characterise human experience might have corresponding structures in the brain anticipated the key interests of today’s neuroscience.

Freud’s interests were in the relationship between a person and their father and mother, and in the ways in which the parents relate according to the child’s experience of it. While some of his ideas seem extreme, and they are irradiated with sexuality, this family-in-the-past approach to people’s problems in the present has endured and remains a dominant model today. Jung did not ignore the family, but he was much more interested in the ways in which what you could call the problems of humanity made their presence known in the individual. Both were interested in dreams, and both have a lot to say about gender. But these different approaches to the psyche suggest fundamentally different ways of understanding distress - as either wounds from personal history or as disruptions to archetypal patterns of development.

\_\_\_

Many therapists report that their clients and patients are disturbed by the climate crisis as if it were an internal, personal and subjective matter. Which, in a way, it is.

\_\_\_

These matters are relevant to a world in which ‘mental health’ and ‘mental illness’ are ubiquitous. But a third line of exploration is emerging, different from biological facts or personal dynamics. This third line is the ‘psychosocial’ one. The main idea is that the political, economic and social arrangements live vividly in the inner worlds of citizens. Indeed, the division between inner and outer is something that is being disputed by therapists of many persuasions.

Many therapists report that their clients and patients are disturbed by the climate crisis as if it were an internal, personal and subjective matter. Which, in a way, it is. At moments when the political becomes intense – Brexit, 9/11, Trump – the session is often going to revolve around those issues. And even the more personal themes, such as how to manage financially and whether or not to divorce or separate, are infused with the external influences on the issue. From a Jungian perspective, these sorts of events activate archetypal patterns in our collective unconscious, making them deeply personal psychological experiences.

\_\_\_

Class and economic inequality are extraordinarily present when it comes to emotional distress.

\_\_\_

The question of therapists and therapy is important because of developments in the provision of ‘talking therapies’ in many countries, not only Britain. A class divide has opened up in which those who can pay get what is called [depth](http://dictionary.apa.org/depth-psychology), or relational, therapy. The state supplies drug treatments and behavioural remedies. We get what I called in a letter in *The Guardian* ‘second class treatment offered to those deemed to be second class’. The psychosocial domain doesn’t just shape our individual psyche – it shapes the frameworks and relationships we can access to make sense of it.

Class and economic inequality are extraordinarily present when it comes to emotional distress. Even middle-class clients and patients attending therapy sessions are preoccupied with the great themes of class psychology. One thing that has fascinated me for ages is what it means to do ‘better’ than your parents. And, therefore, what does it mean to do worse? Patients and clients worry about these matters all the time. Even when a person breaks away from their family, what the family’s opinion of them is remains central. These class anxieties could be viewed through the lens of family dynamics, or instead as expressions of collective patterns of privilege and deprivation.

Another interest of mine is the whole question of inherited wealth, understood psychologically. I have suggested that there are psychological obstacles within us that make any hope of increased equality quite difficult to envisage. There can be a collective, often unspoken satisfaction to inequality. I call it [‘economic sadism’](http://www.routledge.com/Politics-on-the-Couch-Citizenship-and-the-Internal-Life/Samuels/p/book/9781855753471?srsltid=AfmBOopZ1XlFF8bI3SSIZ3h6EjQEt5Z6RfaIQJA5mYVLIHv-Z85mh_vs) and watch with fascinated horror as this is played out in Trump’s America. Not only is inequality skyrocketing. So is the tendency for rich people to marry one another (called ‘assortative mating’). Personal psychological tendencies and broader social structures can reinforce each other, creating cycles of distress that can't be understood through purely individual or social lenses.

I am a Jungian psychoanalyst and, though I am also known as the chief in-house critic of Jungian psychology, I think there are some ideas of Jung’s that are interesting and useful. One of these is ‘one-sided development’. Think of binaries like thought and feeling, or materialism versus spirituality – for example, becoming too rigidly intellectual and emotionally distant, or pursuing work and money at spiritual cost, or vice versa. It is possible to see that, for an individual, their development has gone too much in one direction. Sometimes, psychological symptoms such as distress can be understood in a Jungian sense as attempts to achieve more of a balance. But what that balance might be is a highly individual and even idiosyncratic matter.

This leads to a second of Jung’s ideas that I would like to highlight: his theory of individuation. Succinctly, this means becoming who you are, or as close to your ‘authentic’ self as possible. Now, this thinking must seem, and is, naïve. Yet it is important when we think of present-day concentration on trauma. Rather than placing trauma within a medicalised model as something to be ‘cured’, it can instead be viewed as part of a pattern of psychological development that is unique to each person. Individuation, as an idea, is intended to be different from mental health and even from illusory notions of maturity. And, crucially, individuation cuts across social realities. It is not something only for the comfortable off. Or the educated.

Jung’s ideas about animus and anima are also relevant to many contemporary themes clustered around sex and gender, including the transgender experience. Anima, the feminine aspect in men, and animus, the masculine aspect in women, disturb gender fixity, undermining ‘male’ and ‘female’. Crudely, everyone is a bit of both. So bisexuality and gender transition are not impositions on what is going on in a person; they are extensions of it. They are not pathologies but the psyche’s integration and exploration of its gendered elements. Individual distress connected with sexual and gender fluidity is never going to be absent, but our connections and opinions change when such distress is understood as also coming from collective and social attitudes.

At the beginning of this short piece, I mentioned the nature-nurture debate. I think that what is happening in the psychotherapies generally is a ‘political turn’. This means, not only that the social order is recognised as alive within the individual psyche, but that therapists may have something to contribute to thinking about society and politics generally. I have been fortunate to work as a political consultant to leading politicians, parties and activist groups – and with senior management in the NHS. Whilst therapy thinking is not always received with open arms and minds, it is often seen to have a place. Imagine a spectrum. At one end sits the statistician; at the other, we find a therapist, each offering very different modes of insight into the complexities of human society, from numerical data to the lived experience of human meaning.

In my consulting room, leadership is the political theme that gets my clients and patients the most worked up. In psychotherapy, we use the term 'good-enough'. This is taken from developmental theory, where it is used in relation to a parent figure who is neither idealised as perfect nor denigrated in an angry way for having failed. The psychoanalyst [Donald Winnicott](http://psychoanalysis.org.uk/our-authors-and-theorists/donald-woods-winnicott) spent decades observing mothers and babies and noted that perfect attunement wasn’t merely impossible, but also undesirable. Small, manageable shortcomings in maternal care helped the infant develop resilience and its independent sense of self. Failure is, as parents know, inevitable. The question is whether children manage to tolerate their parents' failures. In politics, the place where we urgently need something like good-enough is in [relation to our leaders](http://aras.org/sites/default/files/docs/PoliticsinaTraumatizedWorld.pdf). At present, we tend to build them up and idealise them so that they can have dictatorial powers even in a democratic society. Then, as we saw in the case of Tony Blair, the populace and the media work together to destroy the hitherto invulnerable leader (who was by no means blameless in his downfall).

\_\_\_

What *looks like* apathy is actually a pervasive sense of powerlessness, often coupled with intensely guilty self-criticism.

\_\_\_

Good-enoughness implies a relation to and acceptance of failure - Winnicott said that the mother must fail the baby but in the baby's own way. We can take the idea out of the family context so that, without claiming that leaders are nothing more than parents writ large, we could try to do something new with their failures. The idea, which is relevant to leadership in any situation such as within organisations, is to release leaders from the shackles of always having to be in control of things and always having to be right. I think many of us know that such strength in a leader is always a compensation for profound insecurity and confusion. They could start to be less heroic, less macho, and become more collaborative with citizens, more 'sibling leaders'. They can fail the country but in the country's own way. As Rumi put it, 'failure is the key to the kingdom'.

To alter our ideas about leaders also means to alter our ideas about ourselves as citizens. A lot has been written recently about apathy and a loss of hope and belief in politics. Fair enough! But, listening to clients and to people who attend my 'political clinics' at which feelings about politics are shared and worked with, I'd like to suggest that the problem with our polity is not apathy. The problem is, rather, too much passion, too many aspirations, a belief in perfect solutions - leading, inevitably, to disappointment and withdrawal. What *looks like* apathy is actually a pervasive sense of powerlessness, often coupled with intensely guilty self-criticism. Just as Winnicott saw the parent-infant relationship as in need of manageable failures, and just as Freud and Jung understood psychological trauma through the different frameworks of personal history and collective patterns, we can learn from both personal and collective frameworks when working with political distress.

This is where the insights of psychotherapy’s psychosocial turn might be useful. The personal and political malaise are inseparable. If we can accept that political perfection is unattainable, if we ask of ourselves only that we be good-enough citizens (just as we can only hope for good-enough leaders), we may be freed from the sense of depressive despair that mires us at present, so that political hope can reawaken.
