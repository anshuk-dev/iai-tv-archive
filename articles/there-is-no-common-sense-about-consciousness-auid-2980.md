# There is no common sense about consciousness

*If a tree falls in a forest...*

**By Justin Sytsma** | *25th October 2024* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,928 words*

![There is no common sense about consciousness](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/consciousness-and-qualia.webp)

---

*Theories of consciousness like dualism and panpsychism often assume that conscious experiences involve acquaintance with mental qualities – qualities wholly internal to the mind. These qualities are supposed to be totally obvious to anyone who introspects, and so part of our “common sense” picture of the mind. Yet, argues Justin Sytsma, people who are untrained in analytic philosophy don’t find these qualities obvious at all. Philosophers of consciousness should therefore beware of making assertions about “common sense” in their arguments, for “common sense” turns out to be a shifting ground, even when the topic is our own mental life.*

“In attempting to explode the myth I shall probably be taken to be denying well-known facts about the mental life of human beings, and my plea that I aim at doing nothing more than rectify the logic of mental-conduct concepts will probably be disallowed as mere subterfuge.”   
 (Gilbert Ryle, [*The Concept of Mind*](https://www.routledge.com/The-Concept-of-Mind-60th-Anniversary-Edition/Ryle/p/book/9780415485470?srsltid=AfmBOooiHeS6PzpRU5SqVNvSTickriXOXzNwdQ4fee5xr_qrjyAdiwGv), 1949/2009, p. 6)

You’ve probably heard the following thought experiment before: If a tree falls in the woods and no one is there to hear it, does it make a sound? Indeed, this question now borders on a cliché. Nonetheless, thinking about it can offer a nice entry point into understanding a particular notion of consciousness that has played a central role in contemporary philosophy of mind. This is the notion of *phenomenal consciousness*. More on this in a minute.

\_\_\_

Can there be unheard sounds or, similarly, unseen colors, unfelt pains, and so on?

\_\_\_

So, does the tree falling make a sound or not? (Or, more generally, can there be *unheard sounds* or, similarly, [*unseen colors*](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/5141/), [*unfelt pains*](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/14548/), and so on?) Let’s set aside complications, such as that the wildlife in the forest might hear it, even if no humans are around. As such, imagine that the falling of the tree goes completely unheard.

I’ve been asking my students this question for the past decade when introducing the topic of consciousness. And, while anecdotal, I’ve observed an interesting and quite consistent pattern over the years. In my introductory courses, the overwhelming response is affirmative, often with upwards of 90% of students answering that the tree falling makes a sound. Indeed, in discussion, these students tend to treat this as rather obvious. Things start to change in my intermediate courses. While the majority still answer that the tree falling makes a sound, a sizable minority (roughly 40% give or take) deny this. This shift continues in my advanced courses, with a majority now denying that there is a sound, although a notable minority continues to give the affirmative answer. And, in discussion, the deniers – now in the majority – tend to treat *this* as obvious.

The orthodox philosophical and scientific response is to deny that the tree falling makes a sound. Here, for instance, is a snippet from one of the earliest discussions I could find. In the “Notes & Queries” section of *Scientific American* (April 5, 1884, p. 218), a reader asks our focal question. In response the editors offer an amusingly inconsistent answer: “Sound is vibration, transmitted to our senses through the mechanism of the ear, and recognized as sound only at our nerve centers. The falling of the tree or any other disturbance will produce vibration of the air. If there be no ears to hear, there will be no sound.”

Of course, if “sound is vibration” and if the tree falling “will produce vibration,” then it would seem that it would generate a sound whether or not there are ears to hear. And yet the editors conclude with the opposite answer – that there will be no sound. Charitably, we can interpret them as distinguishing between two senses of “sound”, one that can go unheard and one that only exists when heard. This is a point commonly noted in the philosophical discussions. Often this is pushed a touch further, treating the *vibration* sense of sound as the stimuli that causally leads to the sound being produced in us (the *heard* sound). Further, as with the editors of S*cientific American*, it is common to treat the heard sound as being what is ultimately at issue for the question.

This distinction offers a potential explanation for the pattern I described above: It might be that responses shift as people engage with further philosophy of mind because they tend to shift from interpreting the question in terms of one sense of the word “sound” (vibration) to the other (heard). I don’t think this is the primary explanation, however. Rather, when I talk with beginning students, and ask them why they think that the falling tree makes a sound when no one is around to hear it, I get the sense that their answer is driven by a certain picture of the world, which we can call the *naïve picture*. This contrasts with what we can call the *phenomenal picture*, which seems to drive the standard philosophical response that the falling tree makes no sound if no one is around.

Starting with the naïve picture, it should be noted that terming the view “naïve” isn’t meant pejoratively. Rather, the term reflects that this is the picture that we tend to have before being trained into an alternative view, perhaps through philosophical study. Put another way, the naïve picture is meant to correspond with our pre-theoretical, common-sense beliefs. The basic idea for the naïve picture is that when we perceive (for instance, when we hear a tree falling), we stand in a perceptual relation to mind-independent qualities. Put more simply, this view distinguishes between the perceptual act (e.g., *hearing*) and the qualities perceived (e.g., *sounds*), and in cases like for the tree falling in the forest, places those qualities in the world outside the perceiver’s mind/brain.

Importantly, the naïve picture is not simply using “sound” to refer to physical stimuli (e.g., vibrations) but instead to the qualities that we hear – the qualities that we are acquainted with in auditory perception. Separating the qualities from our perception of them, this picture allows that they can exist without being perceived, including that there can be unheard sounds. Correspondingly, it allows that the tree falling made a sound even if nobody heard it.

The phenomenal picture, by contrast, separates the physical stimuli from the sounds we hear. On this view, the qualities that we are acquainted with are not mind-independent, but are mental: they are held to be produced by the mind/brain in response to the physical stimuli. Thus, sounds are not to be associated with the state of the air, but the state of the mind. The result is that there can be no unheard sounds on this view, since to be a sound is to be heard.

\_\_\_

In comparison to the naïve picture, the phenomenal picture shifts qualities like the sound of a tree falling from the external world to the internal world of the mind/brain.

\_\_\_

Earlier I mentioned the concept of phenomenal consciousness. Although this is a somewhat messy notion in philosophy, the standard understanding corresponds with – indeed, I believe largely falls out of – the phenomenal picture. To see this, we need to introduce one more technical term: *qualia*. [Qualia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualia/) (otherwise known as *phenomenal qualities*) are qualities that we are acquainted with like sounds, colors, pains, and the like, with the key addition that these qualities are understood to be mental. Associating such qualities in the mind/brain with mental states, the standard view is that a mental state is phenomenally conscious just in case it has qualia, and that a being is phenomenally conscious just in case it has phenomenally conscious mental states.

Connecting up the dots, in comparison to the naïve picture, the phenomenal picture shifts qualities like the sound of a tree falling from the external world to the internal world of the mind/brain. The technical term “qualia” then offers a label for discussing such qualities *as they are understood on the assumption of the phenomenal picture*.

While the existence of qualia is a topic for debate in analytic philosophy, it tends to be assumed, with attention instead focusing on how (or if) they can be explained in physical terms and what implications their existence would have for our understanding of ourselves and the broader world. Indeed, explaining qualia in our ordinary scientific ways is what David Chalmers calls “[the hard problem of consciousness](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-conscious-mind-9780195117899)” – where “hard”, here, is essentially used a euphemism for “seemingly impossible.” In line with this, a number of prominent philosophers have argued that if qualia exist, then there must be more to the world than just the physical, leading them to embrace a position such as dualism or [pansychism](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/consciousness-and-fundamental-reality-9780197766392). These are hefty conclusions, to say the least, which serves to highlight a key question: *Why should we believe in qualia?*

Philosopher Amy Kind offers a concise answer in her essay “[How to Believe in Qualia](https://philpapers.org/rec/KINHTB)”: “Why believe in qualia? Because our every experience reveals their existence.” In other words, the standard response to this key question is to say that the existence of qualia is simply obvious. And not just obvious *after* you’ve adopted a particular type of view or reasoned through things in a certain sort of way, but obvious before all of that. The existence of qualia is supposed to be *pretheoretically* obvious. It is supposed to be manifest to us just from undergoing ordinary everyday episodes, such as hearing something fall.

\_\_\_

How did so many philosophers come to misplace common sense, taking it to fall on the side of the phenomenal picture rather than the naïve picture?

\_\_\_

Here’s where things get interesting. Note that the above claim is tantamount to asserting that it is actually the phenomenal view that should be considered the “naïve” view. If our every experience reveals the existence of qualia, then we would expect people to tend to operate with the phenomenal picture by default, such that this picture would coincide with our pre-theoretical, common-sense judgments about perceptual episodes. As we saw above, however, this is also what is often assumed about the naïve picture! So which one is it?

While I lack the space to delve into it here, I believe [the evidence points toward the naïve picture better capturing our commonsense understanding](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/16030/). Further, this strikes me as the way that ordinary perceptual episodes seem. When I hear something, for example, my attention is directed outward toward the objects producing the sound. Similarly, when I see something, say a red apple, the visual qualities I am acquainted with – such as the redness – seem to belong to the surface of the object. And, personally, [no matter how intently I try to “introspect” this quality, the red does not strike me as being in my head](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2214186). I expect it is the same for you.

Assuming this is accurate, what happened? How did so many philosophers come to misplace common sense, taking it to fall on the side of the phenomenal picture rather than the naïve picture? I suspect that this muddle owes in part to rather general difficulties we have with adopting naïve perspectives. It turns out that we’re often just quite poor at remembering how we thought about things before training and the passage of time. And this can promote projecting a highly technical position onto others. By itself, this isn’t necessarily a problem; but when the main support for that position rests on a claim about it being pretheoretically obvious, this difficulty can result in begging the question. And this is far from benign: it offers a false sense of support for a theoretical construct that has led many analytic philosophers down the primrose path to dubious metaphysical positions like dualism or panpsychism.
