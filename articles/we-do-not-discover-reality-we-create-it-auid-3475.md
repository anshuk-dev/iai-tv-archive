# We do not discover reality, we create it

*Our theories help make the world, not uncover it*

**By Manuel Delaflor** | *23rd January 2026* | *Read time: approx. 16 mins* | *3,122 words*

![We do not discover reality, we create it](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/we-do-not-discover-reality-we-create-it.webp)

---

*We tend to think reality is out there waiting to be discovered. But philosopher **Manuel Delaflor** argues that experience, meaning, and ontology are active constructions, not passive receptions of a knowable world. Once we see that categories are created, not discovered, realism gives way to responsibility, and the question shifts from what reality is, to what reality are we choosing to create.*

My sister is a graphic designer, someone who has spent two decades swimming in colour palettes. She once laid out some blues on her screen and asked me to name them. Azure, cerulean, cobalt, sapphire, she rattled off, pointing at squares I could only call “blue.” To her, they were as different as a dog and a horse. Her visual system had carved the continuous spectrum into slices mine simply could not access. That moment has stayed with me. If her blues are not my blues, and if training made the difference, then what else have I been missing? What categories do we walk around believing we discovered in the world, when in fact we manufactured them ourselves, through language, through habit, through purpose? And if this is true of something as basic as colour, what does it mean for everything else we think we see?

We tell ourselves a flattering story: out there is a world, pre-sorted into kinds, and our job is to discover those kinds. Rocks as rocks. Faces as faces. Colours sit waiting in the spectrum like crayons in a box. This is the myth of discovery, and it runs so deep we rarely notice it. It feels grounded, and we need grounding. It feels like the opposite of arrogance. Right? But examine it closely and the story collapses. I give you a hint on the reason: when something makes sense, it never means it is right. It means it clicks into place inside your existing maps. And that is why when we go around pretending to be certain about what “makes sense” is the easiest form of self-deception.

Return with me to the blue. Russian speakers make an obligatory distinction between lighter blues (*goluboy*) and darker blues (*siniy*), a division as fundamental as the English distinction between green and blue. This touches on [the linguistic relativity Benjamin Lee Whorf hinted at](https://dn790009.ca.archive.org/0/items/languagethoughtr00whor/languagethoughtr00whor.pdf). Now, of course we could console ourselves by saying this is just language, just words, but that consolation evaporates the moment we look deeper. The implication is merciless. The blues my sister sees were not discovered. They were instantiated. Brought into being by attention, vocabulary, and purpose. And it goes further still. Researchers [Gabriele Jordan and John Mollon report](https://vision.psychol.cam.ac.uk/jdmollon/papers/JordanMollon2019Tetrachromacy.pdf) rare cases consistent with *tetrachromacy*: four cone photopigments instead of three, and in some cases colour discriminations most of us cannot make. These individuals are not seeing the same spectrum more accurately. They are seeing a different spectrum altogether.

\_\_\_

Even the most fundamental boundary we take for granted, the boundary of the self, is an achievement of an active model.

\_\_\_

But colour is only where this begins. There is a story, perhaps apocryphal, traced to Joseph Banks during James Cook’s 1770 voyage. Some Aboriginal Australians did not respond to the distant presence of HMS Endeavour. Marshall McLuhan later turned this [into an epistemological parable](https://archive.org/details/ETC0624). Whether or not the story is literally accurate, it captures something essential about how our cognition works. They may have lacked the model to instantiate it as an object worthy of attention. A giant vessel for hundreds of humans is not a concept in the mind of those without the technology to build it. And maybe, if we have no category for Y, then there is no Y to be seen. Perhaps they saw something, some disturbance on the horizon, but the “isness” we attach to objects, the recognition of what a thing “is”, requires a category to hold it, and without the category, the object cannot fully arrive.

And even when the category exists, the computation can fail. Anthropologist Colin Turnbull [recounts a moment like this](https://ia601400.us.archive.org/5/items/forestpeople00turn/forestpeople00turn.pdf) with the BaMbuti people of the Congo rainforest, who live in such dense foliage that they rarely see more than a few meters ahead. When Turnbull took a young man named Kenge to the open savannah, Kenge saw buffalo grazing in the distance and asked what kind of insects they were. As they drove closer and the “insects” grew into massive beasts, Kenge was terrified. He had never needed to compute size constancy over long distances. His visual system had no practice making that correction. For Kenge, the buffalo were small because they looked small. Not because his eyes were broken. Because his model had never been built. And this is not a story about primitive minds encountering the modern world. It is a story about how all minds work. In the late 1950s, Sidney Bradford underwent surgery that [restored sight after decades of profound impairment](https://www.richardgregory.org/papers/recovery_blind/recovery-from-early-blindness.pdf). Light reached his retinas. His visual cortex responded. But, surprisingly, Bradford could not see. He could not recognise objects he knew intimately by touch. He could not judge distances. He had the hardware, but he had never built the model, and without the model, the data was noise. And it gets stranger. Even when the model runs, experience itself can be absent. Lawrence Weiskrantz at Oxford documented patients with damage to their primary visual cortex who insisted they were completely blind in parts of their visual field. They saw nothing. They experienced nothing. But when forced to guess about objects in their “blind” region, they guessed correctly far above chance. They could point to things they could not see. [Weiskrantz called this blindsight](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959438896800754?via%3Dihub). The machinery was running. The experience was absent. Somewhere between the computation and the cognition, something failed to arrive. This is why sight is not reception. Sight is construction.

Consider now the inverse: experience flooding in without any input at all. Charles Bonnet syndrome afflicts people whose vision is failing. They know their sight is deteriorating. And yet they begin to experience vivid, detailed visual hallucinations: faces, figures, elaborate geometric patterns, sometimes entire scenes. They know these visions are not “real.” They are not psychotic. But the imagery is there, rich, and structured, generated entirely from within. Conscious seeing does not require the world to be sending signals. And lest we think this is pathology, consider the experience that visits every one of us, every night. In dreams, we inhabit fully coherent worlds that feel utterly real while we are in them, generated without the world feeding us the scene. No light hits the retina. No sound enters the ear. Yet we see, we hear, we interact with entire worlds where we feel, we fear, we desire. The realist demands a referent for experience. But every sleeping human demonstrates nightly that the referent is unnecessary. So experience is generated, content is constructed. But surely, you might say, meaning is anchored to what we see? Consider prosopagnosia. Some people see faces perfectly clearly and cannot recognise them. The eyes are there. The nose is there. The mouth is there. The face, as object, is successfully built. But the integration that screams “that’s Maria!” does not fire. Faces become interchangeable masks. Seeing is one achievement. Recognition is another. And, again, both turn to be constructions.

\_\_\_

If you dig down looking for the solid floor of reality, you will not find it in physics or in biology. You will only find more models. There is no floor.

\_\_\_

OK, you say, but surely the boundary between “me” and “not me” is fundamental, hard-wired, beyond question? After certain strokes, patients look at their own paralysed arm and insist it works perfectly. Some will tell you the arm belongs to someone else, the nurse perhaps, or a sibling who somehow snuck into the bed. They invent elaborate stories to explain why a stranger’s limb is attached to their shoulder. And this is not only pathology. In healthy subjects, a rubber glove and a paintbrush can induce ownership over a fake hand in minutes, as [Botvinick and Cohen](https://www.nature.com/articles/35784) demonstrated in 1998. The illusion is so robust that people wince when the rubber hand is threatened. Move a camera and headset in the right configuration and you can displace the felt location of the self entirely. These are not party tricks. They demonstrate that even the most fundamental boundary we take for granted, the boundary of the self, is an achievement of an active model. Not a given.

If you have been nodding along, you have probably felt you were finally getting the real story. The brain behind the curtain. Neuroscience as solid ground beneath constructed experience. Notice the language you accepted without flinching: visual cortex, prosopagnosia, circuits, retina, machinery. You probably felt you had found the floor. This is a relatively new understanding, that what we see is constructed, and even famous researchers like [Anil Seth](https://iai.tv/video/the-science-of-consciousness-with-anil-seth) and [Donald Hoffman](https://iai.tv/video/perception-as-a-fantasy-donald-hoffman) stop around here, one focusing on the brain, the other on conscious agents. But then they have just swapped the myth of the Objective World for the myth of the Objective Brain, or the Objective Mind. Pick your idol. If you are there, you traded one realism for another and did not notice the exchange.

Look closer. What are “neurons”? What are “synapses”? What are “photoreceptors”? A substratum? Or just another layer? We are using the Biological Toolkit to explain the Experiential Toolkit. We are using one model to examine another. And if you ask why the biological model is correct, what will you say? You will point to chemistry. You will point to physics. And if I ask why those are correct? There is an ancient problem in philosophy called the Münchhausen Trilemma, named after the baron who claimed to pull himself out of a swamp by his own hair. It says that any attempt to justify a claim must end in one of three disasters: infinite regress, circularity, or arbitrary assertion. The scientific realist says neurons are real, unlike the colours they merely produce. But ask why neurons are real. They will point to empirical observation. Ask why empirical observation is reliable. They will point to the success of science. Ask why success equals correct. They will point to... another model. And another. Until, eventually, they must either admit the chain never ends, or loop back to their starting assumption. The trilemma does not refute realism by some clever trick. It reveals that the demand for model-independent grounding is itself the problem. There is no view from “outside all models.” There is no place to stand that is not already a toolkit. If you dig down looking for the solid floor of reality, you will not find it in physics or in biology. You will only find more models. There is no floor. [Turtles all the way down](https://iai.tv/video/turtles-all-the-way-down).

So let me offer a different frame. Let us say that worldviews are just toolkits. Give a culture a hammer, and for them, everything starts to look like a nail. The toolkit is not just instruments; it is narrative, the stories we tell ourselves about what matters, what counts as evidence, what success looks like, who we should be. [Model Dependent Ontology](https://iai.tv/articles/truth-is-the-most-dangerous-fantasy-our-species-ever-invented-auid-3263) says that the very hunt for an “ultimate foundation” is the mistake. There is no final layer that is not already another toolkit doing another job for another purpose. What changes your life is not discovering what reality “ultimately is” but noticing that toolkits are *choices* with costs. Other toolkits are not errors; they are resources and blind spots. Thomas Kuhn saw this mechanism at work in scientific revolutions. A paradigm is not just a set of facts. It is a toolkit that defines what counts as a fact in the first place. The story selects the tool. The tool selects what the story even notices. And each successful application of the toolkit produces a satisfying click of confirmation. See? The world really is made of nails. The more we use it, the more natural it seems. The more natural it seems, the more invisible the toolkit becomes. Until we forget we are holding a hammer at all. And this strikes me as incredibly naïve, and incredibly sad.

\_\_\_

When we mistake our models for the way “things are,” we freeze the world. We look at the economy, at borders, at money, and we act as if they are as immutable as colours.

\_\_\_

This is how ontologies entrench themselves. Not by corresponding to a pre-sorted world, but by working well enough, often enough, that they stop feeling like choices. The physicist’s particles are as much an artifact of the particle toolkit as the mystic’s energies are of the energy toolkit. They differ vastly in predictive grip, error-correction, and the constraints they respect. But neither escapes the trilemma by achieving model-independence. Both are building navigable territories with different instruments. Change the instrument and you end up with another territory. And then narratives reinforce the toolkits. Every time a prediction lands, every time a manipulation succeeds, we pat ourselves on the back. “Well done,” we exclaim with fervour, “this is how things really are.” The construct acquires the glow of independence. Utility becomes confused with correspondence. And the trilemma vanishes from view because we stopped looking at the foundations we were standing on.

But perhaps you still want to retreat to measurement. Surely metrics give us objectivity. Consider the coastline paradox. Measure Britain’s coast with a 100-kilometre ruler and you get one length. Use a 50-kilometre ruler and the length increases. Push the scale down to meters and the number explodes. There is no determinate length converging at the bottom. Benoit Mandelbrot formalised the point mathematically in 1967, and it remains as unsettling now as it was then. Without specifying scale, “length of coastline” is not a discoverable fact, there is no “true length” to find. It is just an artefact of the method. And this abstract geometric trick turns lethal when we apply it to our lives. Even our most consequential social metrics follow this pattern. GDP does not reveal economic health; it operationalises one particular model of what economic health means, a model its own inventor, Simon Kuznets, [expressly warned should not be read as welfare](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/natincome_1934/19340104_nationalinc.pdf). Change what you count, and the “economy” changes with it. IQ does not discover intelligence; it operationalises a specific, culturally contingent notion of cognitive performance. The ruler creates what it measures. And yet, we stand there marvelling at the objectivity of the result.

This is where the real danger emerges. When we mistake our models for the way “things are,” we freeze the world. We look at the economy, at borders, at money, and we act as if they are as immutable as colours. We let the tools we invented dictate the terms of our survival. We starve because the metric says there is no “money,” as if money were a resource we mine rather than a token we track on a ledger. We are stuck in a prison of our own design, convinced there is no escape because we have forgotten we hold the key. E.O. Wilson [put it starkly](https://archive.org/details/originsofcreativ0000wils_n7n0): “We have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions and god-like technology.” We wield powers of creation and destruction our ancestors would have called divine, yet we guide them with the fearful, dogmatic software of a primate seeking certainty. We cling to our models because we are afraid of the void. But this dogmatism drags us into stagnation. It turns difference into heresy. After all… If I hold the one and only correct view, then you are not just using a different toolkit. You are wrong. You are broken. You are the enemy.

Now, and this is important. No, I am not saying there is no world. I am not saying everything is arbitrary. I am not offering a permission slip for believing whatever you want. Actions have consequences, and they are what matter. Try building a bridge out of cheese and the results will quickly show you the strength of your model. You do not trip on “the world.” You trip on your model’s failure. And that failure is the veto, the part you do not get to negotiate.

What I am saying is that our goals decide the kind of questions we ask, and the questions decide the kind of answers we can even notice. I am saying that we do not discover ontologies, we instantiate them. Some instantiations work brilliantly: they let us land rovers on Mars, cure diseases, communicate across languages. Others fail spectacularly: they lead us to burn witches, drink bleach, destroy ecosystems, get stuck with endless wars. This is what Model Dependent Ontology makes visible. Instead of objectivity or relativism; responsibility. Because once you understand that categories are made, not found, you can no longer hide behind “that’s just how things are.” Every category becomes a choice. Every toolkit implies values. The colours you see, the faces you recognise, the body you inhabit, the metrics you trust: all of them are accomplishments of a running system, not deliveries from an external order. Everything you see is a construct. Built to predict possibilities and constraints. And there is no bottom. There is no final reassurance hiding underneath the next layer down.

And this is where it becomes uncomfortable. Because once categories are made rather than found, other people are no longer simply failing to see the same world correctly. They are operating with different toolkits, aimed at different problems, under different constraints. Some of them work, some others fail. Some of them harm. Some of them collapse under operational constraint. But none of them were ever wrong *because* they failed to mirror an imagined objective order. They failed because they did not work. This is the moment where certainty stops being innocent.

We have spent millennia pretending to be archaeologists, carefully brushing dust off truths we believed were buried there before us. But there was never anything buried. We were building the ruins as we dug. Every ontology, every metric, every category was an intervention, not a discovery. We did not find the world, we fabricated ways of navigating possibilities and constraints, then mistook those navigational tools for a terrain. But there is no final layer waiting underneath this one. No reassurance hiding beneath the next explanation, no place where the digging ends and the ground finally becomes “real”.

This is terrifying exactly once. After that, it is unavoidable. Responsibility replaces realism, not as a moral upgrade, but as a structural consequence. The question is no longer what the world *is*. The question is what your models do, what they blind you to, and what they cost. And once you see that you have always been building, not discovering, there is no retreat back into innocence. You are already choosing. The only question left is whether you will keep pretending you are not.
