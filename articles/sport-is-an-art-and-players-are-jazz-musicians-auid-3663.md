# Sport is an art, and players are jazz musicians

*Sport cannot be reduced to 0s and 1s*

**By Sara Heinämaa** | *26th August 2026* | *Read time: approx. 11 mins* | *2,206 words*

![Sport is an art, and players are jazz musicians](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/sport-is-art-and-players-are-jazz-musicians-2.webp)

*There is an increasing movement in competitive sports to rely on data and optimisation to make decisions on player performance. But professor of philosophy **Sara Heinämaa** and former NHL hockey coach **Alpo Suhonen** argue that this is a mistake with dangerous consequences. We should not treat players as mere biological organisms, but expressive agents, each like an improvising jazz musician on the field. Only then can we escape the unimaginative and clumsy coaching strategies plaguing contemporary sports and enable creative play on the field.*

Contemporary sports management is dominated by idealized notions of human interaction. Competing, playing and games are paralleled to warfare and combat—football teams are said to “battle it out” on the field to win; training and coaching are modelled on the processes of industrial production, and players are treated as mere physical bodies needing to be optimised. The life of the athlete is divided into separate activities and exercises in the same manner that the production of a car was organized into assembly units at the Ford factories in the early 1900s. One programme and one set of specialists is needed for muscle conditioning, another one for speed development, a third one for mental training, etc. The head coach is assumed to have an overview of the process as a whole but, in reality, no one can control such an imaginary assembly machine.

The dominant paradigm operates on the assumption that all sports activities—from basic exercises to final performances in front of an audience—can be adequately understood and interpreted by the quantitative, probabilistic and computational methods of the natural sciences.

This view needs to be challenged. It is problematic in principle but especially [damaging for all ball, field and team games](https://like.fi/kirjat/valmentaja-ja-filosofi). Namely, such models demand, in their very establishment, that the game be reduced to calculable numerical functions and systems of such functions. The capacities of basketball players, for example, are measured by their “personal time of possession” and “touch duration,” but such measures cannot predict much, if anything, about the performances of the players when they are given new roles in the game in unpredictable situations. To apply such models to real life, the freedom of the players and the autonomy of the teams must be minimized. Leadership is to be understood either as control and discipline or else as artificial top-down motivation that trickles down from mental trainers or psychological consultants to the players. For this end, the open-ended and unpredictable dynamism of real play-acts must be neglected or else conceptualized as an additional achievement. Such downplaying of the players’ free aspirations, interests and decisions ultimately equates to [a denial of their creativity and ignorance of their talent](https://www.academia.edu/123674565/Personal_Communal_and_Temporal_Structures_of_Vocational_Life_A_Husserlian_Account).

In reality, [playing a sports game is a highly dynamic human activity](https://iai.tv/video/the-game-of-life); understanding and managing it requires intimate cooperation of several different professionals. The life sciences alone are not enough, since athletes are not just biological organisms or psycho-physical systems, but concrete human agents who experience the world in their own unique ways and estimate it constantly as an open whole that not only enables, but also demands their participation in it.

A profound reform in sports culture is thereby urgently needed, and this requires insights from the human sciences and philosophy. But even more urgently, sports need a new understanding of the *expressive and performative* dimensions of competition. This understanding is not to be found at universities or in laboratories, but on theatre stages. Sports share crucial components with the expressive arts of theatre and dancing.

As a new framework for the development of sports, an [existential-phenomenological understanding](https://www.routledge.com/Phenomenology-and-the-Extreme-Sport-Experience/Brymer-Schweitzer/p/book/9780367374501) of human beings is necessary. This framework is particularly appropriate for the reform in team sports because it analyzes human beings, not as exchangeable parts of social assemblages, but as unique individuals who constantly develop their relations to the world as a whole. Moreover, existential phenomenology allows us to avoid the traditional oppositions between mind and body, thought and matter, reason and emotion, culture and nature. It conceives the athlete as a minded body that permanently connects itself to other similar bodies in volitional action and sensory affection.

Phenomenologically considered, the sport field is [organized around the athlete’s operative body](https://www.academia.edu/32138386/Embodiment_and_bodily_becoming) which serves as the constant starting point of orientation and movement, in relation to which all other locations are merely optional. The significant foci of this field also involve the moving bodies of the other players, teammates as well as opponents, and the positions and movements of the game tools, be that a basketball, a baseball bat, or a hockey stick. These main themes are distinct from the other elements of sports, from hailing and booing audiences, music, lighting, broadcasting units and TV cameras, commercials... For the players, the two groups of objects differ not (only) in their physical features, but more crucially in their functional value and emotive significance.

The high jump arena, for example, is structured by the bar to be crossed and the different distances that the jumper can take in relation to it. Similarly, what distinguishes the basketball court from all other spaces is not its physical measures or objective locations, but the dynamic structure formed by the ball, the baskets, the spatial dimensions, and the changing positions and formations of the players. The ice hockey field is further structured by the hockey sticks that operate as extensions of the arms of the players, and in fencing the operative field is articulated by one of the three weapons: epee, foil and saber. Many surrounding entities also belong to the overall experience of the players, but their roles are marginal in game situations. The main themes of the match are the functional and value-laden objects immediately given to the players’ perceiving-moving bodies: obstacles, passes, tools, teammates, opponents, positions reached, lines opened...

The performance of a hockey player or a figure skater on ice cannot adequately be explained by the fact that their feet carry out commands that issue from their brain, nor by the fact that they apply previously acquired knowledge in novel practical situations. The only movements that are relevant to the understanding of their unique performances are governed by themselves, not their brain cells, coaches or managers. Moreover, all skaters have unique personal moving patterns which communicate their overall relationships to relevant objects on the field. The teammates and opponents need to understand these patterns in order to respond to them adequately and efficiently.

Furthermore, the bodies of athletes are not merely instruments of their wills or objects of their professional know-how, but are also, and more fundamentally, irreplaceable sources of values and unique starting points of actions. The playing body is above all the material expression and manifestation of the athlete’s personal aspirations, interests and experiences.

The aim of maximizing power and speed requires that athletes “automatize” their movements. A common-sense way of conceptualizing this process is to consider it as *the flow phenomenon*, which assumes that it depends on innate talent and externally governed training. The existential-phenomenological analysis provides a more detailed account of such “automation”: In training sessions, the athletes must first volitionally and very consciously perform the rehearsed movements that they are supposed to learn, as if in “slow motion.” The trainer or coach may even grasp the arm or the leg of the athlete and set it in the optimal position, the one to be acquired and habitualized via repetition and refinement. We know this practice best from ballet classes where the master walks around and physically “puts right” the positions of the pupils. Similar modelling and mimicking processes are crucial to all movement learning and all sports, and they all depend on gestural bodily communication.

A further condition of competing sports is often completely ignored: all playing depends on *improvisation*. The word already points at the core of the matter: improvizing agents are able to proceed without ready-made models, preparations and calculations and are thus capable of responding to unforeseen circumstances. They are not preoccupied by habitual constellations, nor do they stagnate into calculations about the best possible, but are on watch for exceptional events. The most challenging game situations demand exactly this ability: the ability to respond to what cannot be planned or what comes about for the first time. To understand the central role that such improvisation has in all game playing, one needs to understand its deeply intersubjective character. “Intersubjectivity” is not just another name for sociality but refers to the special kinds of connections that human beings are able to form as free and freely expressive agents. This form of connecting with others is necessary for beginning something new together and thereby bringing something unforeseen into the world, be it a set of fresh ideas, an artwork or a manner of moving and playing the game.

To be sure, the focal object of the game—a ball or a puck—is controlled by one player at any given moment: someone carries, passes or shoots the object into the goal, acting on the basis of immediate perception and intuition. However, the movement of a single player makes sense only as part of the game, which is a dynamic whole enacted by all players at the same time and determined by their mutual actions and expressions. In this respect, playing resembles musical improvisation: although the performances of others cannot determine my own responses, they structure the functional and significative situation in which my own acts take on meaning and find direction. Moreover, the performances of players form mini-histories—measured in minutes and seconds—which allow each singular player to invent novel solutions and find new unforeseen possibilities here and now.

A jazz band that improvises on stage provides a paradigmatic example of [the special bond between individual subjects](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-030-00659-4), who form an intersubjective whole—an ensemble—by their creative and expressive acts. The ensemble’s performance is, of course, based on a composition and exercises, but neither operates as a rulebook or blueprint for the activities of the performing instrumentalists. Rather, the composition works as a framework which each musician, in turn, interprets and develops into new directions. At the same time, it offers a common chordal structure and musical theme to which [each player can return at will](https://www.routledge.com/Philosophy-of-Improvisation-Interdisciplinary-Perspectives-on-Theory-and-Practice/Ravn-Hoffding-McGuirk/p/book/9780367546687), and must return at some point of their common performance.

Similarly, the players on the field are mutually related by their expressive movements and manners of moving. They do not just collide with one another as billiard balls do on the table but [address and challenge one another by their movements](https://benjamins.com/catalog/aicr.14?srsltid=AfmBOopGfqo3iEnjz01R3uBtMnKTpyeN9hgSz0eYRmZDEXb_DSHVnGgs). Their expressive interactions cannot be captured by algorithms or strategic models. Rather than completing commands issued from their brains, or their coaches and managers, players constantly move in rhythmic synchrony and mutual bodily understanding of one another. As an audience, we are not watching analogs of industrial robots or sophisticated automata, but analogs of performing dancers, actors and instrumentalists. What attracts and energizes spectators—audiences and other players alike—across the globe is the combination of combatting and expressive creative movement, not end results calculated by bettors and investors.

A new understanding of the expressive and performative dimensions of play is urgently needed for strengthening and liberating the improvisational capacities of singular players. It is also needed for energizing and intensifying the communication between individual athletes, which on its own can trigger team development.

Even if expressive situations cannot be foreseen or captured by prior plans or numerical calculations, understanding of them can be advanced by drills that help players realize their own capacities and the operative potentials of other players. This allows them to free themselves from habitual patterns of movement and opens for them a field of yet unexplored possibilities. The experts of such cultivation cannot be found in laboratories—they work in [theaters and dance halls](https://tupress.temple.edu/books/the-phenomenology-of-dance).

Each game is a unique event—like a theatre play or a ballet performance. It is a totality of non-repeatable actions and interactions by unique individuals and teams. The systems and strategies devised by the coaches are therefore not the game itself, just as the theatrical script or dramaturgy is not the theatre performance. The game is the constellation of the expressive movements of the players or, better, [the dynamic entity formed by them](https://iai.tv/articles/thinking-is-a-kind-of-dancing-auid-3519).

Players are, of course, similar and comparable in so far as they share skills and aspirations that differ from those of average persons; and analogously, the matches are akin since they share goals, norms and values that differ from those of everyday life. Yet each player is a unique, unparalleled individual, and therefore not repeatable. The same holds for teams and matches: they too are non-repeatable entities and, as such, analogous to works of art.

Performative acts in sports can and should, of course, be trained. However, in this context, training does not mean learning better rules, mastering new theories or controlling personal emotions. It means the development of unique skills of interaction and expression, which allow for harmonious play on the field. This can only be done by imitation, modelling and improvisation: by watching and seeing directly what other skilled players are doing, by reproducing what has been seen in [one’s own body](https://iai.tv/articles/the-body-is-the-zero-point-of-the-world-auid-3537), and by correcting what is done to conform to an even better model. A crucial task is to integrate new movement patterns into one’s own repertoire so that they too can become new possibilities for oneself and for others.
