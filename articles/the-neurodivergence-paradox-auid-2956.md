# The neurodivergence paradox

*How we are all diverse*

**By Lucy Johnstone** | *26th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 7 mins* | *1,436 words*

![The neurodivergence paradox](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/the-neurodivergence-paradox.webp)

---

*The neurodiversity paradigm argues that each of us is different, not disordered. But at the same time, the neurodiversity movement sees many common difficulties as signs of disorders like ADHD and autism. This drive to diagnose normal human behaviour, writes Lucy Johnstone, is contradictory. It assumes a group of perfect neurotypical people, and promotes inclusion while putting people in boxes. We should ditch the “neuro” prefix and find other ways to value our diversity.*

The term ‘neurodiversity’ was coined in 1997 by Australian sociologist Judy Singer. In her words:

‘As a word, “neurodiversity” describes the whole of humanity. But the neurodiversity *movement* is a political movement for people who want their human rights.’

For Singer, neurodiversity’ is not a medical or diagnostic term, but a way of accepting the undoubted fact that we all vary in the ways our brains work. As both she and the wider neurodiversity movement argue, it is very important to frame this in terms of difference not disorder. These equally valid ways of experiencing the world do not require a cure and nor should they be seen as a tragedy; we all have unique skills and struggles, and thus ‘neurodiversity’, like biodiversity in nature, is something to be welcomed.

Few would disagree with Singer’s broad position and intentions. And in the last few years, the term ‘neurodiversity’ has become part of everyday language, along with a rapidly growing number of organisations, training courses, and businesses based on the concept. Clinicians of various backgrounds, journalists, teachers, policy-makers and parents have adopted this language, and social media posts such as ‘[Neurodivergent TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/discover/neurodivergent?lang=en)’ receive millions of views.

But along the way, the concept has evolved into something very different, encompassing a much wider range of experiences than the core ‘neurodivergent’ presentations typically labelled as autism, ASD or ADHD. The consequences, I believe, are not always to be welcomed.

Firstly, there has been anger from parents and carers of children diagnosed as autistic according to the original, much narrower criteria in the diagnostic manuals, and who are by any standards disabled. The merging of this group with ‘Asperger’s or ‘high-functioning’ presentations means that children who never learn to speak or live independently find themselves in the same diagnostic group as articulate adults with friends, partners and successful careers. This includes a growing number of celebrities who now describe themselves as ‘autistic’ or as having ADHD, such as Chris Packham, Anthony Hopkins, Melanie Sykes, Gary Numan, Rory Bremner, Stephen Fry, Ant McPartlin, Sheridan Smith, Sue Perkins, Jamie Oliver, Johnny Vegas, Shaparak Khorsandi and Heston Blumenthal.  Meanwhile, according to some, the more vulnerable core group is being excluded from scarce resources as more articulate and assertive voices take centre stage.

Secondly, inclusivity has evolved into the belief that the population can be divided into two broad camps – neurodivergent and neurotypical – with the latter positioned as intrinsically privileged because the world is designed by and for them. But thissubdivision entirely lacks a neurological basis. Despite a flood of studies, no one has ever substantiated the specific ‘wired differently’ claims in relation to ASD, ADHD, or ‘[neurodivergence](https://www.madinamerica.com/insane-medicine/)’ in general (as opposed to the obvious fact that we are all to some extent ‘wired differently’ from each other.) Thus, there is no scientific basis for the ‘neuro’ prefix, and no medical tests to confirm or disconfirm any of these conditions. We are left with an essentially circular argument: ‘Why is my child so fidgety and restless?’, ‘Because he has ADHD; ‘How do you know he has ADHD?’, ‘Because he is so fidgety and restless.’

Thirdly, the lack of any objective way of drawing the boundaries between autistic or ADHD and ‘normal’, or between neurodivergent and neurotypical, has inevitably led to a vast expansion in the kinds of experiences now qualifying as neurodivergence, to the extent that NHS clinics are reporting assessment waiting lists of up to 11,000 people. Indeed, almost every human behaviour and its opposite can now be seen as indicating ‘neurodivergence’: not concentrating, or being over-focused; talking too much, or too little; difficulty in switching tasks, or inability to stick to one; making eye contact too often or too infrequently; being particularly sensitive or insensitive; and so on.

There is also a whole new terminology to describe what were previously seen as unremarkable behaviours: fiddling with your hair is now ‘[stimming](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/behaviour/stimming)’; disliking loud music is a ‘[sensory sensitivity’](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensory_processing_sensitivity); losing your temper may be a case of ‘[autistic meltdown’](https://www.verywellhealth.com/what-is-an-autistic-meltdown-260154); upset at the end of a relationship is ‘[rejection sensitivity dysphoria’](https://www.webmd.com/add-adhd/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria); and so on. Moreover, neurodivergent people are said to be very skilled at ‘[masking](https://www.healthline.com/health/autism/autism-masking)’ their differences in order to ‘fit in’; this ability itself then becomes a sign of their ‘autism’.

This leads to a strange paradox. The more behaviours that are subsumed under the heading of neurodivergence, the smaller the pool of neurotypicals becomes – until, if the trend continues, everyone will be neurodivergent, and we will be back to square one.

Meanwhile, despite the mantra of ‘difference not disorder’, many who identify as neurodivergent do use and seek psychiatric diagnostic labels. In fact, faced with long waiting lists, a growing number are claiming the right to ‘self-diagnose’. This is despite the fact that [diagnosis](http://iai.tv/video/against-psychiatric-diagnoses) by definition means identifying a deficit of some kind – indeed, this is implied in the terms ASD and ADHD themselves – they are officially ‘disorders.’

Diagnosis opens the door to educational support and workplace adjustments, and less tangibly, to a sense that you are not alone in your struggles. In phrases repeated endlessly on social media, people report their relief at confirmation that ‘*I wasn’t crap because I found VAT returns painful, blurted out stuff and was messy. I have a neuro difference*….’  *‘I thought I was a failed version of normal…but I wasn’t alone… I had an ADHD tribe.*’

These are real and important benefits. But at the same time, some neurodiversity activists firmly reject notions of ‘treatment’, and oppose research that might clarify unanswered questions about neurological factors, on the grounds that this is ‘ableist’. Diagnoses have become ‘identities’, and as such, sought-after, validating, life-affirming and precious. If ‘symptoms’ are at the same time an expression of your ‘authentic self’, then change –through therapy, or simply through maturing and developing – is not only impossible but undesirable, and any suggestion to the contrary may be viewed as offensive and oppressive.

The one point of agreement in this contentious area is that the differences associated with being neurodivergent share an important feature: they contradict dominant social expectations about how we ought to feel, think, behave, and relate. The 6-year-old boy who daydreams in school; the student who prefers reading to parties; the office worker who is often distracted; the parent who is untidy and disorganised – all can now ascribe these traits to their neurodivergence. But attributing people’s very real struggles to unconfirmed brain states locates the problem firmly within the individual. It stops us asking the crucial question: How have we have created a society in which almost no one feels they ‘fit in’?

Perhaps we should be questioning the implicit picture of the neurotypical person, who apparently floats through life effortlessly, competently and serenely, never losing their keys or getting distracted by their phone, and always knowing exactly what to say and do in any given social situation. How have we been persuaded to aspire to these completely unrealistic standards, just as the world gets more demanding and difficult?

In a recent [blog](https://www.madintheuk.com/2024/07/part-1-neurodiversity-what-exactly-does-it-mean/) series, I and a colleague have suggested that it is not surprising that in today’s pressurised, materialistic, fragmented, target-driven, achievement-oriented world, a growing number of children and adults see themselves as failing. And inevitably, under capitalism, a host of career, business and drug company opportunities arises to exploit and perpetuate the demand for services in this new field.

While an attribution of neurodivergence may seem to offer an explanation, perhaps we need a better, less contradictory way of acknowledging and accepting our human struggles and skills, similarities and differences. Meanwhile, it seems that much of this confusion could be avoided if we dropped the ‘neuro’ prefix. We are all diverse. We want to live in a society that accepts all of us. Let us celebrate diversity, while being appropriately cautious about neurodiversity as a way of achieving this.
