# Creating populist martyrs is a dangerous game

*Impeachment merely makes a martyr out of an opponent*

**By Alasia Nuti** | *5th August 2025* | *Read time: approx. 12 mins* | *2,459 words*

![Creating populist martyrs is a dangerous game](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Using-the-courts-against-your-political-opponents-is-doomed-to-fail.webp)

---

*Many people believe that the use of the courts backfired for the Democrats. But does strategy point to a more fundamental flaw? Indeed, does it merely make martyrs out of populists around the world? Alasia Nuti and Gabriele Badano, both lecturers in politics at York University, argue liberals need to avoid using the courts to disallow their opponents and instead look beyond the state to advocate ideas that inspire grassroots politics with a positive vision of the future.*

Liberals rely too much on the state to fight illiberal and anti-democratic forces and this is a strategy that is doomed to fail. In March 2025, Marine Le Pen, the leader of the French far-right party Rassemblement National, was prosecuted for the embezzlement of European parliamentary funds through an unprecedented fake jobs scheme. Found guilty of those charges, Le Pen was not only sentenced to four years in prison (with two years to be served outside jail with an electronic bracelet, and the remaining two suspended), but she was also banned from running for public office for five years, with immediate effect. Crucially, this prevents her from running for President in 2027.

Surely, if Le Pen committed a crime, courts, one would assume, were *legally* bound to prosecute and eventually give her an appropriate sentence. As noted by opponents like Marine Tondelier, the leader of France's Green Party, and Olivier Faure, a prominent voice in the French Socialist Party, [nobody should be above the law](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/31/g-s1-57262/france-marine-le-pen-embezzlement-verdict), not even Marine Le Pen. However, *politically,* this means halting the advance of a political leader who has vast popular support and was [predicted to win the next presidential elections](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/aug/25/marine-le-pen-could-become-next-french-president-minister-warns). Unsurprisingly, Le Pen immediately labelled the verdict against her a “[denial of democracy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F17gLTFl0LE)”, while her right-hand man Jordan Bardella denounced it as an “execution” of democracy. Many far-right leaders across the world rallied behind Le Pen, depicting the prosecution and sentence as politically motivated. This pattern suggests a strategic error. Instead of relying on the machinery of the state, such as courts, to defeat illiberal forces, liberals should look towards the grassroots and the local, working at the same time to reinvent how their political parties operate, to erode the far-right from the ground up.

\_\_\_

While Democrats generally defend the rule of law, Trump has relentlessly attacked the courts prosecuting him.

\_\_\_

This is, of course, not the first time in which courts have prosecuted far-right (or populist) leaders for their alleged crimes, thereby potentially contributing to neutralizing a political figure with widespread popular support. Donald Trump is the first president in US history to be criminally convicted and has [several other criminal and civil cases](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/donald-trumps-criminal-civil-cases-2025-01-10/) against him. While Democrats generally defend the rule of law, Trump has [relentlessly attacked the courts](https://www.reuters.com/investigates/special-report/usa-election-threats-courts/) prosecuting him. He portrayed them as complicit with the “radical left” and the Biden administration, going so far as to categorize their strategy as “[lawfare](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/av/world-us-canada-67338163)” and to threaten violence and harassment for the judges and their families. Before Le Pen and Trump, there was the Italian media tycoon and politician Silvio Berlusconi, who dominated the Italian political scene for at least 20 years, starting in the early 1990s. Over the years, he constantly depicted himself as being [persecuted by politicized judges and courts](https://www.nytimes.com/2008/06/25/world/europe/25iht-italy.4.13989109.html) (infamously labelling them *toghe rosse*, that is, “communist gowns”) who were only motivated by the desire to remove him from office.

\_\_\_

When it comes to defending liberal-democratic institutions, courts are likely to do more harm than good.

\_\_\_

Should liberals aiming to defend [liberal democracy](http://iai.tv/articles/if-we-insist-on-growth-we-will-need-autocracy-auid-3240) cheer when illiberal and anti-democratic political leaders are prosecuted and punished by the courts? Relying on judicial power to defeat the enemies of liberal democracy is extremely risky, at the very least because it gives those dangerous actors a further rhetorical advantage: [the opportunity to once again picture themselves as victims of the establishment](https://www.lse.ac.uk/research/research-for-the-world/society/weaponisation-victimhood), at the same time strengthening their support and creating distrust in the role of courts as part of a liberal system of checks and balances. So, when it comes to defending liberal-democratic institutions, courts are likely to do more harm than good.

Of course, we are not suggesting that far-right or populist heavyweights should be granted effective immunity from prosecution – at the end of the day, the rule of law is a pillar of liberalism. However, courts and other relevant actors often have ample room for maneuver above and beyond the decision to prosecute a crime. For instance, in Le Pen’s case, the court could have postponed the bar on standing for public office until the appeal process was exhausted. On their part, Joe Biden and the various political leaders of the opposition to Berlusconi could have done more to redirect the attention of the public away from Trump’s and Berlusconi’s court cases, instead of [too often focusing it on them even further](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/06/04/politics/biden-slams-trump-convicted-felon-analysis).

In general, the problems with using courts as a tool to resist illiberal and antidemocratic forces reveal how complicated and potentially counterproductive it is to rely on the state as the main agent tasked with defending liberal democracy. Traditionally, theories of so-called “militant democracy” argue that, at least when there is a clear threat to liberal democracy, legal repression of illiberal and antidemocratic actors is in order. Originally associated with the work of [Karl Loewenstein](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/militant-democracy-and-fundamental-rights-i/7C5EF30572005FB60B70F4A7D6FC0654)—who soberly reflected on the Nazis’ democratic rise to power in Germany—militant democracy has recently gained a new interest. For instance, in Germany, there is [an ongoing heated debate](https://www.euractiv.com/section/politics/news/germany-labels-far-right-afd-party-as-extremist-group/) as to whether the far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD), which is the country's second-largest political party, should be banned. Moreover, militant democracy relies on the state in several other ways above and beyond the infringement of individual rights of free speech and political association. [As observed by Benjamin Schupmann](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/democracy-despite-itself-9780192873026?cc=gb&lang=en&), militant democracy also deploys constitutional entrenchment (embedded, for instance, in Germany’s [Basic Law](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/EN/BasicLawLegalBasis/TheBasicLaw/thebasiclaw_node.html)), which makes certain constitutional principles impossible to amend, and entrusts the judiciary with the task of deciding which laws breach constitutional rights.

\_\_\_

Relying on the judiciary ends up politicizing a state institution that should be widely perceived as neutral

\_\_\_

Even more nuanced approaches, which are skeptical of the use of coercive strategies to defend liberal democracy, are often unable to look beyond the state. For instance, [Corey Brettschneider](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691171296/when-the-state-speaks-what-should-it-say) argues that the expressive power of the state, exemplified by its ability to erect monuments and establish national holidays that celebrate liberal ideas of freedom and equality, can stem the growth of illiberal and anti-democratic ideas. [Other liberals](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674011236) place their faith in state education and curricula designed to instill liberal-democratic values in young generations and create citizens committed to them.

In contrast, [our recently published book](https://academic.oup.com/book/57563) argues that liberals place too much trust in state institutions. This amount of trust is misplaced as it overlooks important agents who might be better placed to erode the support enjoyed by far-right forces. As seen, when other efforts to diminish such support are unsuccessful, relying on the judiciary ends up politicizing a state institution that should be widely perceived as neutral. Moreover, as shown vividly by Trump’s second presidential term and the situation of a few other well-established liberal democracies, important state institutions can well be captured by far-right actors. We surely cannot expect them to carry out the expressive and educational policies liberals call for. In the face of that sort of capture, the question naturally becomes, which agents other than the state can counter the far right?

One such agent, which liberals neglect, is given by municipalities, which are any subnational administrative units with some powers of self-government or jurisdiction (ranging from provinces and regions to cities and towns). Municipalities are sites of political power that have the capacity and resources to reduce the appeal of the message conveyed by far-right forces. At the most confrontational end of the spectrum, municipalities can refuse to cooperate with central governments captured by far-right political leaders when they enact legislation that violates basic rights and opportunities. There are several examples of municipalities that appear to have gone down this route. For instance, in 2018 and 2019, the then Italian Deputy Prime Minister and Interior Minister Matteo Salvini—leader of La Lega—enacted two “security decrees” that gravely curtailed the [basic rights of asylum seekers](http://iai.tv/articles/immigration-is-not-a-zero-sum-game-auid-3062). When that happened, several mayors of Italian cities—spearheaded by the then mayor of Palermo, Leoluca Orlando—refused to implement that legislation and directly [challenged the central government](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/02/01/world/europe/italy-mayors-migrants-salvini-security-decree.html). Sanctuary cities in the US have fought against Trump’s immigration policies since his first term and [are once again ready to protects the rights of vulnerable migrants](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2024/nov/29/trump-mass-deportation-plan-cities).

Less confrontationally, municipalities can and should promote initiatives that attempt to establish and nourish relations of freedom, equality, and fairness (characteristic of any society that was truly organized around liberal-democratic values) within their jurisdiction. Municipalities—especially cities and towns—are often proactive in bringing together different social, religious, and ethnic groups, encouraging them to embark on collaborative activities (from commensality to local sport ventures) and fostering relations of mutual understanding and respect. For example, the small municipality of Crevalcore in the Metropolitan City of Bologna organizes [a five-a-side football tournament](https://www.cittametropolitana.bo.it/sanitasociale/Primavera_contro_le_discriminazioni/Settimana_per_il_dialogo_interculturale_contro_il_razzismo_e_tutte_le_discriminazioni_2012/Torneo_di_calcetto_a_5) with local cultural associations where teams made up of young players of both migrant and non-migrant origins compete against each other.

Political parties are another example of non-state actors that must step up in the fight against the far-right. Although written off by many as outdated, political parties committed to liberal-democratic values compete for popular support and, in turn, power against far-right forces. So far, many such parties have fallen prey to the temptation of mimicking far-right rhetoric and policies. In May 2025, the UK Prime Minister and Labour leader, Sir Keir Starmer, was called out for echoing the far-right with [his speech on migration](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2025/may/12/keir-starmer-defends-plans-to-curb-net-migration),  lamenting that the UK is on the verge of becoming an “island of strangers.” [As stressed by Cas Mudde](https://www.theguardian.com/news/2019/may/14/why-copying-the-populist-right-isnt-going-to-save-the-left) and many other political scientists, this strategy—known as “copy-catting”—is ineffective as it just makes far-right messages more salient. Moreover, liberals should regard it as morally unacceptable because it normalizes exclusionary narratives and fuels the climate of hate and suspicion of diversity created by the far-right.

In contrast, political parties loyal to liberal-democratic values should be more creative, thinking harder about how to radically transform the platform they campaign on without buying into the whitewashed version of morally repugnant far-right staples. This means sticking to liberal-democratic ideals while offering a [vision for society](http://iai.tv/articles/to-defeat-populism-the-left-must-focus-on-work-auid-3158) that can nonetheless be compelling to *some* voters who are currently attracted to the far-right. Political parties need to be strategic and intercept some of the demands currently catered for by far-right competitors, as exemplified by the need to address the socio-economic harms of globalization, but also the demand for a better representation of certain religiously motivated Christian constituencies. Importantly, our dark times require a joint effort from all parties committed to liberal democracy, from the left to the conservative right. One of the strengths of liberalism is that it is a broad church, encompassing progressive political forces that champion egalitarian [redistributive policies](http://iai.tv/video/the-wealth-paradox) as well as more traditionalist actors. When facing an enemy like the far-right, whose success has complex and heterogeneous causes, multiple parties (and perhaps multiple currents within the same party) occupying different positions on the left–right spectrum must be mobilized in the hope of eroding as much support as possible.

This discussion of the present failings of political parties and the need for parties to do better brings us back to the courts. The Democratic Party in the US and many other major North American and European parties that are now facing threatening populist far-right competitors are characterized by [a centralized and hierarchical structure that is largely disconnected from grassroots members](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/40407077.pdf). This structure hinders those parties’ ability to re-energize civil society in the way we have just outlined, leaving the courts as the only available (but, as seen, ultimately flawed) system to try and defeat the populist far-right. Therefore, both as a matter of principle related to the stability of liberal democratic institutions and a matter of self-interest in the face of the ever-growing support for their populist far-right opponents, political parties loyal to the liberal democratic order should urgently restructure themselves [in the direction of greater participation by grassroots members and more intra-party democracy](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jopp.12064).

\_\_\_

Liberalism contains the necessary theoretical resources to put forward an attractive vision for society that is antithetical to that promised by the far-right.

\_\_\_

Common citizens have an important role to play, too.  Most individuals loyal to liberal democracy know supporters of the far right. Some of their friends, relatives, or colleagues might vote for far-right political leaders and parties, or might simply have started feeling the pull of their ideas. Although liberals are generally reluctant to ask much of common citizens, these are times that require [active citizenship](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jopp.12134). Therefore, citizens must be ready to engage with the supporters of far-right forces they know, taking advantage of the individualized knowledge of their interlocutors to persuade them to change their minds. Importantly, if citizens do their part, the job of political parties fighting the populist radical right and other kinds of political campaigners become considerably easier. Anat Shenker-Osorio is a progressive campaign strategist with a long experience, ranging from racial equality initiatives in the US to the campaign to legalize abortion in Ireland and campaigns to improve the treatment of asylum seekers in Australia. In her view, successful campaigns never push their messages on their own. They always give their broader base of followers something to repeat to family, friends, and colleagues. This is because the way to persuade others of your vision for society is [by making sure that they come across it all the time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P7_OB6LYC_E), allaying any initial suspicion and making the message progressively more and more convincing.

Liberalism contains the necessary theoretical resources to put forward an attractive vision for society that is antithetical to that promised by the far-right. For instance, the influential liberal political philosopher [John Rawls](http://iai.tv/articles/a-new-vision-for-the-centre-left-auid-2887) advances a conception of society as a fair cooperative scheme between free and equal citizens that can appeal to people with different political, moral, and religious beliefs. Rawls’s liberalism suggests that pluralism is unavoidable. In itself, pluralism should not be worrying precisely because a powerful justification for the most basic liberal-democratic institutions can be offered to social groups buying into very different beliefs about religion, excellence in life, and other “comprehensive” matters. However, Rawlsian liberalism also tells us [when pluralism becomes problematic](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9760.2004.00202.x). For instance, beliefs that do not recognize the freedom and equality of all citizens are threatening and must be contained when they gain traction in society. However, if liberals want to make some normative recommendations in this area, either in general or with respect to the far right, they must look beyond the state, focusing instead on municipal engagement, active citizenship, and renewed political parties.
