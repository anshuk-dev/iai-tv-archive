# Science gets sexual experience dangerously wrong

*Sex needs rescuing from scientific imperialism*

**By Paul Giladi** | *2nd April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,507 words*

![Science gets sexual experience dangerously wrong](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Science-gets-sexual-experience-dangerously-wrong.webp)

---

*Today’s science of sex often reduces arousal and pleasure to pulse rates, brain waves, vaginal lubrication and other bodily metrics, prioritizing quantifiable data over subjective experience. This framework originated in the progressive sexual revolution of the 1960s – yet, as philosopher **Paul Giladi** argues, far from contributing to sexual emancipation, it risks leading us to disbelieve what people, and especially women, say about their own states of arousal. For true sexual liberation, we need to reclaim the erotic from scientific imperialism.*

Scientific naturalism is the idea that reality is just what the natural sciences deem it to be, and the ways in which we make sense of things are justifiable only by the methods of the natural sciences. Part of what makes scientific naturalism so interesting is that some philosophers seem to regard developments in physics, chemistry, and biology with envy. In the Western European context, the spectacular and rapid expansions in scientific knowledge – especially in the 19th century – grossly outpaced the progress made in philosophy. Revolutions were taking place in the discovery of atoms, evolutionary adaptations, and infectious diseases. By contrast, philosophers were (and still are) grappling with how to solve the "classic" problems of free will, universals, induction, etc. A thought then occurred to some of them: what if advances in scientific inquiry could be used to settle disputes in metaphysics, epistemology, philosophy of mind, and moral philosophy?

This insight led to the development of logical empiricism in the 1920s – which famously proclaimed metaphysical statements and theological statements to be meaningless – and [Willard Quine’s project of "naturalistic epistemology,"](https://philarchive.org/rec/QUIEN) which aimed to recast discourse about knowledge and justification as a branch of empirical psychology. While logical empiricism and Quine’s "Epistemology Naturalised" no longer dominate the Anglo-American philosophical landscape *per se*, their imprint cannot be overstated: much Anglo-American philosophy has yet to emerge from their shadows, and remains quite charmed by scientific naturalism.

**"Can scientific research make sex good again?"**

In her [most recent book](https://www.versobooks.com/en-gb/products/2564-tomorrow-sex-will-be-good-again?srsltid=AfmBOoqxTEhCRierZl59N29i0B9Af-mcZ81T3cZBmN_Fls-NUxjewVQ4), Katherine Angel asked "Can scientific research make sex good again?", a question that riffs on Michel Foucault’s provocative line in his [*The Will to Knowledge* (1978)](https://www.amazon.co.uk/History-Sexuality-Will-Knowledge-v/dp/0140268685). The matter is not just whether a scientific naturalist account of sexual arousal enables more pleasurable sexual activities. It’s also whether scientific naturalist conceptual frameworks might be employed to produce uplifting sexual worlds. With this in mind, consider the following lines from Season 1 of Showtime’s *Masters of Sex*, a drama series focusing on the academic and personal lives of William H. Masters and Virginia E. Johnson, whose co-authored work, [*Human Sexual Response* (1966)](https://www.abebooks.co.uk/book-search/title/human-sexual-response/author/virginia-johnson-william-masters/first-edition/), transformed sexology:

*What if we could understand the basic physiology underneath all that nonsense?*

*Physiology [serves to] shine a light into the darkness.*

*Science holds the key!*

*I’m wiring Miss DiMello to monitor her pulse, heart rate, and brain waves, to illuminate to my patients and to the general community what happens to the body during sexual stimulation and orgasm.*

*… the provost of the entire university will be watching me [mount and use the Ulysses, a motor-powered plastic dildo with clinical accoutrements].*

*No. He’s not watching you. He’s watching science.*

*All of our subjects know they’re advancing the course of science.*

*Anything for science!*

Across sexology, Masters & Johnson are credited with inaugurating a physiology-centred scientific naturalism. They conceived their research investigations as more rigorous and cognitively penetrative than the 1948 and 1953 [Kinsey Reports](https://kinseyinstitute.org/about/history/index.php). Indeed, a psychophysiological approach to making sense of sexual activity may be deemed appealing, because hard physiological data appears more epistemically attractive than interviews and questionnaires, given that "[o]ur disinclination to be perfectly honest when discussing sex is high" ([Magnanti 2012: 19](https://www.weidenfeldandnicolson.co.uk/titles/brooke-magnanti/the-sex-myth/9780297866404/)). Quantifiable content and datasets cannot lie and misdirect.

Masters & Johnson’s interest in scientific naturalist analyses of arousal and pleasure went beyond academic debates about the alleged theoretical virtues of quantitative research methods over qualitative research methods. More broadly, the paradigm shift they heralded seemed to form a looping feedback relation between progress in science and the progressive sexual revolution sweeping the US in the 1960s. For Masters & Johnson, to help bring about sexual emancipation – as a necessary condition for women’s liberation – one must not block scientific inquiry: the more naturalistic the approach to making sense of women’s sexual arousal, sexual desire, and sexual habituations, the more likely it was that any insights gleaned would be accurate and grounded. Such insights are then supposed to trickle down into improving sexual activity itself, because of the power/knowledge relationship in which the scientific frame directly feeds into the interpersonal frame. In other words, the more psychophysiological the approach to making sense of women’s sexual states, the more educated society becomes about women’s sexual states; the more educated we become about women’s sexual states, the better sex will be.

We are worryingly encouraged to think that genital response is exhaustive of women’s sexual arousal.

\_\_\_

In this way, then, Foucault’s ameliorative vision that tomorrow sex will be good again requires thoroughgoing naturalisation of arousal and pleasure. This does not merely involve studying arousal scientifically. It principally involves transforming the vocabulary human beings use to make sense of arousal. So, as the quoted lines from *Masters of Sex* indicate, Masters considered thoroughgoing naturalisation as a modernising force for intellectual and social progress, one that will finally bring an end to bumbling intellectual as well as sexual *praxis*.The problem is when this approach is adopted exclusively.

**Sexual arousal ≠ genital response**

Consider the American Psychology Association’s *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders* (DSM). Unlike the [DSM-III (1980)](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm/about-dsm/history-of-the-dsm), the [DSM-IV (1994)](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm/about-dsm/history-of-the-dsm) does not mention

lack of subjective arousal or pleasure, or indeed any non-genital changes associated with sexual arousal, in its description of Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD) and Female Sexual Arousal Disorder (FSAD). In the case of women, in particular, DSM-IV has an exclusively scientific discourse, that only treats vaginal and clitoral lubrication – wetness – as constitutive and descriptively exhaustive of women’s sexual arousal. So, we are worryingly encouraged to think that genital response is exhaustive of women’s sexual arousal. In other words, scientific naturalist approaches to women’s sexual arousal reduce it to physiological markers: *genital response = sexual arousal*.[1]

Not only that, the naturalism of the DSM-IV’s vocabulary is bound up with longstanding oppressive social norms and cultures of erotic expectation in western heteropatriarchal contexts. A mutually sustaining relationship seems to exist between mainstream media, pornography, and the DSM-IV vocabulary, insofar as each of these feeds myths about women’s arousal to each other.

\_\_\_

Sexual arousal, especially women’s sexual arousal, is saturated by contexts, vocabularies, and interests that run counter to "zealous physiology"

\_\_\_

I have two objections to "*genital response = sexual arousal*."

First, this equation "tames" the complex phenomenological dynamics of the erotic. Identifying sexual arousal with physiological markers renders the erotic "locatable" and, consequently, easier to epistemically manage by construing it solely in naturalistic terms. This taming of the erotic constitutes an injurious *de-eroticisation*, to the extent that what started out (and is) deeply enjoyable and resistant to being captured in the "nomological net" of physiology forcibly mutates into the deadened affectivity of mechanistic causal relations. As Audré Lorde writes in her essay, "Uses of the Erotic: The Erotic as Power,"

the principal horror of such a system is that it robs our work of its erotic value, its erotic power and life appeal and fulfilment. Such a system reduces work to a travesty of necessities … to a world of flattened affect, a world of the ascetic who aspires to feel nothing. But nothing is farther from the truth. For the ascetic position is one of the highest fear, the gravest immobility. The severe abstinence of the ascetic becomes the ruling obsession. And it is one not of self-discipline but of self-abnegation. ([Lorde 1993: 55-6](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/library-research-services/collections/diversity-inclusion-belonging/zami-sister))

Sexual arousal, especially women’s sexual arousal, is saturated by contexts, vocabularies, and interests that run counter to "zealous physiology" ([Lacan 1982: 89](https://www.abebooks.co.uk/9780333220351/Feminine-Sexuality-Jacques-Lacan-Ecole-0333220358/plp)). A framework that primarily treats embodied agents as objects of physiological study – as aggregative bundles of neural, endocrinological, and biophysical events and processes that swell, stiffen, and drip – seems untouched by the vast array of intimate and cultural contexts that form sites of erotic encounters and pleasure. To play on an expression by Maurice Merleau-Ponty, the agent "in its sexual being," contrary to the phenomenologically detached and disinterested scientific naturalist perspective, occupies a holistically structured sexualised space, one which discloses that agent’s erotic subjectivity and intentionality.

\_\_\_

The scientific naturalist approach to women’s sexual arousal dangerously flirts with viewing women’s own subjective reports of their sexual arousal as dishonest indicators.

\_\_\_

In this way, the dynamics of being aroused involve the erotic subject opening themselves up to the diverse range of unexpected and exciting encounters with the erotic other. For, the affective architecture of arousal is structured by a complex range of "meaning-making" schemas that are primarily rooted in recognitive attitudes, as opposed to dispassionate cognitive dispositions. Emily Nagoski makes a similar point:

It’s not about attending to any specific physiological response, behaviour, or other clue. It’s about attending with a kind of broad, receptive vigilance. ([Nagoski 2015: 218](https://scribepublications.co.uk/books-authors/books/come-as-you-are-9781925228014/extract))

Second, there is a well-established inconsistency between testimonial reports of arousal and genital response in women.[2] Therefore, to insist that "genital response = sexual arousal" also comes across as either *hostile to* or not especially *invested in* the free and reliably authoritative subjective reports of women:

Okay, so Henry and I were messing around and I said, ‘I’m ready, I want you,’ and he said, ‘No, you’re not wet, you’re just humouring me.’ And I said, ‘No, I’m totally ready!’ And he didn’tbelieve me because I wasn’t wet. So … should I see a doctor? Is it hormonal? What’s wrong? (Nagoski 2015: 191)

The scientific naturalist approach to women’s sexual arousal dangerously flirts with viewing women’s own subjective reports of their sexual arousal as dishonest indicators. Henry evaluatively perceives his partner (the interviewee) as devoid of good faith and legitimate credibility as an authority of their own embodied state and disposition. It gets worse. By insisting that his partner cannot justifiably claim to be sexually aroused (because they are not wet), Henry strips them of their long-cultivated sense of having legitimate credibility as an authority on their own embodied state and disposition.

Importantly, my critique is not committed to the idea that it’s impossible to be mistaken about one’s own mental states – e.g. believing you’re happy when you’re not happy. My critique is also not committed to the idea that the default stance should be to believe what people say about their own mental states at least until we get some evidence that they might be mistaken about them. Instead, my critique is committed to the idea that one’s testimony, *unless there are clearly compelling reasons for judging the testimony to be unworthy of credibility*, should be viewed as the principal source of knowledge about one’s mental and somatic state. Henry’s illegitimate insistence that his partner cannot justifiably claim to be sexually aroused silences their testimony in ways that are eerily similar to the following endorsements of scientific naturalist approaches to sex:

Now the focus on what is testable and verifiable has taken root in the study of sex and gender, there is less acceptance of personal opinion as the only source of evidence. This, by the way, is a good thing … (Magnanti 2012: 12). The difference between what women report is turning them on and what is actually getting their bodies to respond, is significant. We may think we know what turns people on, but the data are giving researchers a very different picture (Magnanti 2012: 19).

[Lubricating vulvas and tumescent penises are] unambiguous agents of sincerity ([de Botton 2003: 17](https://www.alaindebotton.com/how-to-think-more-about-sex/)).

For Magnanti and Alain De Botton, the sole honest indicator of sexual arousal is genital response. So, this means that when women *say* that they find trust to be erotically affecting, for example, they are either reporting an illusion or lying about their sexual arousal. The epistemic power relation here is one which sees a woman’s authority of communicating and making sense of her own embodied state and disposition eroded by others. When Nagoski’s other interviewee asks, "what’s wrong (with me)?", the answer is in fact *nothing* to do with any putative psychophysiological "failings" on her part. What’s wrong is the cruelty of "genital response = sexual arousal."

As such, rather than contribute to women’s liberation *via* sexual liberation, the scientific naturalist approach obstructs such emancipation. Instead of ensuring sex will be good again tomorrow, by regularly using "descriptions of our physiology to stand in for descriptions of our states of mind" (Nagoski 2015: 210), the total naturalisation of the erotic fails to produce uplifting sexual worlds.

For sex to be good again tomorrow, Nagoski implores us to "pay attention to your partner’s *words*, rather than their genitals" (Nagoski 2015: 193). However, this is where Nagoski and I differ with respect to our shared anti-scientism about the erotic: Nagoski’s position risks *under*-appreciating ways in which the physiology of arousal helps women not have painful, bad, dangerous sex. Instead of resisting a totalising scientific naturalism by under-appreciating physiology, I think a more effective strategy is to adopt a pluralism of vocabularies. This is because pluralism does not buy into oscillating between over-appreciating physiology and under-appreciating physiology. Instead, pluralism is focused on non-oppressive circulations of epistemic power that run counter to scientific naturalism’s imperialist ambitions. This progressive intellectual orientation, to quote Foucault,

should be seen as a kind of attempt to emancipate historical knowledges from that subjection, to render them, that is, capable of opposition and of struggle against the coercion of a theoretical, unitary, formal and scientific discourse. It is based on a reactivation of local knowledges – of minor knowledges, as [Gilles] Deleuze might call them – in opposition to the scientific hierarchisation of knowledges and the effects intrinsic to their power ([Foucault 1980: 85](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Power_Knowledge.html?id=Aqf309sk_EsC)).

So, to return to the first question "Can scientific research make sex good again?" I would say "Yes, but only when the kind of discourse we adopt is neither scientifically imperialist, nor one that rests content with placing physiological descriptors at the centre and then bolting on complex vocabularies as evaluative extras."
