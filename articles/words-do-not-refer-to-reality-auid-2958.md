# Words do not refer to reality

*How words get their meanings*

**By Paul Pietroski** | *30th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,687 words*

![Words do not refer to reality](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/words-don-t-describe-the-world-720.png)

---

*We tend to think that words refer to things in the world. We believe words latch on to parts of reality, and that these connections are the fundamental source of linguistic meaning. But linguist and philosopher Paul Pietroski argues this is a mistake and offers an alternative approach to meaning. Words do not refer to things, Pietroski argues, and meaning is instead determined by the construction of complex concepts from simpler ones.*

Many animals can think about things. A horse can think intently about an apple that it sees in your hand. Humans can think and talk about lots of things that we can’t perceive. Consider dinosaurs, Shakespeare, the Earth’s metallic inner core, fairness, nuclear fusion, and next week. How can our minds reach so far, with such ease? Words surely help. But how?

A typical child acquires thousands of words. Acquiring ‘apple’ usually involves thinking about an apple that someone is talking about; but it’s hard to see how we could think about dinosaurs or weeks without having any words. We can think by “talking to ourselves;” but it’s hard to see how using *words* could let us *think* about things. To help organize the questions in this vicinity, I’ll sketch two pictures of how speech is related to thought, and then modify the first by stealing from the second.

**1. Words as Vehicles for Personal Concepts**

Often, things grab our attention. Thinking about something is a more creative mental action of using cognitive resources to represent or depict something. Such actions can be prompted by perceptions. Seeing something fall from a tree might lead to depicting it as an apple. But depictions need not be veridical. Perhaps the falling object was a red ball. Alone in the dark, one can generate lots of mental representations and think about things that aren’t there.

Someone might focus on the die that came up two and think about it in that way. Someone else might think about the same die as the one that landed on the right. You might think about something *as* an apple, while a horse thinks about it *as* another one of those delicious treats. Following a long tradition, let’s say that thinking about things involves the use of *concepts*—e.g., concepts of being an apple, or concepts of landing on the right—that can be used to represent things in various ways. For each concept, there is a corresponding “application condition”—e.g., being an apple, or landing on the right. A concept applies to each thing that meets this condition, but to nothing else. Using small capital letters to indicate concepts, let’s say that a child can have a concept apple that can be connected with ‘apple’ or a similar word like ‘pomme’ in French.

There may be multiple ways of thinking about something as an apple. So even if each of us has only one concept of being an apple, your concept apple may be a little different than mine but close enough for most conversational purposes. Compare minor variations in pronunciation. (Relatedly, we don’t need to say that everyone who speaks English speaks the *same* language. We can allow for a family of English languages that are, like the Romance languages and their human speakers, similar for historical reasons.) This invites a simple idea about how words are related to thinking: in acquiring a word, a speaker links a pronunciation to a concept they already had (e.g., apple), and henceforth uses the word to express that concept.

Words can be combined to form phrases like ‘red apple’. And many words are complex. (Consider ‘antidiscrimination’ or ‘pushed’, which combines ‘push’ with past tense.) But for each speaker, the complexity bottoms out in a finite number of memorized “lexical items” that can be combined to form complex expressions. Presumably, concepts can be combined in similar ways, with concepts like red-apple being built up from atomic concepts. But one wants to know what the atomic concepts are and how they are acquired. Do we have thousands of them, including giraffe, squander, and religion?

Lexical items need not express atomic concepts. For example, ‘mare’ seems to indicate a complex concept, perhaps horse-and-mature-and-female. John Locke, followed by many others, hoped for reduction of the concepts expressed with lexical items to a much smaller stock of concepts that are rooted in perception or logical capacities. (Think about red, moving, and red-and-moving.) Alas, the hope is forlorn. Adapting an example from Hilary Putnam, horse isn’t a concept of the form animal-and-x: we can coherently imagine that horses *are not* animals, but rather cleverly disguised machines left behind by alien explorers. More generally, as Jerry Fodor often argued, it seems that most lexical items are used to express atomic concepts.

But if speakers have these concepts *prior* to acquiring the lexical items, we face hard questions about how the concepts are acquired. So one might hope to replace our first simple idea, about how talking is related to thinking, with the idea that acquiring lexical items is itself a way of acquiring concepts that are shared by members of a linguistic community.

**2. Words as Public Concepts**

Smart people once thought that stars are holes in a celestial canopy, and that water is a basic substance not composed of anything else. Until the 1920s, the Great Nebula in Andromeda seemed to be just that—a big nebula in our own Milky Way system, not another galaxy. With such examples in mind, Putnam and others wondered what words like ‘star’ and ‘water’ mean. A concept of the form hole-and-x wouldn’t apply to stars. But it’s easy to acquire a concept star that does apply to stars; and scientists start with ordinary concepts before they develop theories that might be false. This suggested that concepts like star and water are theoretically neutral and probably atomic.

Water is made from hydrogen and oxygen. It doesn’t follow that water is complex, much less that it is built up from hydrogen and oxygen. But water applies to samples of H2O, regardless of who does or doesn’t know this. Likewise, star applies to things that turn out to be giant nuclear fusion reactors. Ordinary ways of thinking about things tend to be neutral about the *natures* of things. Discovering what stars *are* requires studies of stars, not analysis of star. Putnam combined this important point with an influential thought experiment. Imagine being transported to a place that is superficially just like Earth but chemically different: in place of H2O, there is some other stuff—call it XYZ—that only experts could distinguish from H2O; on “Twin Earth,” XYZ is used (e.g., for drinking) in the ways that water is used here.

According to Putnam and many others, XYZ isn’t water, and water wouldn’t apply to samples of XYZ. The idea is that our word ‘water’ has been used in ways that established a connection to H2O, even if nobody could describe water in that chemical way until the 18th century, with the result that ‘water’ applies to all *and only* samples of H2O. In which case, acquiring ‘water’ is not just a matter of linking a pronunciation to a concept the acquirer already had: the word lets a speaker tap into a communal way of representing some stuff of a certain kind. So maybe children can acquire ‘dinosaur’ and ‘week’ without having their ownways of thinking about dinosaurs and weeks. Maybe. But it seems just wrong to say that ‘water’ applies to all and only samples of H2O, even if we allow for minor impurities.

Diet Coke is about 99.5 percent H2O. My well in New Mexico delivers water that is about 99.4 percent H2O, similar in granularity to decent coffee. City water varies. Ocean water, at around 96.5%, is closer to Bud Light and distilled vinegar (about 95%) than pure H2O. Noam Chomsky noted that a cup of liquid from Boston Harbor will have less H2O than a cup of tea. In short, a lot of stuff that counts as water has a *lower* percentage of H2O than a lot of stuff that *isn’t* water. One can say that the extra ingredients in Diet Coke matter more, for these purposes, than the iron and sodium and sulphur in my well water. But this is to admit that ‘water’ does not have a Putnamian application condition. Instead, it seems that ‘water’ can be used to express a theoretically neutral “kind-concept” (call it water-k) that lets us think about water in terms of its underlying nature—whatever that turns out to be—*or* to express a “functional-concept” (call it water-f) that lets us think about water in terms of its roles in our lives. We can imagine these roles being played by stuff other than H2O, much as the roles played by spoons can be played by things made of metal, wood, or plastic. So we can say that water-f would apply to samples of XYZ but water-k wouldn’t. Application conditions are for concepts, not the words we use to express concepts

Even with regard to words like ‘rabbit’, usage is complicated. A child might acquire ‘rabbit’ during encounters with members of a single species, say the prolific European rabbits, or a few random members of the seventeen species of Cottontails. So even if taxonomists have a concept that covers all the rabbits, but no hares or jackrabbits, this isn’t the *only* concept that can be linked to ‘rabbit’. And even if many thinkers share a kind-concept of water, this isn’t the only concept one can express with ‘water’. If a child has concepts of water and rabbits before they acquire ‘water’ and ‘rabbit’, how could you stop them from using the words to express their own concepts? So it seems unlikely that *word* usage is stable enough to establish application conditions for ‘rabbit’ and ‘water’—much less ‘sky’, ‘time’, ‘wit’, ‘democracy’, etc.

Questions about concept acquisition remain. But instead of effectively treating lexical items as pronounceable concepts that speakers share, maybe we should abandon the idea that each lexical item corresponds to *one* concept, and say that a lexical item can be used to express related concepts that have different application conditions. Allowing for lots of atomic concepts, including pairs like water-k and water-f, may make acquisition less mysterious.

**3.  Words as Headings for Families of Concepts**

Consider sentences (1-3).

(1)   The book weighed two pounds.

(2)   The book had been favorably reviewed.

(3)   The book weighed two pounds, but it had been favorably reviewed.

In (1), ‘book’ is used to talk about a thing that can be carried, dropped, or defaced. This thing, call it a book-vehicle, differs from the content conveyed. In (2), ‘book’ is used to talk about the downloadable content that can be criticized or plagiarized. In (3), ‘book’ is used to talk about the book-vehicle; but the pronoun ‘it’ is used to talk about the relevant book-content, which was created before the book-vehicle came off the press. A speaker who understands (1-3) seems to have linked a word to distinct but related concepts like book-vehicle and book-content.

This kind of equivocality is called *polysemy*: a single word, corresponding to one dictionary entry, can have a core meaning that allows for multiple senses. By contrast, in cases of *homophony*, two lexical items (each with its own dictionary entry) share a pronunciation. For example, the OED distinguishes the noun ‘bank’ that is used to talk about river edges from the homophonous noun that is used to talk about financial establishments. But the polysemy of the second noun is also noted: distinct senses are specified, corresponding to *places* where transactions can be conducted and *institutions* that can have branches; and the places can be buildings or interior spaces, as illustrated with (4).

(4)  He broke his glasses walking into the bank that he had to get into before it closed.

The noun ‘bank’ in (4) is a header for a family of concepts. And families can grow.

We can use ‘window’ to talk about certain holes in walls, framed panes of glass, places where bank tellers meet customers, or storefront display spaces. The word ‘line’ can be used to talk about certain perceptible marks on a blackboard—or lines in faces, or fishing line, or waiting lines—or certain abstract things that have no width and permit “construction” of triangles and squares that we can’t perceive but can prove theorems about. This suggests that ‘line’ can be used to express concepts of different sorts. Likewise for ‘square’ and ‘triangle’. From this perspective, understanding ‘red triangle’ is not a matter of calculating an application condition for the phrase. It’s more like recognizing the phrase as an invitation to combine a concept from your ‘red’-family with a suitable concept from your ‘triangle’-family. Likewise for ‘isosceles triangle’, ‘red apple’, and ‘red paint’.

They have noticed, and are inviting others to notice, the lack of a word for a concept worth expressing. Shakespeare was very good at this.

Speakers who coin new words—or make old words a little more polysemous by using them in new senses—are not tapping into antecedently established communal ways of thinking about things. They have noticed, and are inviting others to notice, the lack of a word for a concept worth expressing. Shakespeare was very good at this. Likewise, if a speaker looks up a word in a dictionary and then uses it with a specific sense, she is not merely intending to use the word as others do. A good definition gets you to *think about* things in ways that are in the ballpark of the concepts expressed by others who use the word.

Children may be like horses in some respects and like word-coiners or poets in other respects.

A child can think about apples on their own and later connect ‘apple’ with apple. But especially once kids have some words, they can be led to think about things in new ways and acquire new words (or new word senses) accordingly. Trying to figure out what others are saying can be like visiting a new place that prompts you to acquire concepts you wouldn’t otherwise have acquired. Some of these concepts may correspond to very indefinite descriptions like ‘a thing of that sort’. But a word introduced for such a concept can become polysemous as a child acquires more ways of thinking about things and deals with speakers who use words in many ways. (Polysemy may be inevitable, given that kids can’t treat every new use of a word as a use of a new word.)

\_\_\_

We can talk in these ways because our words are not tied to the things we talk about. Words are more flexible tools that let us express concepts in interesting ways, in part by helping us organize our concepts into extendable families.

\_\_\_

This leaves room for shared concepts, especially in small communities of specialists who can be consulted when their expertise is relevant. But we shouldn’t conclude that acquiring concepts is typically a matter of tapping into communal ways of thinking about things by acquiring words that have somehow latched onto things. We can say that water is H2O, Diet Coke isn’t water, but the stuff from my well is. A book that was favorably reviewed may be too heavy to carry. We can talk in these ways because our words *are not* tied to the things we talk about. Words are more flexible tools that let us express concepts in interesting ways, in part by helping us organize our concepts into extendable families. And luckily, when humans lead each other to acquire new ways of thinking about things, the concepts we acquire are similar enough to make conversation worth the effort.
