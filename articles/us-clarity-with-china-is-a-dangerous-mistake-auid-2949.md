# US clarity with China is a dangerous mistake

*5 reasons the US benefits from ambiguity on Taiwan*

**By Peter Harris** | *17th September 2024* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,258 words*

![US clarity with China is a dangerous mistake](https://iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWyI4MDAiLCI1MDAiXQ/5-reasons-the-US-benefits-from-ambiguity-on-Taiwan.webp)

---

*War between China and the United States would end our civilisation as we know it. In the face of such risk, it may appear sound for all sides to declare their intentions to descalate the Taiwan Strait. [Peter Harris and Jared M. McKinney](https://press.armywarcollege.edu/monographs/964/) argue that this is mistaken and dangerous: strategic clarity lacks credibility, is unlikely to work, and could set off a third world war.*

China’s military drills in May, which surrounded Taiwan in a manner [reminiscent](https://apple.news/A5RBHqb6kQC2Rw2XUIgRX8A) of China’s “exercises” in 2022 after Nancy Pelosi’s visit to Taiwan, once again raised the prospect that sometime in the not-too-distant future China’s “drills” might become the real thing. [A Chinese invasion of Taiwan would](http://iai.tv/articles/only-states-can-wage-war-and-why-it-matters-auid-2923) be even harder to contain than Russia’s war in Ukraine or the conflict in Gaza. It might even be the spark that sets off World War III.

In Washington, the conversation is understandably focused on how the United States can best deter war in the Taiwan Strait. To those who regard Taiwan as the [ultimate domino](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/taiwan-catastrophe), one suggestion stands head and shoulders above the others: the idea that the United States must provide “strategic clarity” that it intends to defend Taiwan in the event of a Chinese invasion.

This proposal—[recently advanced](https://asia.nikkei.com/Politics/Defense/Taiwan-defense-strategic-ambiguity-needs-clarifying-U.S.-analyst) by Matt Pottinger, a former China hand in the Trump administration, for example—draws on the old adage that the best way to secure peace is to prepare for war. There is some logic to this. If China were convinced that invading Taiwan would bring about a ruinous war with the United States, perhaps no such invasion would ever take place. But as we show in a [new peer-reviewed article](https://doi.org/10.1093/ia/iiae071), there are at least five good reasons why a U.S. commitment to defend Taiwan ought not be made.

First, a U.S. commitment to strategic clarity would be what scholars of International Relations call an “incredible commitment”—that is, a promise that others have good reason to doubt. [Such commitments cannot](http://iai.tv/articles/strategic-ambiguity-doesnt-work-in-a-multipolar-world-auid-2902) by themselves be considered robust deterrents.

It is worth emphasizing that President Biden has already said that he would defend Taiwan, yet his repeated insistences have not been taken as evidence that strategic clarity already exists. Why not? The reason is that, in world politics, talk is often seen as cheap. Simply *saying* that the United States would defend Taiwan is not nearly enough to establish a credible deterrent.

For strategic clarity to have more of a deterrent effect, the United States would have to put its money—not to mention the lives of its Soldiers, Sailors, and Airmen—where its mouth is. In other words, it would have to take material steps to assure Beijing that when they say America will defend Taiwan, this constitutes a pledge that will be fully followed through on.

This is easier said than done, however, which constitutes the second reason for why strategic clarity would be hard to achieve in practice. Of course, Washington is no stranger to making its security commitments to other allies—Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, and NATO countries, for example—seem credible in the eyes of its adversaries. But in the case of Taiwan, the usual mechanisms for bolstering the credibility of “extended deterrence” are not readily available.

Most obviously, there is no chance of signing a mutual defense agreement with Taipei because the United States already agreed to end such a relationship with Taiwan in the 1970s in exchange for diplomatic recognition and strategic cooperation with Beijing. Indeed, the United States does not even recognize Taiwan as a sovereign nation capable of concluding binding defensive pacts.

Nor would it be prudent to establish a permanent U.S. base on Taiwan given China’s 2005 anti-secession law, which signals the conditions under which the PRC would pursue forceful unification with Taiwan. These conditions include the “fact” of Taiwan’s separation being accomplished or the possibility of peaceful unification being foreclosed.

Third, even if strategic clarity could be made credible in the eyes of Chinese strategists there’s no guarantee that this would have the intended effect of dissuading Beijing from attempting an invasion. On the contrary, telegraphing America’s strategic intentions ahead of time would actually simplify China’s task of planning for a successful invasion.

As matters stand under strategic ambiguity, Chinese planners face a conundrum: strike forward deployed U.S. forces as part of an invasion of Taiwan (and start a war with the United States that might have been unnecessary) or refrain from striking these forces in hopes that Washington might decline to intervene on Taiwan’s behalf, but risk a U.S. intervention during a moment of peak PRC operational weakness.

Clearly, the most propitious scenario for the United States and Taiwan is for China to be tempted to avoid striking the United States, which would allow U.S. forces to enter the war at a time of its choosing—for example, when China’s loaded troop transports are in the middle of the Taiwan Strait. Such a strike would produce the proverbial “million-man swim” and end any chance for China to achieve its objectives swiftly, or at all.

Only strategic ambiguity imposes this conundrum on China. Strategic clarity, in contrast, creates massive incentives for a Chinese first strike, which would undermine U.S. military power and create a potential window of vulnerability in which a landing could succeed. Viewed in this light, it makes little sense to adopt a strategic posture that invites war at a time of China’s choosing.

Fourth, relying on U.S. threats of war as a means of deterring China would have the unwanted effect of disincentivizing Taiwan and others from rapidly investing in better, more credible deterrents. Ideally, the [deterrence gap](https://press.armywarcollege.edu/monographs/964/) in the Taiwan Strait would be closed today by Taiwan’s autonomous ability to stop an invasion.

After all, direct deterrence—that is, when governments lay plans to provide for their own defense—is far and away the most credible form of deterrence. This is something that only Taiwan can offer. Admiral Lee Hsi-min, Taiwan’s former chief of the general staff, has [already outlined](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/210927_adm_lee_hoover_remarks_draft4.pdf) how Taiwan could achieve such deterrence, but since he left office in 2019, Taiwan’s military leaders have de-emphasized his asymmetric approach.

Finally, it is essential that U.S. leaders consider the opportunity costs of moving to strategic clarity. Bringing Taiwan back under the U.S. security umbrella would likely be viewed in Beijing as a repudiation of America’s long-standing [One China policy](https://www.csis.org/analysis/what-us-one-china-policy-and-why-does-it-matter). This would represent a sea change not just in US-China relations, but China-Taiwan relations, too. The prospect of meaningful political and economic cooperation—attenuated as they are already today—would be badly diminished, despite overlapping common interests when it comes to global economic stability, promoting regional peace, and mitigating the effects of climate change.

The risk of war in the Taiwan Strait is high and getting higher. But strategic clarity that the United States intends to defend Taiwan would not ineludibly serve the cause of peace. On the contrary, it might very well serve as [an accelerant to a war](http://iai.tv/articles/iran-has-fallen-for-israels-trap-auid-2813) that nobody wants. There are safer and more credible ways to deter China from invading Taiwan that do not upend fifty years of US-China relations. These deterrents ought to be tried first.
