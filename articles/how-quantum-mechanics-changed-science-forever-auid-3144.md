# How quantum mechanics changed science forever

*Rationality, anarchy, and bad science*

**By Marij van Strien** | *17th April 2025* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *1,935 words*

![How quantum mechanics changed science forever](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/Quantum-mechanics-upends-the-scientific-method.webp)

---

*Quantum mechanics is extremely successful and extremely weird. Its weirdness has made it controversial. It doesn’t even try to do what we often think scientific theories must do: it doesn’t try to describe reality. Karl Popper thought this went against science’s very essence. But philosopher Marij van Strien argues here that quantum mechanics’ success shows that science has no fixed rules: progress is often made by breaking from conventional standards of rationality. This needn’t leave us in anarchy, unable to distinguish between good and bad science, as some fear: we just need to be more pragmatic when it comes to judging whether a theory is any good.*

The theory of quantum mechanics is extremely successful: it accounts for a large range of experimental results, enables highly accurate predictions and [has led to many technological applications](http://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mechanics-gives-us-power-but-no-answers-john-horgan-auid-2451), such as lasers, the semiconductors found in every computer, nuclear energy and nuclear weapons. At the same time, it is a strange theory. People often quote Richard Feynman, who once said, “[I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aAgcqgDc-YM&t=483s).” It has [weird and counterintuitive](http://iai.tv/video/the-madness-of-quantum-jim-baggott) implications, and there has always been an air of mystery about it; it is telling how often the term “quantum” appears in alternative medicine, self-help books, and [texts on spirituality and mysticism](http://iai.tv/articles/quantum-mysticism-is-a-mistake-philip-moriarty-auid-2437).

\_\_\_

How can a theory be so successful without bringing actual understanding?

\_\_\_

Quantum mechanics deals with processes at the atomic and subatomic scale. Part of what makes this theory strange is that it does not do what we usually expect from a scientific theory: it does not give a description of processes taking place in nature, but merely tells you how to calculate probabilities of measurement outcomes. For example, you can calculate the probability that if you measure the position of a particle, such as an electron, you will find it at a particular location. However, you will not be able to determine where it will be found exactly: quantum mechanics is indeterministic and gives you no more than a probability. Furthermore, you cannot simply assume the particle always *has* a well-defined position. [Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qt-uncertainty/) says that we cannot simultaneously assign an exact position and an exact momentum to a particle: the more precisely the momentum of a particle is given, the less precisely its position can be specified, and vice versa. In the double-slit experiment, particles are fired through slits towards a screen: you can specify where they start and where they end up, but you cannot specify where they were in between without getting into problems. In short: the equations of quantum mechanics cannot straightforwardly be interpreted as describing physical processes, and it is not easy to supplement them with a story of what is actually going on at the atomic level .

It is partly for this reason that many have felt that despite its impressive successes, [quantum mechanics fails to offer an actual understanding of what happens at the quantum scale](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/do-we-really-understand-quantum-mechanics/55F7E65EE05AF230012AE269814E1B7D) – which raises the question whether this *can* be understood. This is a puzzling situation: how can a theory be so successful without bringing actual understanding?

Given the success and prestige of quantum mechanics as well as its strangeness, it is not surprising that it has raised the interest of philosophers of science. Karl Popper, for example, [wrote extensively](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/edit/10.4324/9780203713990/quantum-theory-schism-physics-bartley-iii-karl-popper) about quantum mechanics, starting from the 1930s. He felt that there was something deeply wrong with the theory. The main issue for Popper was exactly the issue described above: quantum mechanics merely tells you how probable various measurement outcomes are, and says nothing about what happens in between measurements. [In Popper’s view, this went against the very essence of science.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02698595.2023.2207404?af=R) Scientists should try to understand the world and should aim to describe what actually happens – this is what science is ultimately about. According to Popper, the fact that quantum physicists did not try to develop such an understanding could be explained by the fact that quantum mechanics is both very difficult to interpret and very successful in its applications: this led to a focus on technical development, while deeper questions about what is actually going on were simply put aside.

In 1952 a young philosopher from Vienna (also the birthplace of Popper) came to work with Popper in London. This was Paul Feyerabend, who would later become notorious for his views on science. Feyerabend initially fully agreed with Popper’s criticism of quantum physics. He emphasized that in the development of scientific theories it is generally more fruitful to formulate detailed hypotheses about what might be going on and to search for explanations, rather than merely focusing on what can be measured and observed.

\_\_\_

By making some extra assumptions, Bohm was able to develop a theory of quantum physics in which particles do always have a well-defined position.

\_\_\_

Feyerabend gradually realized that in the case of quantum mechanics, this is easier said than done. He came to think of Popper’s criticisms as superficial. Since, for example, Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle is built into the theory itself, it is impossible to develop an account of what actually happens at the atomic level within the framework of the existing quantum theory – not just in practice but in principle, too. So if we did want to try to say what happens at the atomic level, we’d need to develop a rival theory, based on different concepts. Developing such a theory is exactly what Feyerabend’s friend and colleague David Bohm tried to do. In 1952, Bohm proposed an [alternative account of quantum mechanics](https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.85.166), which, though not quite yet a radically new theory of the sort Feyerabend (and Bohm himself) envisioned, cast an interestingly different light on quantum phenomena. By making some extra assumptions, Bohm was able to develop a theory of quantum physics in which particles *do* always have a well-defined position – though it has some other peculiar properties. The fact that quantum physicists largely ignored Bohm’s proposal showed in Feyerabend’s view that [they dogmatically held on to the established theory](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0039368195000058), despite its flaws, and refused to consider alternatives. Feyerabend thus had a strong opinion about quantum physics: to overcome current difficulties and make real progress, physicists should try to start from the bottom again and develop a fundamentally new way of thinking about quantum phenomena.

This all changed in 1965, when Feyerabend had a conversation with the physicist Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker. Feyerabend made his standard complaints about the dogmatism of quantum physicists and the need for a radically new approach, and von Weizsäcker responded with a detailed historical account of the development of quantum mechanics: a complex narrative of physicists exploring diverse ideas, trying out various approaches and grappling with experimental findings. Feyerabend found that he had nothing to say in response:

Compared with this rich pattern of facts, principles, explanations, frustrations, new explanations, analogies, predictions, etc., etc., my plea seemed thin and insubstantial. It was well enough argued, but the arguments occurred in outer space, as it were; they had no connection with scientific practice. For the first time I felt, I did not merely think about, the poverty of abstract philosophical reasoning ([*Killing Time*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/K/bo3638505.html), p. 141).

For many years he had thought and written about quantum mechanics, motivated by the idea that there was something wrong with this strange theory; but now his criticisms seemed to evaporate.

\_\_\_

Science does not have fixed methodological rules, and sometimes progress is made precisely by going against established methodology.

\_\_\_

Later, Feyerabend wrote: “Professor von Weizsäcker has prime responsibility for my change to ‘anarchism’ – though he was not at all pleased when I told him so” ([*Against Method*](https://www.versobooks.com/en-gb/products/1041-against-method?srsltid=AfmBOooy5JI7uf5pI2iFom6nLngr_jkU6jBERH_1dqysppBpzy42sAzG), p. 262). In 1975, *Against Method* appeared, the work for which Feyerabend became best known. In this book, he presents a controversial “anarchistic” view on scientific methodology. He argues, in contrast to Popper, that science does not have fixed methodological rules, and that sometimes progress is made precisely by going against established methodology. He mentions Niels Bohr, one of the founding fathers of quantum mechanics, as an example of a creative scientist who was unafraid of breaking methodological rules. Quantum mechanics is indeed a strange theory which does not do what we usually expect from a scientific theory; but if Bohr had held on to strict ideas about what scientific theories should be like, for example that one should aim to give complete and consistent descriptions of processes taking place in nature, he would never have been able to develop quantum mechanics. We should therefore not be too restrictive in our ideas about the methods and aims of science. Feyerabend criticizes the tendency among philosophers of science to specify what the “right” scientific methodology is: [scientists do not need philosophers telling them how to do science](http://iai.tv/video/philosophy-bites-back). It is not hard to see how this view could have developed from his conversation with von Weizsäcker, during which Feyerabend himself was made to feel that he was inappropriately telling scientists how to do science.

The conclusions which Feyerabend arrives at in *Against Method* are extreme. If there is no fixed scientific methodology and science has no fixed aims, there is nothing essential that sets science apart from enterprises like astrology. Feyerabend questioned the high standing which science has in society, and his views often go in the direction of science skepticism; he even became known as the [worst enemy of science](https://academic.oup.com/book/49888?login=false). Feyerabend sometimes purposely defended outrageous views; he enjoyed being controversial and shocking his audience. There is a value in this: it can be good to have one’s basic assumptions challenged, and his work tends to be fun to read. But there is a danger as well. His arguments can easily be misused, for example to deny the scientific consensus in climate science. Especially in our current times in which scientific expertise is already under great pressure, we cannot let Feyerabend’s attacks on the authority of science go unchallenged.

\_\_\_

Our convictions of what a scientific theory should look like may be too narrow and restrictive.

\_\_\_

It is often enlightening to look at the context within which ideas were developed. For Feyerabend, this means seeing how [some of his views on science originate from his work on quantum physics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0039368118303364?casa_token=9h_7-7L556sAAAAA:6btQckva5IQsxzziBS2YhAAnrTJccincBx-577VFsn7LRrBpNFbg5rHbbbVZKI5OaPGzyC-gIAU). Quantum mechanics seems to violate what a scientific theory should look like, and Feyerabend came to appreciate that our convictions of what a scientific theory should look like may be too narrow and restrictive. It was partly this realization that set him on a path to thinking that science has no fixed methodology and ultimately to question the authority of science. However, we can learn from his views of quantum mechanics without arriving at such extreme conclusions.

Nowadays, there are lively debates within the philosophy of physics about how to make sense of quantum mechanics. Various interpretations of quantum mechanics have been developed, including David Bohm’s, [which continues to be further developed](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-bohm/). It may be possible to understand quantum mechanics after all, or it may be that we have to change our conception of what it means to understand something. But meanwhile, we should not lose sight of the fact that the theory of quantum physics we have works very well. It is significant that we can indeed tell that quantum mechanics is successful. Despite the peculiarities of quantum mechanics, its success is clear from the fact that physicists can work with it, from the accuracy of its predictions and the fact that it can be used in many types of experiments and applications (the same cannot be said about astrology, or, for that matter, about “quantum healing”). This shows that we may have to be more open about the nature of science, but also that in practice we are nevertheless often able to differentiate between legitimate science and non-science.
