# From the Beatles to Bowie, genius is contagious

*How the greats became great*

**By Tony D. Sampson** | *4th September 2026* | *Read time: approx. 10 mins* | *2,035 words*

![From the Beatles to Bowie, genius is contagious](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/from-the-beatles-to-david-bowie-genius-is-contagious.webp)

---

*We think of creative genius as something a rare few just have, but digital theorist **Tony D. Sampson** argues this is a myth. Using Gabriel Tarde's theory of social contagion and Harold Bloom's 'anxiety of influence,' he shows that even the best-known creative geniuses *—*  from Homer and Brahms to the Beatles and Bowie — weren't the source of their own originality. Instead, they were skilled borrowers, copying and reworking what came before them, and who gets recognised for it depends less on raw talent than on privilege and access. Genius isn't a possession, it's a contagion.*

Given the recent buzz around the genius author behind the *Odyssey*, it’s worth recalling [Christopher Hitchens’ doubt that Homer was actually a single living individual](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/dhVISyFlt3c). Hitchens’ proposition unsettles a deeply entrenched cultural belief. Our encounters with historical figures are often frozen in time, like marble statues or bronze busts atop pedestals in the formal hush of museums and galleries. These institutions train us to see greatness solely in discrete human form, providing a false impression that only certain individuals possess innate, extraordinary qualities that transcend ordinary mortals. The very essence of their genius; the undeniable source from which all that is [original and culturally valuable](https://iai.tv/video/the-crisis-of-the-new) spreads through society. Creativity established through engagement with others, implicated in privileged social networks or the dynamics of public reception, takes a backseat to something of substance that is *always already* there.

While clearly not denying the existence of individual geniuses, through a sustained figural reframing, I will contend that genius itself is not a self-contained wellspring of essence. Similar to Gilbert Simondon’s theory of [psychic and collective individuation](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/cpo/article/id/42/download/pdf/), the philosophic question here pivots from a question concerning *what this individual is* understood in terms of substance and essence, to an enquiry about states of stability and metastability: *how individuals come to be, and how they persist in becoming*. The elevated figures of creative genius, cast in bronze and marble, are relational artifacts. They are time-linked embodiments caught up in social currents, propelling certain individuals into the archival light, while sweeping others, often just as talented, invariably less privileged, into the obscurity of forgotten creative endeavors. From an [event and contagion theory](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5749/j.ctttsp1n) perspective, we will see how the solitary genius narrative fundamentally misreads how contaminations of social influence and fame operate to insinuate certain artists into public consciousness across time.

My approach does not deny the creative brilliance of Homer, or for that matter Brahms, the Beatles or David Bowie. Crucially though, it points to a tendency to mistake the *temporal resonance* in which their individuation occurs for an unaffected *personal emergence.*Which is to say, there is a tendency to see geniuses in their discrete human (individual) form without acknowledging the individuation of creativity in relation to external potentialities and capacities that permit creativity itself to echo through time. Along these lines, events are not a static backdrop to emergent genius. Events are the very medium through which individuality and creativity persistently surface. To isolate individuals from these social currents is to arrive at an apolitical, transcendent, essentialist tautology: the revered are revered simply because they exist as such.

**From the shoulders of giants to rhizomatic relation**

Our reconfiguration of genius begins in the sciences, where Newton famously noted the inseparability of his brilliance from the giants who preceded him.Newton’s lofty metaphor usefully mapped a chronology of influence. Yet, he framed these influential subjects in deceptively hierarchical genealogies. Arguably, influence unfolds through more indiscernible horizontal histories.  Like Deleuze and Guattari’s botanical metaphor of the rhizome, [influence resonates through relational, turbulent currents, intricate knots, and contingent breaks](https://iai.tv/articles/biology-not-physics-holds-the-key-to-reality-auid-3163).Unlike arboreal geniuses, branching outward like a tree, rhizomatic influence emerges without origin; [multi-directional, unpredictable, collectively entangled](https://iai.tv/articles/a-theory-of-everything-will-never-work-at-all-scales-auid-3175). Where hierarchical genealogies lean on noble lineages, rhizomes reveal a world in which creativity emerges from unpredictable encounters, borrowings, and accidents.

But it is a philosophical oversight to [render the spreading of rhizomatic influence entirely arbitrary](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816670055/virality/). The horizontal movement of influence becomes interlaced with vertical structures. Influence is never fully random; it is socially patterned and politically pressured contamination. To grasp these contingent entanglements, we need to trace the rhizome to Deleuze’s precursor, the sociologist [Gabriel Tarde](https://sciencespo.hal.science/hal-01027760/document). In books like [*Laws of Imitation*](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=TPAtAAAAIAAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PR2&dq=Laws+of+Imitation+&ots=fuAX4fyzfm&sig=7j7WfvaOLiC8xeDJCHHs7sQ32_8) and [*Monadology and Sociology*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1410631/1/Monadology_and_Sociology.pdf), Tarde offers a contagion theory of influence composed of countless autonomous micro-subjects whose primary activity is the physical and mostly nonconscious psychic repetition of imitation.

**Monads, Clinamen and universal repetition**

Inspired by [Leibniz’s monad metaphor](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/2183641.pdf) (tiny living entities mirroring the entire universe from a unique perspective), Tarde’s society of imitation is composed at amicrolevel ofinterpenetratingpsychosocial connections.Unlike crude Neo-Darwinist memes or copy-paste social mechanics,imitation spreads according to the law of *universal repetition:* wave-like vibratory and generative radiation, significantly guided by *contagions of example*. These examples are not socially isolated; their openness to contamination is central to my argument. Which is to say that geniuses do not simply appear out of nowhere. They are cultivated through the currents of social encounter; buffeted and shaped by the politics of influence; privilege, patronage, access, opportunity, determination, serendipity: forces that are never neutral. Like monads, they are mirroring social subjects open to dynamic interplays where the “triumphant” imitated can become rivalrous and collaborative imitators. For Tarde, counter-imitation shapes outcomes as fully as [imitation itself](https://iai.tv/articles/theres-no-such-thing-as-ai-art-auid-3621). Innovation is a repetition of these events of conflict and synthesis—*across time*.

Tarde’s law of universal repetition profoundly influenced Deleuze’s [*Difference and Repetition,*](https://arsenal.arch.ethz.ch/site/assets/files/1790/voluptas-qen0e2k4fhntwyj72cnv-pdf.pdf) wherein recurrence produces variation rather than more of the same. Later in [*Logic of Sense*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.3366/j.ctvxcr939), Deleuze similarly frames events as repetition and return, this time invoking Lucretius’s Clinamen, an atomic swerve that disrupts and renews. The metaphor of the Clinamen proves vital here, as Harold Bloom, developing Lucretius independently, offers a strategic model to show how poetic genius emerges from imitative and counter-imitative encounters.

The first of Bloom’s six ratios, the Clinamen itself, is a swerve a poet must make in order to evade paralyzing comparisons with their precursor. It provides a necessary correction or completion of something missing in the original. Tessera then fills this vacuum with a token of recognition imbued with new, antithetical meaning. Kenosis, closest to repetition and difference, is the poet’s endeavor not to become a simple echo. Paradoxically, they accept a derivative status while retroactively draining the precursor’s originality to fortify their own strength as a survivor of influence. In Daemonization, the poet frames inspiration in generalized uniqueness, exaggerating their genius by means of comparison. The penultimate self-purgation, Askesis, emphasizes separate individuality by stressing the precursor’s limitations. Finally, Apophrades, an *Athenian Return of the Dead*, is the triumphant moment when the later poet opens their work deliberately to the precursor’s influence, absorbing and surpassing it. It now appears as if the precursor’s work derives from the later poet’s, uncannily reversing influence.

By way of illustration, [Kevin Korsyn traces Bloom’s *Anxiety of Influence* to relations between Brahms’s music and his precursor Chopin’s compositions](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/853998.pdf). Brahms traverses all six ratios, swerving into a subtle but crucial correction of Chopin’s influence, pushing it in new directions before absorbing and surpassing him retroactively. Similarly, the relationship between Beethoven, Mozart and Haydn is fraught with rivalries that shaped their music. They form a line of triumphant atoms whose influence becomes a contagion that later composers like Brahms and Chopin must swerve from, empty out, and finally complete in their work to establish their own place in the canon.

For Bloom, the successful completion of the six revisionary ratios does not dispel individual genius; it helps to secure it. Poets emerge not as mere disciples but as precursors in their own right. Bloom’s treatment of Oscar Wilde is revealing in this light. He dismisses the writer as a “weak poet” who “lacked the strength to overcome his anxiety of influence.” As a self-confessed disciple of Coleridge, Wilde admitted his own failings. As one of his literary characters, Lord Wotton sneers in *Dorian Gray*, to be influenced is to be an “echo of someone else’s music.” Again, for Bloom, this is precisely the fate the genius poet must escape. This is a struggle at the “central agon of western literature,” beginning with Plato’s philosophical swerve around the irrational, immoral and imitative poetic truth of Homer to contemporary precursors and followers.

Despite describing the anxiety of influence as contamination, Bloom’s ratios fall short of fully capturing the full temporal dynamics and epidemiologic nature of contagion.The Beatles swerved away from their early rock’n’roll imitations of Little Richard’s gospel whoops and falsetto shrieks to novel experiments in the recording studio. However, the latter avant-garde techniques of *Revolver* and *Sgt. Pepper* are not reducible to Lennon and McCartney’s struggle with their precursors. Before working with the Beatles, George Martin produced the Goon Show, a radio program that acted as a laboratory for studio sound inventions. The Beatles’ Clinamen rhizomatically associates them with Martin’s own swerve away from precursors like Debussy, Ravel, and Bernard Herrmann.

***“You know, I’ll be free / Just like that bluebird”***

Avian metaphors abound in David Bowie’s relationship with his many precursors. Unlike that bluebird in his song Lazarus, he never seems to be truly free from influence. Following Bloom, Bowie’s persistent swerve to mastery makes his abundant precursors seem, in retrospect, to have been leading up to his own work. His career is nevertheless more like a murmuration: a constantly morphing swarm of borrowed gestures, each swerve generating a new formation.Often criticized for being a cultural magpie, he exceeds Bloom’s *Anxiety of Influence* through uninhibited borrowings from Little Richard, The Beatles, Velvet Underground, Kraftwerk, Lindsay Kemp, Bertolt Brecht, William S. Burroughs, J.G. Ballard, George Orwell, and more. To be sure, Bowie is an artist who raises imitation to a new art form. Like [Deleuze and Guattari’s observation of the Stage-Maker bird](https://www.performancephilosophy.org/journal/article/view/145/265), whose art emerges through collecting leaves and colorful debris to create a display marking its territory, Bowie’s did not simply imitate his influences but appropriated them, rearranged them, and staged them to create a new and continuously shifting territory.

**The politics of imitation**

While Bloom’s ratios effectively map the open-endedness of poetic influence, he does not look beyond the aesthetic rivalry of notable geniuses, problematically endeavoring to keep politics out of literary criticism. Bloom’s disdain for the politicizing social histories of “Foucault, Marx and political feminism” removes the creative subject from the unpredictable contagion of worldly events most creatives have to endure. Bloom’s own anxiety seems to be a deeply personal, Oedipal struggle that purposefully brackets out the persistence of contaminating political forces. But I would argue that the Clinamen is not merely psychological. It is social, economic and intensely political. This is as important today as it was when Bloom conceived his contaminations of influence.  The hierarchies currently entangled in the rhizomes of creative genius restrict free access to the arts; cuts to arts and humanities education ensure that widening access is stalled from the get-go, as privilege ties a knot in the flow of creativity. The swerve is not random; it is shaped as much by privilege, access, patronage, and opportunity as it is by ingenuity or chance encounter. The individuation of [creativity does not just crop up out of nowhere without a “point of reference.”](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/opinion/profound-threat-exeters-humanities-reality-i-cannot-face) Even if an emerging genius completes all six ratios, his or her place in the canon is never truly fixed until he or she becomes fully institutionalized. Where Bloom sees only personal drama, we need to see collective contagions where chance encounters with influential flows are filtered and obstructed by hierarchies, institutional membership and capricious events. The politics of the swerve is not simply observed in individual rivalry; it is the unequal distribution of influential power that decides who gets to be a triumphant resonating atom and who remains, or will eventually become, an obscure echo.

Ultimately, the contagion of examples like Homer, Brahms, the Beatles, and Bowie do not disprove this political account of contagion; they exemplify it. Their mastery lay not in escaping influence but in their capacity to swerve, to counter-imitate, to transform the *contagion of example* into something that, through sheer persistence in collective attention, comes to feel like the very essence of genius itself. The greatest artists are not those who possess some inner essence of genius, but those whose influence proves most contagious.
