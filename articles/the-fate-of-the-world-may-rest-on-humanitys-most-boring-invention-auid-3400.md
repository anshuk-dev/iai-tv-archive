# The fate of the world may rest on humanity's most boring invention

*Global risks, insurance, and the future of government*

**By Louis W. Pauly** | *27th October 2025* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,522 words*

*In an uncertain world, it can be difficult to know who to trust and which voices to listen to. In the face of such uncertainty, perhaps the best people to look to are those who have been containing risk in a chaotic world all along, the insurers. Professor Louis Pauly argues that amid high profile examples of the state of Florida being largely uninsurable and Australia being described as “fast becoming an uninsurable nation”, we need to look to this industry to cut through the political noise and build solutions based on shared risk, joint responsibility, and cooperation before we all pay a premium on catastrophe.*

We have always lived in an uncertain world. But, amidst the uncertainty surrounding us, human beings have become quite adept at carving out zones of risk that can potentially be governed. The social and financial technology of insurance developed on the foundation of this experience-based ingenuity. Today, when political polarisation makes it ever more difficult to govern mounting global risks, insurance and its history may open the road to effective solutions.

Coinciding with the appearance of early insurance firms was the state form of government, the ultimate provider of protection. Together, if mainly for the world’s relatively prosperous societies over the past few centuries, the insurance industry and the states supporting it and providing it with backstops worked to pool and cover various kinds of risk. This entailed aggregating and transferring the potential costs of insurable perils from those less able to manage them to others willing and able to bear them. Their specific consequences included the provision of physical and financial assistance during emergencies, the bolstering of societal resilience after accidents and catastrophes, and the supply of data-driven assessments aimed at preventing or preparing for future disasters. Without them, we would today be living in another world. Insurance made possible the dominant socio-economic system of [modern capitalism](https://iai.tv/video/the-holy-grail-of-economics), which now intricately if variably links states, societies, and markets across the planet.

Beyond the vital issue of how that system can be reformed for the benefit of those left out, an urgent question confronts all of humanity today. In the face of new [risks still shrouded in some uncertainty](https://iai.tv/articles/it-is-time-for-the-world-to-embrace-uncertainty-auid-2986), like those arising from global warming and the dawn of artificial intelligence, has the system itself reached its limit? A story well told by economic historians puts the question into practical perspective.

It was hardly unusual in May 1861 for a strong south wind to blow through the mountain pass leading into Glarus, an emerging industrial town in the eastern part of Switzerland.  Late one night, a small fire broke out on its edge. Within hours, eight residents were killed and 793 buildings, including two-thirds of its homes, were destroyed or damaged. When the smoke cleared, its citizens faced a monumental recovery challenge. Most of the households involved were desperate. They had no insurance. Forty percent of the ones who did were underinsured, and not many had any kind of coverage for the contents of their homes. The most exposed private insurance company found itself on the hook for one quarter of all covered losses. It was saved from bankruptcy when government agencies agreed to absorb a large chunk of unpayable debt. Pre-purchased public insurance covered some other losses, but even those policies had been badly under-priced. Town and regional governments themselves soon faced the prospect of bankruptcy.

The scale of Glarus’s catastrophe made it famous, but the event was hardly unique in pointing to the need for better instruments to help modern industrializing societies cope with rising risks. Indeed, from the late seventeenth century until a decade after the Glarus disaster, urban fires had destroyed much of London, Hamburg, and Chicago.  By then some dangers were well understood. Why were so many unprepared?

A better and more comprehensive insurance system in all of those cities might have effectively shared the burdens of recovery. In anticipation of staggering losses, moreover, government officials and insurers might have worked to make insurance more attractive and affordable. As well, they may have encouraged preventive measures, including better zoning, building standards, and fire safety protocols. Governments, in particular, may also have taken advantage of the observation that people appear more willing pay premiums for personal insurance protection than they are to pay taxes whose purposes can seem opaque, distant, and debatable.

 Over time, many governments did eventually discover that both methods of preparing for and dealing with disasters could be useful. It nevertheless took time and experience to make the leap from pooling risks and the private or public capital to cover them from local levels of government to higher levels.

But what about risks that transcend the boundaries of states? What urban fires were in previous centuries, continental wildfires, extremely damaging hurricanes and rising sea levels are indicators of a dramatically changing global climate today. Private insurance providers and the reinsurance firms that insure them and increase their underwriting capacities  are innovating to try and cover new risks, but they are also pulling back in some areas in the context of [mounting uncertainty](https://iai.tv/video/how-do-you-solve-a-problem-like-uncertainty). Some governments at various levels are also experimenting with new instruments of risk management. But much more seems required to cope with a widening array of global risks. In the face of these enormous, all-encompassing risks, a new chapter in the history of insurance is being written in our time.

New financial instruments arising from the [discovery of risk](https://iai.tv/video/lies-damned-lies-and-economics) and the inception of probabilistic reasoning began to spread around the world five centuries ago.  Where fate, luck, and the divine had traditionally been viewed as the sources of good and bad events, data-based decisions could now build a sense of confidence in a future at least partly under human control. Ancient instincts to speculate could be directed toward rational saving and investment strategies. More generally, societal resilience could be enhanced by pooling risks, managing them, and facilitating burden sharing. As insurance practices developed, often on the basis of painful experience and better data analysis, so too did the idea of expanding the capital available for relief and recovery by insuring primary providers of insurance.

By tapping into larger and deeper capital markets, reinsurance firms, like Swiss Re, which grew up in the wake of the Glarus catastrophe, and Munich Re, vastly increased the capacity of insurers to pool risks and lengthen planning horizons. They helped fill protection gaps in between the perils local insurers could cover and those relevant governments were willing and able to cover. Such risks are only insurable in private markets, however, if entrepreneurial primary or secondary firms can figure out how to make a profit by covering them. By providing those firms with subsidies or guarantees or by directly underwriting unprofitable risks, governments on their own can sometimes stretch the frontiers of insurability. Such decisions are fundamentally political, and the ability to make them obviously varies. They are, ultimately, fiscal in nature. Even when risks are quite well understood, though, some governments may be willing to let uninsured losses fall where they may. Others may simply lack the capacity to do much else.

After the Glarus fire, explicit or implicit governmental support for private insurance and reinsurance markets became routine in Switzerland. Not long afterwards, the citizens of many other reasonably well-off countries started expecting their governments to begin closing protection gaps, and sometimes also wealth gaps created by the profit-making system within which private insurers thrived.

Today, firms with global ambitions as well as the states licensing and supervising them collectively face the challenge of adapting and designing anew politically tolerable instruments capable of governing risks that cannot be contained or adequately insured within established political boundaries. For example, it is predicted that by 2050 on current trends sea level rise will put [$35bn of US real estate](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/35-billion-worth-of-real-estate-could-be-underwater-by-2050/) underwater and an area the size of Connecticut. This is but one example amid the increased prevalence and strength of [hurricanes, storm surges](https://www.c2es.org/content/hurricanes-and-climate-change/) and droughts.  Our political appetite for insurance is being put to the test.

We know that private insurance and reinsurance price signals can be invaluable guides for public policy at local levels. Why discount them at the global level? When markets will not provide cover for business-continuity risks posed by cyber-criminals, for example, public sources of financial support for post-disaster resilience and risk reduction must be found and ultimately shared. In their absence, social and political structures can quickly become brittle.

We do not need to imagine what can happen then, for we witnessed such fragility repeatedly over the past century. In 1931, for example, national efforts to backstop and contain financial risks failed. The idea of forestalling cascading bank failures with collaborative monetary and fiscal responses seemed anathema in leading countries. Economic depression and systemic war soon followed. Since the end of World War II, the potential social costs of an expansive array of other cross-border risks, some measurable and some only plausibly intuited have loomed ever larger.

Perhaps we have lately been more lucky than smart. Since 1945, however,  pragmatic international collaboration deepened, especially in the financial arena. Sometimes it took the form of explicit co-insurance facilities through such organizations as the World Bank. During the Global Financial Crisis of 2008, it took the form of informally coordinated official reinsurance instruments, like central bank reserve swaps. During subsequent crises in Europe, it involved indirect reinsurance through joint governmental guarantees of bonds sold to investors around the world. Such bailouts had  significant political repercussions. Perhaps their causes and consequences could have been handled better, but refraining from them would plausibly have led to worse disasters. In such cases, contentious political questions remain. Who will ultimately pay, and how will they do so?

The reasonable urge not to push our luck in other arenas of risk may well motivate the next generation of political experiments aimed at transcending self-defeating impulses to forget the past and dream of self-reliance. Again, risks that cannot be contained within national silos are obviously increasing. The COVID-19 pandemic surely pointed us in the direction of necessary change with regard to vaccines and future anti-biotics, even if a cacophony of discordant and confused voices later made clear thinking more difficult. Despite disarray and backsliding in some national capitals, the stage is set today for experiments in deeper social and political collaboration when the insurance techniques of risk pooling, diversification, and transfer reach current political limits. Some of the risks associated, for example, with global warming, biodiversity loss, and the rapid development of generative artificial intelligence may be insurable. Functional instruments could take the form of novel private or public insurance arrangements backed by more robust collaborative institutions that take a step beyond voluntary intergovernmentalism. Some places in our world, like Europe, have already discovered the federalizing logic of insurance and reinsurance. Unless it is followed through, just to cite one example, there will never be an efficient, competitive, and safe European capital market.

Not every risk needs to be governed at higher than national levels of political aggregation, but some certainly do. Can we really imagine that the end-point in the evolution of a world health system that began with sanitary conferences of the 19th century is reflected in the current structure of an embattled World Health Organization? Can truly global capital markets continue evolving without reliable and legitimate arrangements for backstops and oversight? And where risks are discernibly existential in nature and inherently global in scope, do we not already understand that the more assertive matching common interests and common governing capacities cannot be avoided? On the basis of such understanding, history suggests that the language of insurance and mutual protection will be useful in building support for essential cross-border and cross-generational transfers of opportunities, burdens, and responsibilities.

With that history in mind, my new book [*Insuring States in an Uncertain World: Towards the Collaborative Government of Complex Risks*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/insuring-states-in-an-uncertain-world/0DE52749C8B132B7ADD92F7BC59A34BF) (Cambridge University Press) assesses the political terrain that today’s leaders must traverse in the search for functional if limited arrangements to govern inexorable global risks. It looks back to highlight the conditions under which risk awareness and novel insurance techniques evolved from the earliest stages of capitalism to the international system within which we now live. At the dawn of that system, maritime insurance developed as trade expanded. Much later, deposit insurance gradually stabilized ever growing banking markets. After 1945, third-party liability insurance made possible private investment in nuclear power plants, and through focused multilateral treaty arrangements soon vastly increased the cross-national scale of such investment. During the 1970s, the International Monetary Fund was transformed into an effective insurance provider for member-states facing the prospect of default on their debts. In general, private insurance and reinsurance markets grew to cover a range of cross-border risks that were relatively simple in nature and relatively short-term in scope. Recently, moreover, the deepening of insurance-linked securities markets, including markets for so-called catastrophe bonds, has multiplied the volumes of private capital available to cover such risks as coastal flooding during hurricanes.

Evolving insurance and reinsurance practices reflected progressive change in the roles of both private and public authorities. They strengthened resilience in many societies and fostered cooperative governing experiments among willing nation-states. Without ignoring the contemporary realities of great power rivalry, populist backlashes, and rising authoritarianism, I defend the idea that collaborative judgments—instinctive, pragmatic, and informed by the experience and logic of insurance—can continue pushing us toward a more sustainable world.

A fundamental question nevertheless remains. In a world still organized around nation-states or characterized by civilizational fault lines, do we need actual catastrophes to motivate deeper experiments in global collaboration in the face of increasingly complex global risks? Before we come to grips with them, must we actually experience the nightmarish cataclysms that readily come to mind, for example, as biodiversity losses mount, microbes with pandemic potential adapt, and rapid advances in artificial intelligence remain unregulated? Hubris and myopia in government and business may well indicate an answer in the affirmative. But reason combined with the availability of the social technology of insurance points in a more promising direction.

After the great fire of Glarus, the credible prospect of similar future catastrophes spawned both the reinsurance industry and the insuring state. When markets for pooled risks began to reach their economic and political limits, the collaborating state started to emerge. Pragmatic innovation across leading societies is not bound to culminate in a new and stable inter-state balance between the demands of global solidarity and the necessity of retaining as much local control as feasible. But the ideology of self-reliance can only end in failure. Collaborative government in the face of complex global risks is not beyond the realm of imagination. If we can learn from it, the history of insurance justifies a realistic sense of hope.
