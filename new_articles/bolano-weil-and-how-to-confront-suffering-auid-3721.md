# Bolaño, Weil, and how to confront suffering

We are a culture obsessed with covering up pain. From painkillers to doomscrolling, we treat anything uncomfortable as a problem to be solved. Finn Janning, a philosophical counselor and essayist, argues that we should embrace discomfort as a central part of being human. Looking to the great Chilean author Roberto Bolaño and to the ethics of Simone Weil, he contends that only by focusing on real suffering can we live ethically and connect emotionally with each other.

In an interview, the Chilean writer Roberto Bolaño (1953 – 2008) stated: “The library is a metaphor for human beings or what's best about human beings, the same way a concentration camp can be a metaphor for what is worst about them. A library is total generosity.”

The secret behind the poetic and imaginative power of Bolaño’s work lies in a rare synthesis of radical attention and generosity—an affirmative practice. He does not merely reflect life; he expands it, turning existence itself into art.

\_\_\_

Bolaño is using fiction to create a space where what cannot be conceived is confronting us. This is painful. This is real.

\_\_\_

When Bolaño turns life into art, I am not referring to life in general but to *a* life understood as a particular form of existence or way of living. The most illustrative example is the novel *2666*, where he, in the fourth part, called "The Part About the Crimes," with shocking precision, describes how numerous young girls and women were mutilated and killed in Santa Teresa in Mexico (i.e., Ciudad Juárez). For instance, he writes: ”Two weeks later, in May 1994, Mónica Durán Reyes was kidnapped on her way out of the Diego Rivera School in Colonia Lomas del Toro… She was twelve years old… According to the forensic examination, she had been raped and strangled."

For more than 200 pages, the horror goes on. Through Bolaño's painful precision, life appears richer. Without pain there is no reality. Or as the French philosopher Simone Weil writes in her *Notebooks:* “Pain as evidence of the actual existence of the world.” This contrasts with  today’s society where nothing should hurt and where pain, often, is comforted through various forms of consumption, entertainment and pills. Often with the result that we become addicted to the pleasantness without exploring the pain underneath.

Despite the violation, mutilation, and killing, Bolaño uses these destructive facts to awaken our mutual responsibility. The detailed description activates the reader's senses and confronts the reader with the fact that they live in a world filled with unsolved femicides. Or to put it differently: Where everything is pleasant, nothing is real.

Recalling Bolaño’s use of the library as a metaphor for what is best about human beings, he treats life—in his writing—with delicacy, as if life were only on loan. However, in many parts of the world, lives are not treated with care and kindness but with violence and murder. If Bolaño aims at anything with his literature, it is liberation, because through freedom, the human being becomes capable of loving. For this reason, the heroes in many of his novels and short stories are poets, artists, or vagabonds. They are not afraid of confronting the dark sides of life.

The secret behind Bolaño’s poetry and fiction has nothing to do with inspiration or lofty vision. And yet, he wants to watch life and pass on what is worth passing on. Elsewhere in *2666*, he writes: "There's *nothing* inside the man who sits there writing. Nothing of himself … Nothing, I mean to say, that his wife, at a given moment, might recognize. He writes like someone taking dictation.”

This echoes Weil’s reflections in *The First and Last Notebook*: “Humility is attentive patience.” Humble here means not humiliated but being grounded and in contact with the earth. She speaks of an ethics of attention, where the person empties themselves to become no one. “In such a work all that I call ‘I’ has to be passive. Attention alone – that attention which is so full that the ‘I’ disappears – is required for me. I have to deprive all that I call ‘I’ of the light of my attention and turn it on to that which cannot be conceived.”

Bolaño is using fiction to create a space where what cannot be conceived is confronting us. This is painful. This is real.

The secret, thus, is not a dreamy vision, nor is the Chilean author trying to moralize his readers by telling us what to do or not do. On the contrary, he repeats that in life, which is worth repeating. What is worth repeating? The answer is found through the attentive approach, where the writer takes dictation or obeys as a way of seeing and listening. It resembles the mystical or contemplative attention of Weil. In Waiting for God she writes, “Attention is an effort … Attention consists of suspending our thought, leaving it detached, empty and ready to be penetrated by the object.” This form of attention, like Bolaño’s poets, is inactive and lacks any will. “Above all our thought should be empty, waiting, not seeking anything, but ready to receive in its naked truth the object which is to penetrate it.” Paying attention in this way opens for a truth worth repeating.

The starting point for a writer who sees themself as one taking dictation on behalf of life is humility. Bolaño is free to go where life takes him in his writing; he repeats whatever happens until it almost matches life. Full of the same energy.

Thus, the secret story of his fiction is what makes life livable; for him (and I assume many of his readers), it's the free and passionate living that often is found among poets, artists, and other dreamers, but dreamers who do not refer to some utopian ideal. Instead, they seem to live it daily.

The idea of focusing on “the secret story” of Bolaño’s oeuvre came to me on a warm late summer day while I sat on a bench on the beach in Barcelona. I sat and read one of the Chilean short stories called “Dentist” from *Last Evenings on Earth*, where he writes:

“But that’s where art comes from, he said: life stories. Art history comes along only much later. That’s what art *is*, he said, the story of life in all its particularity. It's the only thing that is really particular and personal… What I mean is the secret story… So now you’re wondering what I mean by the secret story? Asked my friend. Well, the secret story is the one we’ll never know, although we’re living it from day to day, thinking we’re alive, thinking we’ve it all under control and the stuff we overlook doesn’t matter. But every single damn thing matters!” (2006, 192).

Every damn thing matters. That is the secret. Yet, most people don't realize that perhaps because our focus is elsewhere, somewhere in the past or in the future, probably because we get too easily distracted, or perhaps, as Bolaño suggests: "We tell ourselves that art runs on one track and life, our lives, on another, and we don't realize that's a lie" (ibid, 192).

According to the Chilean writer, life and art cannot be separated. What connects our lives with art, or better, what makes a life art, is the secret story. I suggest that a lack of freedom makes us unaware because, instead of paying attention to "every damn thing," most people focus on what gives them status and prestige or makes them belong somewhere. Bolaño, of course, does not answer what the secret of life is; in the short story, before answering the friend, someone "started waving," pulling the narrator right back into the messiness of life.

In *The Savage Detectives*, Bolaño, almost like a mantra, writes: "No objective reason, anyway. But poetry (real poetry) is like that: you can sense it, you can feel it in the air, the way they say certain highly attuned animals (snakes, worms, rats, and some birds) can detect an earthquake" (p. 5). That poetic or animalistic attention drives his fiction. For the same reason, some people appear like ghosts, being there and not really being there, or they walk around like zombies.

\_\_\_

Bolaño succeeded, I think, because he did not separate life and art.

\_\_\_

Life touches and provokes emotions. It is difficult to give a definitive account of who, what, or how something influences what and how. It lacks a clear beginning or reason. Instead, Bolaño first focuses on what touches or moves people: encounters. He rarely specifies age or time. "At the time, I was twenty-something," he writes in “Sensini” from *Last Evenings on Earth*. Similarly, the story *Distant Star* opens the following way: “I saw Carlos Wieder for the first time in 1971, or perhaps in 1972.”

Life makes something possible; it happens, and that difference causes something new to arise. This relationship can also be described differently. When a person encounters something inexplicable, e.g., how someone moves their body or speaks about a poem, it activates a lust to explore it. Hunger saturates most of Bolaño’s work (i.e., a sexual lust or a creative power); he consumes life and opens new territories through this consumption. An intimate relationship exists between the past being produced, distributed, and consumed at the same time.

In the Spanish writer Javier Cercas’ novel *Soldiers of Salamis*, Bolaño appears, in what is likely his own words: "’You don't need the imagination to write novels,’ said Bolaño. ‘Just a good memory. Novels are written by combining different memories’” (2005, 149).

Such memories are example of reality of the past. In *Gravity and Grace* Weil writes: “Not to accept an event in the world is to wish the world did not exist.” Whether we liked what happened is not the question. It’s not a matter of being for or against but obeying and accepting that it did happen. This doesn’t lead to ethical avoidance or positivity. On the contrary, instead of wishful thinking and seducing imagination, we might respond differently to what we accept. It could be as simple as asking how so few people saw the femicides? How can we remain open to the horror?

For those familiar with Bolaño's writing, you can see repeated descriptions—almost down to the level of person and situation—that recur; for example, longer and shorter chunks of his earlier short stories appear in *The Savage Detectives* (especially part two). In this way, the affirmative approach to life and writing illustrates that memory is a map drawn in water. Memory is constantly being reorganized and composed. It is, therefore, not just about extracting something from memory; instead, it is about reinventing it now – that is creating a room for it to flourish. Remembering is an action that springs from this creativity, which springs from our capacity to pay attention. It is because of that that each story opens for an infinite past and future because the writer, like a seismographic snake, senses a movement coming from nowhere, going elsewhere. Instead of chasing the possible beginnings or ends, Bolaño stays with the movement as if movement literally is life. “We do not obtain the most precious gifts by going in search of them but by waiting for them,” Weil writes in *Waiting for God*.

Thus, if Bolaño’s secret is his hyper-attentiveness to each present moment that he then passes on, then his creation does not so much deal with inventing things, places, or persons; instead, he moves the same events and people around, thereby trying new variations or perspectives. More straightforwardly, he does so in the second part, *The Savage Detectives*, which consists of numerous interviews with people who have met the creators of an avant-garde poetry group, Arturo Belano and Ulises Lima.

It is tempting to categorize most of Bolaño’s work as autobiographical; however, according to him, this would be too obvious, since life and art cannot be separated. Yet, autobiographical does not mean self-portraits. It's about living an artistic life. Humble, curious, and compassionate, constantly on the lookout.

Another way in which humility shows itself is in the worship of others. Bolaño likes to refer to others who have given them something. In an interview, Bolaño even says reading is more important than writing because you gain experience while reading. When you read, you produce a more complex past, which can qualify your gaze—now and here —and actualize a more prosperous future. The past matters as it comes to life, here and now. Weil says something similar: “In our act of obedience to God, we are passive.” Only waiting, attention, silence and reading. Bolaño was not religious, but he spoke about literature with the same humility as Weil about God.

So, what did Bolaño want from life? "What I wanted was to live like a poet, even though today I wouldn't be able to specify what it meant to me to live like a poet,” he says before—classic Bolaño—he does exactly what he said he could not do: “For me, being a poet meant being revolutionary and completely open to all cultural manifestations, all sexual expressions, in the end, being open to every experience with drugs,” from the interview, "Positions are positions, and sex is sex" in *Roberto Bolaño: The Last Interview*. In another interview from the same collection, he says: “At heart, to make life a work of art.” What does that mean? It means that beauty is an integral part of life, or as Weil would say: “God is pure beauty.”

Bolaño succeeded, I think, because he did not separate life and art. Instead, he affirmed that life is beauty also when it is full of pain. Perhaps, it's pure romanticism, but it's the only romance worth repeating.
