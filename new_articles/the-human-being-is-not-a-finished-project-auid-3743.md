# The human being is not a finished project

*Human becoming is more important than human being*

**By Leon Tsvasman** | *26th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 17 mins* | *3,456 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![The human being is not a finished project](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-human-being-is-not-a-finished-project2.webp)

---

*We treat the human being as though we are a finished project. We are not. Humanism gave us dignity and protection, and modern society has become expert at educating, employing and managing humans within set roles. But this misses what it means to be human, argues philosopher **Leon Tsvasman**. To be human means to be in a constant state of becoming, towards an unknown potential. We must protect our agency in the face of AI automation, and build new institutions to enable this human potential.*

The humanist achievement harbors an ambiguity that becomes all the more apparent as the institutions founded upon it grow more technically proficient. Humanism liberated the dignity of the individual from inherited rank, utility, religious or political designation, and from the presumption of any authority to decide whose existence merits recognition. Nothing in my argument should be misconstrued as a trivialization of this achievement. Yet, the human being it protected was, at the same time, a creature already shaped within a specific web of survival imperatives, societal expectations, and institutional legibility. Protecting a human being and recognizing what that being might become are not the same thing; the remarkable success of the former may even obscure how rarely the latter has been attempted at all.

My concern, therefore, is neither the discovery of a supposedly authentic human nature nor the creation of a more highly developed specimen. Both endeavors would project into the future a definition whose very authority ought to be called into question. The question raised is less comforting: What if the human being—whose dignity civilization has learned to defend—remains, in crucial respects, the product of [conditions that oriented human life](https://iai.tv/articles/human-history-in-the-age-of-the-anthropocene-auid-1887) primarily toward the perpetuation of those very conditions? One can be free from the explicit command of a ruler and yet remain almost entirely occupied with reproducing a sense of relevance, without ever having been in a position to determine for oneself the criteria by which one is actually measured. To equate the lifting of an external prohibition with the emergence of an autonomous orientation is to confuse a necessary historical victory with a complete anthropology.

How does a human being become legible to modern civilization? Whether as a student, a customer, an employee, a patient, a citizen, or a taxable economic entity—or perhaps as a profile whose characteristics allow a system to anticipate one’s next action—these descriptions are by no means fictional; in many cases, they are even indispensable. Institutions cannot respond to a living being in its entirety. They reduce complexity in order to provide treatment, education, care, coordination, protection, and so on. The problem arises at a level that is rarely recognized as such. The categories developed to facilitate a specific practice or operation—through constant repetition and evident practical success—gain the authority to determine what is even considered relevant regarding the individual to whom that practice is applied. Whether a patient is being rendered medically quantifiable, a learner is being assessed, a citizen is being represented, an employee is being prompted to perform, or a user is being served, the question of who actually establishes the criteria by which these actions are defined as such disappears, becoming absorbed into the practice itself.

It is entirely possible to be comprehensively represented without ever developing the capacity for self-orientation.

This distinction occupied my thoughts long before the current debate surrounding artificial intelligence. My earlier work on communication, orientation, and constructivist cybernetics—[including a contribution to *Constructivist Foundations*](https://constructivist.info/3/2/084.tsvasman) on the possibility of being a “self-orienting subject”—explored the peculiar dual nature of intersubjective validity. There is no form of human orientation that could exist outside of language, relationships, or the resistance posed by an alternative point of view. The claim that [autonomy can be achieved by freeing oneself from all forms of conditioning](https://iai.tv/video/living-without-limits) is not only untenable but also conceptually vacuous. Yet, the acquired patterns—which are the very prerequisites for mutual understanding—may, over time, cease to be perceived as “patterns” at all. This socially stabilized validity (a necessary condition for a world to be shared) begins to appear as the world itself. Within this framework, one may certainly dispute statements, pursue alternatives, or even rebel against institutions—but one can never reach the level at which the validity-bestowing power of the framework itself could be called into question.

The problem is cybernetic in nature rather than moral. When a viable institution must make distinctions, these serve to reduce uncertainty—and what reduces uncertainty becomes repeatable; what is successfully repeated tends to be perceived as self-legitimating. This requires neither a hidden puppet master nor malicious intent. The efficiency of an established framework is entirely sufficient to continually regenerate a sense of its necessity within the minds of those who operate within it. Consequently, a purely political explanation of domination—however useful it may be in its own sphere—falls short of grasping the problem in its entirety. While a subject may formally possess the right to dissent, they have simultaneously adopted the entire framework of questions to which such dissent must necessarily refer.

The more adept a civilization becomes at [the tactical management of human existence](https://iai.tv/articles/humans-arent-special-and-why-it-matters-auid-3242), the less noticeable this strategic deficit grows. We admire the competent adult who earns a living, manages countless instruments and accounts, fulfills administrative obligations, advances a professional career, cares for others, masters new processes, adapts to ever-changing technologies, and remains socially functional within a world defined by constant transformation. All of this entails intelligence, discipline, and, at times, even courage. I would never devalue the practical labor of survival by dismissing it merely as “adaptation.” Yet, it would be equally mistaken to assume that a sophisticated capacity for adaptation constitutes the autonomy of the subject. A conditioned individual can spend an entire lifetime managing means, the mere accumulation of which is mistakenly taken for an enrichment of one’s ends. The fact that an activity demands attention does not in any way imply that it deserves the attention a person might sacrifice to it over the course of their life.

This calls for a distinction that has not yet been sufficiently developed within the humanist lexicon. Human dignity is inherent to a person, regardless of their development, achievements, or any demonstration of intellectual independence. The dignity of an individual who never attains a state of reflected autonomy is not diminished in the slightest. Integrity—as I use the term here—belongs to a different order: it concerns the capacity of an embodied subject to remain a coherent and accountable being throughout all their perceiving, attending, judging, and acting—as well as the consequences that follow—while simultaneously developing the ability to continually re-examine the criteria that guide them.

This cannot be reduced to moral rectitude, consistency of opinion, or the maintenance of a recognizable personal identity. On the contrary, integrity may require abandoning an identity whose coherence has become a means to avoid necessary corrections. Furthermore, the opposite of integrity is not simply “influence.” Without influence, there could be neither learning nor relationship. The crucial point is whether a subject can be transformed by a relationship without the very source of their orientation being co-opted by the relationship that brings about that transformation.

Such a demand only appears paradoxical if one assumes that an “origin” must signify an absolute beginning —one that cannot be further explained by causal factors. I use the term in a different sense. A subject becomes an “origin” not by retreating into private preferences, but by developing the capacity to devise and revise criteria—within a world that responds to those efforts. A criterion is not legitimized simply because one feels it belongs to oneself; empirical evidence can invalidate it, another subject can point out its shortcomings, and the consequences of its application may render it no longer tenable. It is essential to remain open to correction. What must not be abandoned is the possibility of taking responsibility for the criteria themselves—rather than merely choosing efficiently between courses of action to which some other order has already assigned a specific meaning.

We have created sophisticated freedoms of choice. This achievement should not be undervalued. Yet, the ability to choose a profession, an educational path, a consumer good, or an opinion does not reveal who established the available alternatives in the first place—nor why certain possibilities failed to enter the realm of choice altogether. A student who learns to provide the expected answer may possess extraordinary knowledge; a citizen who weighs in on every current controversy may express themselves with great eloquence. Whether such individuals have truly attained a greater degree of self-orientation cannot be inferred from these kinds of successes. Even an act of defiance remains derivative if the framework in question has already dictated what may even count as a point of contention. This is where the distinction between tactical and strategic relevance lies: it is not a matter of differentiating between short-term and long-term planning, but rather a distinction between acting intelligently within given conditions, on the one hand, and the capacity to challenge the validity of those very conditions for a potential future, on the other.

At this point, the conventional opposition between actuality and potentiality no longer suffices—unless one asks which of these two terms gets to set the standards for the other. Something that is given (an actuality) is by no means a flaw. Without forms stable enough to make action possible, there could be neither undertakings nor responsibility nor intelligible consequences. Likewise, one should not conceive of potentiality as an inventory of ready-made, alternative futures from which the best one could simply be selected through improved calculation. What I term “potentiality” transcends any current arrangement of actual possibilities; for the criteria by which a possibility is recognized as such are themselves historically contingent, subject to revision, and never exhaustive. It is therefore entirely possible for a society to produce constant innovations while remaining trapped in an increasingly complex reproduction of what is already conceivable. Novel solutions devised within a rigidly predefined conception of a problem do not necessarily constitute an expansion of the process of becoming.

I have termed the process that enables an alternative developmental trajectory “sapiopoiesis.” This concept does not refer to the construction of an ideal human type—much less a creation ex nihilo—nor should it be conflated entirely with the rhetoric of “self-actualization,” which, while appealing, remains ambiguous. The cybernetic distinction between autopoiesis and allopoiesis helps to capture the crux of the challenge: whereas an [autopoietic organization sustains a living system through its own internal operations,](https://iai.tv/video/eva-jablonka-evolution-beyond-genes) allopoietic arrangements produce something that exists outside of themselves. Civilization has created an extraordinary allopoietic environment for human life—comprising institutions, production systems, tools, technical processes, and symbolic machines. These structures preserve and shape the conditions under which living subjects can survive. Yet, neither continued viability nor the successful production of external goods explains how a human subject might become capable of generating its own orientation—without the pretense that it could somehow escape its embodiment, its history, or its intersubjective dependencies. Sapiopoiesis is an attempt to articulate the conditions of this “becoming,” thereby distinguishing the mere maintenance of a viable existence from the emergence of a more autonomous developmental path within that very existence.

In this context, the distinction between a “lesser degree of conditionedness” and “complete unconditionedness” is significant. The latter is merely a wishful fantasy; the former, by contrast, represents a sophisticated and corrigible possibility. It would be nonsensical to claim that a subject must possess fewer causes or relationships—as if impoverishing one’s world could somehow guarantee freedom. The crucial question is whether the very conditions that enable a subject to have experiences can also be prevented from gaining definitive interpretive authority over what those experiences are permitted to mean. A relationship may open up a possibility, but it must not arrogate to itself the right to co-opt the source from which that possibility derives its significance in the first place. A technical instrument may expand a subject’s sphere of action: yet, it must not tacitly appropriate the criteria by which such an expansion is judged to be valuable.

Artificial intelligence has rendered this problem historically urgent in an unexpected way. Most public debates continue to compare human and machine performance: Who has the larger memory? Who makes decisions faster? Which professions will remain secure? How long will it be before an artificial system can simulate this or that human capability? While such comparisons are not without practical interest, they presuppose the very traditional catalog of human functions that ought, in fact, to be subjected to critical examination.

For a very long time, a significant portion of the lives of educated people has been spent maintaining symbolic instruments and the structures that depend upon them. This includes the paperwork required to validate other paperwork; procedures for monitoring procedures; the constant shifting of attention from one interface to another; the management of accounts, files, certificates, appointments, and institutional correspondence—in short, the labor of making one’s own existence intelligible to technical systems that were ostensibly introduced to make life easier. [A civilization labels much of this activity “work.”](https://iai.tv/video/the-fight-for-the-ai-economy-zoltan-istvan-aaron-bastani) Its results are real. Nevertheless, it is striking to what extent it lays claim to limited human attention.

The possibility of shifting large portions of such symbolic execution into an increasingly autonomous technical infrastructure should not be confused with the supposed replacement of the human being as a thinking entity. Viewed correctly, this could liberate attention from an antiquated framework in which human intelligence has been occupied with overseeing its own tools. This is one aspect of the “infosomatic transformation” I have explored in earlier work. Yet, I would hesitate to label such a liberation as “emancipation” without first asking what becomes of the newly gained time—and, more precisely, who determines the purposes that are to fill this time. [If every gain in efficiency is immediately converted into new demands for optimization, faster responses, or more comprehensive administrative conformity](https://iai.tv/articles/a-coming-age-of-leisure-is-a-dangerous-myth-auid-3629), then the technical achievement merely deepens the very pressures to adapt whose historical burden it might otherwise have alleviated. [Automation can uphold a civilization’s established criteria with unprecedented efficiency](https://iai.tv/articles/we-need-a-new-american-dream-for-the-age-of-ai-auid-3407): under different conditions, however, it could just as easily render some of those criteria superfluous.

The question of design, therefore, arises even before the question of the outcome. We are repeatedly assured that humans remain “in the loop”; yet, a position at the end of an operational chain is not necessarily a vantage point from which the fundamental distinctions governing that chain can be re-evaluated. An outcome, ordered according to specific criteria, dictates what is perceived; a predictive category establishes what may be taken for granted; and a convenient metric of success is treated as if its legitimacy were derived solely from the ease with which it can be measured. At the end of the process, a decision is presented to the human—and should they make it, they are congratulated for having retained control. But control over which part of the process? While a human hand may grant the final authorization, the permissible scope for potential decisions has long since been established elsewhere.

This issue should not be addressed by romanticizing inefficiency or insisting that humans perform tasks manually—tasks that machines can execute more reliably. Such an insistence would merely reinstate the old burden under the guise of autonomy. A more compelling possibility lies in an infrastructure that fosters autonomy: technical systems capable of handling provision, coordination, maintenance, and adaptation without the need for constant human oversight—yet designed in such a way that their underlying criteria remain subject to revision by embodied agents who bear responsibility for the consequences of their actions.

\_\_\_

The human being has not yet “arrived,” for “the human being” is not a term denoting a completed reality awaiting further technical refinement.

\_\_\_

The crucial distinction here lies between execution and orientation, and between delegating processes and relinquishing one’s own agency. A system can be technically autonomous while still expanding human autonomy. Conversely, a system might follow every instruction to the letter while nonetheless deepening dependency—for instance, when the person issuing the instruction was never in a position to determine the very purposes that the instruction serves. Obedience is no guarantee of freedom.

This requirement goes beyond the familiar repertoire of AI ethics. While transparency can render a process verifiable, it does not necessarily call into question the underlying distinctions. Accountability can clarify who is to be held responsible for an outcome, yet it fails to restore the capacity to interrogate why that outcome was pursued in the first place. Human oversight can remain purely ceremonial if the overseeing entity is presented with both a pre-established set of potential actions and the vocabulary used to judge their appropriateness. None of these measures is superfluous; however, they arrive too late if the determination of what counts as relevant has already been surrendered. Preserving the integrity of the origin must therefore be embedded within the architecture of technical mediation—it cannot simply be appended as a human “signature” after the architecture has already decided what matters.

[The consolations offered by transhumanism and posthumanism](https://iai.tv/video/the-worlds-most-dangerous-idea) are, to my mind, far from satisfying, as they sidestep this very issue. An “enhanced” organism may well remain a highly efficient vehicle for traditional, inherited purposes. The dissolution of the human into distributed processes—however fruitful this may be as a critique of simplistic individualism—can obscure the fact that consequences always fall back upon an embodied subject.

The subject of “sapiopoiesis” is neither an isolated, sovereign individual nor merely a decorative node within a larger network. It constitutes itself through relationships; this process of becoming cannot be transferred to a collective surrogate without putting the essential at stake. An intersubjective world gains richness through subjects capable of genuine orientation—not through a perfect alignment of their reactions. A shared life by no means requires the elimination of that very difference from which responsibility can first emerge.

If this issue were acknowledged, education would take a different form. While it would continue to impart knowledge and skills, it could no longer regard successful adaptation to established professional or institutional expectations as the ultimate culmination of the educational process.

Similarly, the existence of public institutions could no longer be justified solely by the fact that their traditional procedures happen to work. Rather, their legitimacy would have to encompass the capacity to continually reconsider the very conditions upon which that legitimacy rests. This is not a call for permanent disruption—which often serves as merely another marketable form of adaptation. It is, instead, a far more demanding proposition: that the means of coordination must always remain provisional—relative to the human process of becoming that they are intended to facilitate.

When confronted with an argument such as this, one might be tempted to ask for a definitive image of the human being who is supposedly meant to emerge. Yet, to do so would be to miss the point entirely. The human being has not yet “arrived,” for “the human being” is not a term denoting a completed reality awaiting further technical refinement. To posit such a finished form would be to grant a contemporary conception the very power to control the potential—a power that ought, in fact, to be withheld from it.

The dignity secured by humanism presupposes no such completion: it belongs to the human being irrespective of any developmental attainment. What still needs to be achieved are conditions under which the protected individual does not have to spend their life reproducing—in increasingly sophisticated ways—the very order that made such protection necessary in the first place.

A civilization may recognize every person and still fail to enable a subject. It may enlarge the machinery of choice while contracting the capacity to originate criteria. It may free attention by technical means only to make the liberated attention available for another cycle of adaptation. None of these contradictions will be resolved by announcing either the superiority of humanity or [the triumph of artificial intelligence](https://iai.tv/video/ai-and-the-mysteries-of-reality). They concern the unfinished organization of human possibility. The relevant measure of progress is no longer exhausted by what a civilization can do for its inhabitants, but must also concern what it ceases to prevent them from becoming.

Dignity forbids the instrumental disposal of a person. Integrity preserves the continuity of the becoming subject, while sourcehood concerns its capacity to originate and revise the criteria of orientation. Sapiopoiesis names the conditions that make such a trajectory increasingly viable. If there is a civilizational threshold before us, it is not the arrival of a better machine or a perfected human. It is the recognition that no achieved form of human life, however protectable, useful or technologically accomplished, is entitled to close the possibility of becoming.
