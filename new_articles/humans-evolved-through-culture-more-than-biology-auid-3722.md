# Humans evolved through culture more than biology

*How homo sapiens came to dominate*

**By Jonathan Loh** | *9th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 14 mins* | *2,870 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Humans evolved through culture more than biology](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/humans-evolved-through-culture-more-than-biology2.webp)

---

*Our biology is central to our survival. This is the evolutionary story and one we take to apply to all living beings equally. However, biologist, anthropologist, and UN and WWF consultant, **Jonathan Loh**, argues that humans are special in a fundamental way. In this article, he proposes the radical new idea that the transmission of cultural characteristics is more important for human survival than biological ones. We pass down our cultural traits using language, which allows us to transcend our ancestry and reach beyond our tribes.*

**The peculiar primate**

By any measure, *Homo sapiens* is an exceptional species. The world’s 8.3 billion humans collectively weigh around 400 million tonnes, while all wild mammals combined make up only about 60 million tonnes. And if we add domesticated animals to our side of the scales, we plus our domesticates outweigh wild mammals by a factor of 17.

But, millions of years ago, hominins were very rare. We know this from the number of hominin fossils that have been found compared with fossils of other mammal species of the same age. What was it in our evolutionary history that led to the vast disparity in population and biomass between us and other animals today? Is it just that we’re smarter—did our large brain give us global dominance? Not entirely—I’d argue that intelligence wasn’t so much the cause as a side effect.

Something quite extraordinary happened early in the evolutionary history of humans. Our ancestors 3 million years ago were apes, not much bigger than chimpanzees or bonobos—our closest living relatives—and at least as intelligent. They almost certainly used tools, just as modern chimps do. But chimps and bonobos are forest dwellers, subsisting mainly on a diet of fruit. Our ancestors lived at a time when the world was cooling and the African rainforests were shrinking. They survived by scratching out a living on the woody savannah surrounding the forest, foraging for edible plants and occasionally scavenging some meat. They spent less time climbing and more time walking—on two legs rather than four.

**The human superpower**

And then, some time between 3 and 2 million years ago, the earliest humans—hominins of the genus *Homo*—underwent the most significant evolutionary transition of any species since the origin of life on Earth. Up until that point, evolution had produced species that were adapted to survive and reproduce in their environment through the action of natural selection. Natural selection operates on variations between individuals within a population that can be inherited by genetic (and epigenetic) mechanisms—the fittest pass on to their offspring those traits that helped them survive and reproduce successfully; those that don’t survive, don’t. And evolution continues to operate that way in the natural world.

But among early humans, their way of adapting to survive and reproduce in the dangerous and hostile environment of the African savannah began to shift from biology to culture. What do I mean by this?

\_\_\_

As people ventured north from the tropics to cooler temperate regions, they didn’t re-evolve the fur they had earlier lost; they made clothes.

\_\_\_

Culture isn’t just art, music and literature; it’s much more than that. Ways of dressing, cooking, speaking, religious beliefs, games, ceremonies, and customs are all elements of culture. Cultures are made up of traditions. These include any kind of behavior, practice, technology, knowledge or belief that’s not innate, but learned from others. They’re transmitted socially, not genetically. Some socially learnt behaviors are copied for a while, then forgotten; but others persist. A set of traditions transmitted from generation to generation within a social group constitutes a culture.

Early humans survived by hunting and gathering in highly cooperative groups. Crucial survival skills were transmitted culturally: how to make stone tools for slicing, chopping, digging, or pounding; how to track and hunt antelope that could easily outrun a human; how to control fire; how to make baskets to carry food, or slings for infants; use gourds to carry water; identify and prepare medicinal plants; and, most importantly, how to work as a group with a division of labor—including between generations and probably genders too—and share the products of their labor in order to survive *as a group*. Indeed, ancient *Homo* began to compete with the big cats and other carnivores as top predators—an ecological position for which they were ill-equipped genetically.

As hunter-gatherer populations spread across Africa, and then from Africa to Eurasia, Australasia and eventually the Americas, they had to adapt to a wide variety of environments with entirely different climates, habitat types and unfamiliar plant and animal species. And they did so culturally. As people ventured north from the tropics to cooler temperate regions, they didn’t re-evolve the fur they had earlier lost; they made clothes. As they encountered new animal species, they developed new hunting techniques. Modern indigenous peoples who still subsist, at least partially, by hunting and gathering are superbly adapted to survive in their local ecologies: Bajau free divers harpoon fish on the coral reefs of Southeast Asia; Huaorani hunters track down and shoot monkeys with blow pipes in the Amazon rainforest; Inuit people hunt seals on the Arctic sea ice. And yet, while humans are extraordinarily diverse culturally, we are virtually identical genetically.

**Life as information**

So, here is an interesting parallel between culture and biology. One definition of life is *information that copies itself*. It propagates itself through time. More specifically, [life is information about how to build bodies that will copy the information](https://iai.tv/video/biology-beyond-genes-denis-noble). Biological bodies result from the expression of genetic (and epigenetic) information; if the body survives long enough to reproduce, that information is passed on to the next generation.

Cultures are the expression of a completely different kind of information—information that is stored in brains and transmitted to other brains through social learning, not via egg or sperm or cell division. Culture is a property of groups, not individuals. A cultural group is a body built by the expression of cultural information, and that group perpetuates that information beyond the lifetimes of its individual members.

Human culture doesn’t exist independently of human brains, of course, which are biological organs. But brains are simply the medium in which cultural information propagates itself, multiplies and diversifies, just as biological information propagates itself in a medium of organic molecules—DNA, RNA and proteins. Culture exists as a living entity in a layer sitting on top of biological life.

**The ratchet effect**

It used to be believed that culture was a uniquely human creation—it marked a clear divide between the human and animal worlds—but many other species have cultures too. Singing, for example, is a culturally transmitted behavior that some animals, especially birds, are famous for. The ability to sing is innate, but the songs are learnt from others. Ornithologists have long known that birds of the same species sing recognisably different songs in different regions; local variation gives rise to dialects.

Orcas display a diverse range of hunting techniques around the world; different populations are adapted to their particular prey species—salmon, seals, sea lions, stingrays and other species of dolphin and whale. Young orcas learn the techniques from older females, and individual populations are very conservative.

Chimps have perhaps the most complex cultures of all. Some populations use rocks like a hammer and anvil to crack open nuts, but others don’t—even where the same rocks and nuts are available to them. Some populations strip leaves from sticks which they use to probe termite or ant nests and fish out their inhabitants. Others use moss as a sponge with which to drink water.

\_\_\_

Humans began to comprehend *reasons why* some tools or techniques worked better than others. Innovations weren’t random but *directed* towards a particular end.

\_\_\_

Ancient hominins must have had cultures at least as complex as chimps, but it’s argued that, yes, although complex culture is seen in some animals, only humans have *cumulative* culture.

Cumulative cultural evolution, otherwise known as the ratchet effect, is the gradual accumulation of modifications to knowledge, practices or traditions that result in behaviors that no single individual could innovate in one lifetime. Of course, evolution by definition is cumulative. We don’t talk about cumulative genetic evolution because, without an accumulation of incremental changes, it wouldn’t be evolution at all. Evolution has to be cumulative. But there’s some debate as to whether any non-human species shows cumulative culture. It can be argued that some do, but the ratchet effect has had an immeasurably greater impact on human culture than it has for any other species.

**Is cumulative cultural evolution Darwinian?**

Cumulative cultural evolution shares some similarities with biological evolution, but there are also big differences. So, if culture evolves, to what extent is cultural evolution Darwinian? We need to look closer at the mechanisms behind Darwinian evolution in biological organisms and cumulative cultural evolution in humans.

[Evolution by natural selection](https://iai.tv/video/the-blueprint-for-life)—Darwin’s dangerous idea, as Daniel Dennett called it—works like an algorithm. If a population of organisms follows three simple steps, then they will inevitably evolve over time to become better adapted to survive and reproduce in their physical or biotic environment.

The three steps are:

1. Differences arise between individuals;  
2. As a result of these differences, some individuals are more successful at surviving and reproducing than others (in the language of evolutionary biology, they have higher fitness);  
3. Offspring inherit those differences from their parents.

Rinse and repeat. Another way to put it is that variation, differential fitness and heritability are three necessary and sufficient conditions for evolution by natural selection.

Now, Darwin didn’t know anything about genetics; he just knew that offspring inherit their parents’ characteristics, and that somehow new variants (he called them “sports”) arise in a population. But that’s true of culture too. So, shouldn’t the algorithm work just as well with cultural as with genetic information?

Cumulative cultural evolution also follows a similar kind of algorithm, but the concepts of variation, differential fitness and inheritance are not the same in culture as in biology, and so [natural selection doesn’t work in the same way](https://iai.tv/video/the-gene-machine).

**Cultural variation and inheritance**

The source of genetic variation within a population is random copying errors—mutations. Most mutations are mistakes—natural selection gets rid of them—but a few turn out to be beneficial. In cumulative cultural evolution, new variants arise when an individual tries something a bit different from the usual way of doing things—through individual innovation as opposed to social learning. Social learning doesn’t require comprehension. You don’t need to know why it’s safe to eat this plant but not that one, or why you should make a spear this way, or a basket that way; you just need to be able to learn and copy accurately. But humans began to comprehend *reasons why* some tools or techniques worked better than others. Innovations weren’t random but *directed* towards a particular end. So, in culture, new variants arise as intentional responses to particular challenges.

What about inheritance in culture? Genetic inheritance is vertical, from parent to offspring, but cultural inheritance isn’t, necessarily. Children acquire many socially learnt behaviors from their parents, in which case cultural and genetic information flow in parallel, but cultural traits can also pass in other directions. Social learning transmits cultural information from teacher to student, mentor to apprentice, or horizontally from sibling to sibling or peer to peer. Crucially, the transmitter and receiver of cultural information needn’t be genetically related. So cultural transmission is both vertical within families and horizontal between non-relatives.

\_\_\_

Cultural evolution works in an altogether different way, where innovations don’t come in small steps, but giant leaps.

\_\_\_

Furthermore, whereas we inherit genes as a package from our parents and are stuck with them for life, we acquire cultural information throughout our lifetimes. In which case, cultural evolution is [partially Lamarckian](https://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-evolution). Lamarck was a French naturalist who proposed a theory of evolution that incorporated an idea known as the inheritance of acquired characteristics. The giraffe, he said, evolved a long neck because generations of giraffes had to stretch their necks to reach leaves on the highest branches. Lamarckism was dismissed because animals generally don’t inherit characteristics acquired by their parents. But the ratchet effect of cumulative cultural evolution does operate like that: innovative ideas or behaviors acquired within an individual’s lifetime can be passed on by social learning.

Lastly, while genes are passed on only every 20-30 years in humans, cultural generation times can be very short. If you taught me how to make pancakes, tie a bowline or play a simple card game, I could pass this information quite accurately on to a third person after practicing just a few times. More complex behaviors like making stone tools or playing the piano take longer to learn, but still have shorter generation times than human genes.

These differences between culture and biology in variation and inheritance—directed innovation, horizontal transmission, lifelong learning and the rapidity with which cultural information can pass from brain to brain—mean that our cultures can and do evolve far more rapidly than our biology.

**It’s the song, not the singer**

What’s the equivalent of differential fitness in cultural evolution? In some cases, a new innovation in the behavior or knowledge of an individual—such as a better way of making a tool or the discovery of a new food source—might help them to survive and produce more offspring. In which case, selection is acting on differences in fitness, as it does in nature. But, thanks to horizontal transmission, what’s important for cultural information is not the reproductive success of the human expressing it, but how successfully an item of cultural information *copies itself*.

A song, for instance, becomes popular because it has a good tune and memorable words, not because the singer has more offspring. This means that cultural evolution can be completely untethered from biology. Some religious orders, for example, require their practitioners to remain celibate. This is disastrous for the reproductive success of the monk or nun in question, but as long as they pass on the order’s traditions to their novices, the cultural information will persist and continue to evolve. In cultural evolution, therefore, we have to distinguish between the fitness of a cultural trait—the song—and the fitness of its bearer—the singer—which isn’t a distinction we normally need to make with genetic traits.

**Mergers and acquisitions**

Clearly, cultural evolution isn’t really Darwinian, because of the differences in variation, heritability and differential fitness. But there’s another big difference. Darwinian (and Lamarckian) evolution works through the gradual accumulation of small changes, and the ratchet effect works that way too—small improvements lead to big improvements. But cultural evolution works in an altogether different way as well, where innovations don’t come in small steps, but giant leaps.

The economist Brian Arthur pointed out that new technologies evolve through the merging of existing technologies. The steam ship, for example, didn’t evolve by a gradual accumulation of small innovations from the sailing ship, but by the merger of the sailing ship and the steam engine, which had an entirely different evolutionary history of its own, powering machines on land.

This has always been true of cultural innovation. A wooden spear for throwing, for instance, merged with a sharp-edged stone tool for cutting to become a stone-tipped spear, a far more formidable weapon. The means of attaching them might be some kind of binding made from plant fibers. All three components evolved independently, by the ratchet effect, but merged together to create a novel cultural item. The making of stone-pointed spears then became a new cultural behavior in its own right.

Interestingly, mergers like these also occur in biology, [through symbiosis](https://iai.tv/articles/the-end-of-being-human-auid-2536). There are many examples of organisms belonging to different species that live together to survive, and in some cases the association is so close that the two become physically inseparable. Coral reefs, for example, are built by polyps—an animal—that have symbiotic algae living inside their cells. In this way, the polyp can supplement whatever food it puts in its mouth with photosynthesis.

The microbiologist Lynn Margulis called such entities holobionts, and suggested that biological evolution occurs in exactly the way that Brian Arthur described for technology. She called this mechanism symbiogenesis; I’d prefer to call it Margulisian (or Arthurian) evolution, to distinguish it from Darwinian evolution or the ratchet effect, and it is undoubtedly important in the evolution of culture. All cultural items evolve through a combination of gradual innovations and occasional mergers.

**The cultural brain**

We were able to make and use complex tools, shelters, weapons, boats, houses, cities, and eventually steam ships and spacecraft, not because we were clever enough to figure them out for ourselves, but because we were able to learn from others and make use of the vast deposits of information already accumulated in the brains of our fellow members of the population, past and present.

The biggest boost to cultural evolution came with language. Language isn’t just an important component of culture—a very particular kind of knowledge and behavior transmitted by social learning—but also the [most powerful *means*](https://iai.tv/video/eva-jablonka-evolution-beyond-genes)of transmitting cultural information. As more and more skills, technologies and knowledge became available as cultural information that could be transmitted via language, so natural selection began to operate on our brains to make us better learners and linguists. Over the course of two million years, our brains doubled in size. But it wasn’t superior intelligence that led to the global dominance of humans, and ultimately the Anthropocene age of *Homo sapiens*; it was the culture that our brains were able to acquire by social learning. As the anthropologist Joseph Henrich put it: *culture made us smarter.*
