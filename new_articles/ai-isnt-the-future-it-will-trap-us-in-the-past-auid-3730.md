# AI isn't the future, it will trap us in the past

*The real danger of AI*

**By Carl Öhman** | *18th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 8 mins* | *1,608 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![AI isn't the future, it will trap us in the past](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/article-template-copy9.webp)

---

*AI seems to be hurtling us into a future that is, depending on your view, terrifying or thrilling. But for political scientist and big-data theorist Carl Öhman, AI is something else: the tyranny of the past over the present. By scraping our digital history and feeding it back to us, AI threatens to lock us into the demands of the past, leaving us unable to begin anew. Even if it achieves genuine innovation, Öhman argues, this will be an innovation controlled by the logic of the past. To resist, we must embrace the unpredictability of the present and the future—realms outside AI’s reach.*

For both its critics and cheerleaders, AI is a harbinger of something new, a force that catapults us into the future with unprecedented speed. I say the opposite: AI, shiny as it may seem, is but the personified agency of the past reaching out into the present. The threat it poses is less an encroaching tyranny of the machine over humanity than a tyranny of the *past* over the present, a necrocratic order by which the dead reign over the living.

To see why this is, we only need to look at the raw material from which AI is built: data—the “new oil” of the world economy. While most commonly used to illustrate an economic shift, the metaphor also holds a deeper truth: that data is essentially our compressed past presented as a single substance. As the name “fossil fuel” indicates, real oil is composed of the remains of marine microorganisms—our long-dead ancestors—that, over millennia, accumulate on the seafloor.

Data is the fossil fuel of the digital economy, for it too is nothing but the accumulated remains of the past, the digital corpses of our former selves and ancestors, if you will. Whether in the form of social media posts, a company’s quarterly sales, or the records of an electric meter, data are the traces—not only a symbol, representation, or reflection—of some prior event. Our daily search history, scrolling, texting, browsing, and socializing are microorganisms that fall to the internet’s seafloor, where they will eventually be extracted to fuel the engines of prediction that propel the digital economy.

\_\_\_

The challenge posed by AI is not so much to save humanity from the machines—the machines are us—but to save the present from the combined force of our past.

\_\_\_

Just like oil, data are the fossilized past, compressed into a single substance. They are literally the *corpus* upon which AI systems are trained. So, when we say that [AI emerges from data](https://iai.tv/articles/information-and-data-will-never-deliver-creativity-auid-3132), what we are really saying is that it emerges from the past; just as a pancake is composed of milk, flour and eggs, the agency of AI is essentially composed of corpora scraped from the web—it is those digital remains, presented in a new format. Victor Frankenstein built his monster from a composite of human corpses; we build ours from the digital corpses that have accumulated on the internet’s seafloor.

When thus construed, the challenge posed by AI is not so much to save humanity from the machines—the machines *are* us—but to save the present from the combined force of our past.

What does “saving the present” mean in practice? It could mean that [the past is imbued with oppressive structures and biases](https://iai.tv/video/the-accurate-the-perfect-and-the-dangerous) that we do not want to bring into the present. That type of discussion has dominated AI ethics for at least a decade. And yet, a tyranny of the past would remain dangerous even where it produced indisputably superior outcomes for our relative welfare and equality—even if it were completely unbiased, fair, transparent, and aligned with “human values.” Because the dominance of the past, even if perfect, would stall our capacity to *begin*.

Beginning is a tricky concept. Colloquially, we associate it with innovation. But cannot AI also generate new things? Is this not the entire point of generative AI? In contrast to many of my colleagues, I am actually rather optimistic about AI becoming genuinely innovative. True, in its current form, it is always a product of what has been, but that, in and of itself, does not prevent it from extrapolating in innovative ways that we ourselves could not foresee. Pick up any newspaper, and there will be plenty of examples, from predicting complex protein structures to solving mathematical theorems. One day, AI might even come to produce genuinely interesting art and music. Yet, innovation, [even creativity](https://iai.tv/video/ai-and-the-creativity-crisis), is not *beginning*.

To see the difference, crucial to our quest to save the present, we may consult the philosophy of Hannah Arendt, whose insight into the art of beginning remains unmatched.

In an evocative parable, [Franz Kafka](https://iai.tv/video/how-to-see-the-world-through-kafkas-eyes-steven-berkoff) once described the condition of man as squeezed between two opposing forces. The first is the past pressing him forward. The second is the future, pressing him back into the past. The past presses forward because it is full of imperatives, obligations, and commitments to be resolved in the future. The future presses backward because its demands seem to point us back into the past as a source of precedents and solutions, which, once resolved, immediately become part of the past.

Invoking this parable, Arendt notes that, for most of recorded history, *tradition* has served as a bridge between the two, a rail that makes our predicament endurable. Yet, in the modern age, where tradition has lost its once indisputable authority, such a comfort is no longer at hand. How can we persevere without being crushed? How can we maintain [a space between the forces of time](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-breaks-from-the-physical-world-by-keeping-the-past-alive-auid-3447)? Though Arendt’s work comprises an abundance of concepts to address these questions, the most useful here is *action*—our “capacity to *begin*,” as she famously phrased it.

*Action*, for Arendt, must be understood against the backdrop of two other modes of human activity. The first is our ability to sustain the biological preconditions of life, including all forms of reproduction (what Arendt calls *labor*). The second is our bringing of new things into the world—the innovative ability to turn objects as given to us into future works of art or tools (what she calls *work*). Although both labor and work are means of altering the world, they do not open a space *between* past and future. They are merely transmitters through which one becomes the other. As anyone in the tech sector can testify, even the most disruptive instances of innovation can be completely thoughtless, mere imperative functions of the past that never occupy any space in the present.

In contrast to labor and work, *action* is not just a clever unfolding of imperatives. On the contrary, because action is immediately integrated into a dynamic web of reciprocal human relations, its outcome cannot, even in theory, be predicted or controlled. To act, therefore, is like bringing a child, an entirely new person, into the world, a force which can never fully be understood. Frightening. But more frightening still—that person is *you*! When we act, we do not fully understand what it is we are doing, what forces we set in motion, and, most crucially, who we become in so doing. To act, therefore, is to begin in the truest, fullest sense of the word.

Our ability to begin, in this sense, is more than the mere creation of new things. [Innovation](https://iai.tv/video/the-great-innovation-race), despite its marvelous speed and constant disruptions, is but the clever unfolding of things past. To begin, by contrast, is [to open up a space between the encroaching forces of time](https://iai.tv/articles/the-flow-of-time-is-ours-to-disrupt-auid-3590). It is not to disregard them, nor is it an embrace of irrationality (e.g., by refuting experience). It requires that we act with the *totality of our being* while at the same time putting that very being at risk. As such, beginning is a *spiritual* faculty—it involves our *faith*. Rather than being confined to the rational faculties of the mind, a true act of beginning transcends them. It goes beyond rationality, beyond the predictive power of the past, however mighty, and embraces the *present*.

In my upcoming book, *Gods of Data: An Atheist Critique of AI*, I describe a variety of everyday practices in which our capacity to begin comes to the fore—parenting, romance, political deliberation, etc.—which are increasingly colonized by AI. None of these activities would be possible were they reduced solely to rational calculation, i.e., if we outsourced them to the past. Yet each is a realm where AI has us mistaking *making* for *beginning*, where our wonder at innovation prevents us from standing in the present, from truly beginning.

As useful as it can be, AI fools us into thinking that we can “hack” life by relying on the firmness of the personified past and its predictive power. The result may be satisfying. AI helps us identify jobs, partners, parenting styles, political discourse, etc. that better meet our goals and desires. But it does so only at the cost of spiritually corrupting these activities, by turning us into innovative machines that bridge the gap between present and past without ever truly occupying it. In short, AI helps us create better lives, but when its logic is carried to its extreme, they may no longer be *our lives*.

The [doomers’ fear](https://iai.tv/articles/longtermism-about-ai-is-dangerous-auid-2886) that AI may one day decide to kill us all is warranted, not in the literal sense of ending our biological existence, but in the spiritual sense that we become an undead version of ourselves—subjects *to* our history, rather than the subjects *of* it.
