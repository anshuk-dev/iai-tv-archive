# The world is not made of one thing, but two

*Mind is irreducible to matter*

**By William Hasker** | *20th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 17 mins* | *3,459 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![The world is not made of one thing, but two](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/world-two-things.webp)

*Dividing mind from matter has dominated Western thought since Descartes. But more recently the idea that everything is fundamentally physcial has taken hold. Here, philosopher **William Hasker**, in conversation with social scientist **Tony Sobrado**, explores the failures of physicalism and argues that mind is irreducible to matter.*

**Tony Sobrado:** Perhaps the most important question in philosophy of mind, and even in neuroscience and cognitive science, is whether consciousness can be fully explained solely in physical terms and thus by the physical brain alone. Physicalism is loosely the idea that everything that exists and can ever possibly exist in the universe, and thus also consciousness, is ultimately physical. Physicalism, albeit notoriously difficult to define, is a philosophical position that permeates the contemporary world and is implicitly assumed across both the social and natural sciences, and even often in philosophy of mind. Long gone are the days of Descartes’ substance dualism; today, few philosophers are even property dualists. Yet our mental goings-on, our inner world, and our subjective experience still pose significant challenges across many fields of study.

When we talk of reductionism and reducing consciousness to the physical brain, there are multifaceted forms of reduction, including theoretical reduction, explanatory reduction, conceptual reduction and ontological reduction. As a pre-eminent philosopher and theologian who endorses dualism, why do you think consciousness cannot be reduced to, and thus completely explained by, the physical brain?

\_\_\_

Consciousness is not merely another physical property among all those other physical properties. Consciousness is experience itself.

\_\_\_

**William Hasker:** The starting point here is what David Chalmers famously called the hard problem of consciousness. We have descriptions of brain processes, neural activity, information processing, and so forth (the easy problem), but nobody has a genuine explanation of how consciousness itself arises from those physical brain processes (the hard problem). What strikes me is that there is simply no clear place to begin. We know what physical explanations look like because physics describes relations, structures, forces, and causal processes of physical properties only. Yet consciousness is not merely another physical property among all those other physical properties. Consciousness is experience itself. That is why I have always found reductionist accounts unsatisfactory.

It is not that they fail to answer a particular technical question, but rather they fail to provide any convincing account of why subjective experience should emerge from physical processes in the first place. A physical law cannot be conscious, and physical properties and physical laws say nothing about conscious experience, so it is difficult to see how physicalism can completely account for conscious experience.

**Tony Sobrado:** The issue regarding physical laws, specifically, and conscious experience is paramount. The intrinsically objective independent nature of physical laws cannot be accompanied by any mental or subjective features. My essay here at IAI News, titled [*Panpsychism does not solve the hard problem of consciousness*](http://iai.tv/articles/panpsychism-does-not-solve-the-hard-problem-of-consciousness-auid-3630)*,* argues how any mental features capable of non-linear and non-deterministic properties cannot be coupled with physical laws because it would fail to guarantee the objectivity of physical laws in the first place. This is because subjective experience is an open-ended domain, failing to possess uniformly determined parameters by virtue of being an experience that is subjective and individualised, as opposed to being monolithic and invariable like physical laws. This objectivity problem of panpsychism illustrates the general problem faced when attempting to marry objective physical laws and subjective experience [that result from basic physicalism](http://iai.tv/video/science-beyond-the-physical).

As to your specific contention here regarding the challenge of how physicalism can completely account for conscious experience, this is fundamental and the million-dollar question. Even if neuroscience one day maps every single neural event correlated with mental phenomena, there still appears to be a conceptual distance between the physical description of the process and [the experience that is felt itself](http://iai.tv/articles/annaka-harris-consciousness-is-fundamental-auid-3136). We may know that a particular neural pattern occurs when someone experiences the colour red, but that does not explain why that neural pattern should be accompanied by a subjective, sentient experience. Some may argue that the gap is only conceptual and not ontological, and we will discuss explanatory gaps shortly. Regardless, many neuroscientists themselves call for general explanatory mechanisms, theoretical roles or bridging principles; so it seems there is something missing when it comes to neural explanations of consciousness.

**William Hasker:** Exactly! Physical descriptions tell us what is happening objectively. Conscious experience concerns what things are like subjectively and the essence of experience. These appear to be fundamentally different kinds of phenomena. The challenge for physicalism is not simply to describe [correlations between brain states and experiences](http://iai.tv/video/imagination-and-the-real). The challenge is to explain why those correlations exist at all, and I do not think any existing physicalist theory has successfully accomplished that.

# **The challenge of intentionality and rational thought**

**Tony Sobrado:** Much of the debate surrounding consciousness focuses on qualia and subjective experience, and how these relate to fundamental physical properties—as just discussed. Your criticisms of physicalism’s inability to capture the domain of consciousness are more nuanced and expansive. Your understanding of the conscious arena and the problems this poses to physical explanation delves into intentionality and rational thought that our sentient minds also possess. What is it about the mental phenomena of intentionality and rational thought that also renders a purely physicalist account of mind particularly problematic from your perspective?

**William Hasker:** Explaining consciousness solely in physical terms is problematic because physical explanations are fundamentally mechanistic, as we just discussed, and so again physical laws describe events in terms of causal interactions. They do not, however, refer to purposes, goals, meanings, or intentions. Intentionality and rational thought are not mechanistic in the way that physical laws are. To borrow an example that my friend Victor Reppert often uses, rocks in an avalanche do not travel where they travel because it is a good idea for them to go there. They move according to physical laws only. The explanation is entirely mechanistic and physical—there is no intention there.

Human reasoning is different. When I am solving a philosophical problem, my thinking is directed towards a goal. I am evaluating evidence, considering alternatives, and attempting to reach a conclusion. Purpose and intentionality are not accidental features of reasoning; they are central to what reasoning is, and this mental landscape cannot be explained by physical mechanistic laws. One of the difficulties facing physicalism is that it attempts to explain rational thought using explanatory tools that make no reference to rationality whatsoever. Physical laws contain no concept of truth, validity or meaning. Yet those concepts are essential to thought itself, and this creates a tension which many physicalists underestimate.

# **Rationality and the physical world—can evolution bridge the gap?**

**Tony Sobrado:** Whilst combining subjective experience with physical laws is an issue, finding explanatory levers for [elements of intentionality](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-drives-evolution-auid-2889) and rationality as functionalist concepts under the framework of evolutionary principles and mechanisms may in this context be possible. Even if physical laws themselves do not explain rationality and intentionality, perhaps evolutionary mechanisms do. Natural selection rewards successful behavior and decision-making, and so over time cognition becomes increasingly sophisticated. Some psychologists argue that there are psychological laws, this coupled with the desire for survival and continued existence facilities a theoretical space where intentionality and reasoned goal pursuit and attainment are grounded in evolutionary mechanisms of the mind. This is not the same issues as subjective experience being completely reduced to the physical as that is about broad consciousness; but for mental intentionality and rationality, specifically, evolutionary mechanisms can go along way here. Why might that assertion still be insufficient for someone like you?

**William Hasker:** It is unsatisfactory because it merely shifts the problem. Evolution may explain why certain behavioral patterns develop and may explain why organisms become more successful in adapting to their environments. What it does not explain, however, is how genuine intentionality emerges. At some point, we still have to account for the fact that thoughts are about things. [We still have to explain meaning, understanding and rational evaluation and their processes](http://iai.tv/video/the-not-so-blind-watchmaker). Simply saying that these capacities evolved does not tell us what they are or how they became possible.

\_\_\_

If physical processes are entirely non-rational, it is difficult to see how rationality can emerge from them without introducing rationality somewhere within the explanation itself.

\_\_\_

**Tony Sobrado:** In other words, evolutionary principles and mechanisms might account for adaptive behavior and why we have consciousness and thoughts via a functionalist framework, but for you, this adaptive cognition and behavior, which is based on selection pressures, is not necessarily the same thing as rational understanding. Physical environmental pressures drive selection fitness, behavior, and possibly even thoughts, but it does not necessitate all the facets of rational thought. Essentially, rationality also cannot escape the hard problem of consciousness when it comes to explanation.

**William Hasker:** Exactly! If physical processes are entirely non-rational, it is difficult to see how rationality can emerge from them without introducing rationality somewhere within the explanation itself. That is why I have always been sceptical of purely evolutionary solutions to the problem of consciousness.

Physical laws describe causal processes that operate without any reference to purpose. They simply govern interactions among physical entities. Reasoning, by contrast, is normative. We distinguish between good arguments and bad arguments. We distinguish valid reasoning from fallacious reasoning. Now consider what physicalism requires. It requires that our thoughts are ultimately governed by physical processes in the brain. Yet the laws governing physical processes and the principles governing rational inference are not obviously the same things. Indeed, human beings frequently reason badly, commit fallacies and reach irrational conclusions. That demonstrates there is no straightforward identity between physical causation and rational justification. The fact that physical events occur cannot by itself explain [why a particular belief should be considered rational, justified or true](http://iai.tv/articles/the-divide-between-belief-and-emotion-is-an-illusion-auid-3396), and that means physical causation alone cannot explain the normative dimension of thought.

# **The explanatory gap and identity theory**

**Tony Sobrado:** This leads to what has become one of the most famous issues in philosophy of mind and the crux of the issue that we have been discussing regarding consciousness and its complete physical reduction: explanatory gaps. Joseph Levine famously argued that even if consciousness is somehow identical with physical brain states, there remains a gap between the [experience itself](http://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-is-not-an-object-we-can-observe-auid-3608) and its physical description, as we have discussed. There is a gap, but what kind of gap is it, and how important is it? Is it merely a conceptual gap? For example, our concepts of subjective experiences are concepts that we apply to our actual experiences as humans by way of recognizing and focusing on them. However, neuroscientific concepts are distinct theoretical concepts. The chasm between these two conceptual frameworks renders virtually no a priori connections between our concepts of subjective experiences and neuroscientific concepts. In this context, there is a conceptual gap. Thinkers like Brian McLaughlin assert this conceptual gap but deny an ontological gap because two concepts with no a priori links between them can refer to the same physical state type (the brain state). Do you see this gap as merely conceptual, or do you believe it reveals something deeper?

**William Hasker:** I certainly believe it is deeper. Many physicalists argue that the gap is merely conceptual. Their claim is that consciousness and brain states are identical, but we think about them using different concepts. I find that explanation inadequate. Merely appealing to different conceptual schemes does not solve the problem; it only labels the problem. If the gap is merely conceptual, then we still need an explanation of why it appears so profound and persistently different. Philosophers sometimes invoke conceptual differences as though doing so immediately removes an ontological problem, but it does not. If there truly is no ontological distinction, then the burden remains on the physicalist to explain why consciousness and physical processes appear so radically different, and that explanation cannot merely be assumed.

**Tony Sobrado:** One thing I find interesting about identity theory is that it attempts to preserve both physicalism and consciousness simultaneously, unlike [eliminativism](http://iai.tv/articles/the-mysterious-disappearance-of-consciousness-auid-1296) and [illusionism](http://iai.tv/articles/the-demystification-of-consciousness-auid-1381), which just dispense with phenomenal consciousness altogether in an ontological sense. The latter frameworks are more controversial, but at least they are internally consistent and parsimonious, as there simply is no room for phenomenal consciousness. The philosophical problem for identity theory is that once phenomenal consciousness is acknowledged as real, further questions immediately arise due to simultaneous identity yet distinction. Although I appreciate the brilliance behind McLaughlin’s thinking, where a gap is repackaged as only conceptual, not ontological, it ultimately collapses back into the traditional hard problem of consciousness: if subjective experience is identical to a brain state, why does that brain state possess experience while other physical systems apparently do not? Why does a conscious brain feel like something from the inside, while a keyboard does not?

**William Hasker:** Yes indeed! The existence of consciousness introduces a distinction that physical descriptions alone struggle to explain. Identity theorists frequently claim that consciousness simply is the brain state, but asserting an identity is not the same as explaining it. The challenge remains to explain why conscious experience exists at all, and I do not believe physicalism has met that challenge, and neither has identity theory.

# **Dualism, physical causal closure and metaphysical assumptions**

**Tony Sobrado:** We have tackled the physicalist charges when it comes to how consciousness cannot be completely reduced to the physical, and how physical laws say nothing about experience and rationality. Furthermore, we have addressed how identity theory cannot overcome an explanatory gap by rebranding the gap as simply ontological. Yet perhaps the physicalist’s strongest charge, which is extremely difficult to overcome, is physical causal closure.

This prolific objection concerns interaction. If mind and body are distinct, how can they affect one another? Even if one is sympathetic to property dualism and states that physical brain processes can cause mental phenomena (think how stimulants, sedatives and general medication affect mental phenomena), this form of dualism may still violate physical causal closure. One can then imagine feedback loops under a monolithic manifold of a physical structure whereby a mental property can still interact with physical causal closure, but this is contentious and may still be seen to violate physical causal closure. Dualism may also violate the conservation of energy principle and produce causal overdetermination. The latter being where every mental element has two simultaneous causes: 1. A mental state and 2. A physical brain state. Avoiding causal overdetermination may entail avoiding even property dualism.

Much of the criticism regarding any form of dualism depends upon physical causal closure. The idea is that every physical event already possesses a sufficient physical cause. How do you think about physical causal closure, and how significant is it a problem for those pushing back on physical reductionist explanations of consciousness?

\_\_\_

Consciousness is neither a separate supernatural entity inserted into the physical world nor merely a by-product of neural activity. It emerges from physical systems while remaining irreducible to them.

\_\_\_

**William Hasker:** My initial response begins with experience itself. Mind-body interaction is not some exotic hypothesis. It is part of ordinary life. I decide to speak, and I speak. I decide to raise my hand, and I raise it. Mental thoughts and mental intentions appear intimately connected with physical actions. As a consequence, I regard interaction as a fact that theories must explain rather than a problem that somehow disproves dualism. I see interaction as something that is immediately given in experience rather than something inferred indirectly. [The burden is often placed entirely on dualists](http://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-the-self-and-the-body), but as we have touched upon already, physicalists also face significant difficulties explaining mental causation in purely physicalist terms. The debate is not as one-sided as it is sometimes presented.

As for physical causal closure itself specifically, I see it as an assumption and not an empirical discovery. If mind-body interaction occurs, then physical causal closure cannot be universally true, and because interaction appears to be part of everyday experience, I see no reason to elevate physical causal closure into an unquestionable metaphysical principle.

**Tony Sobrado:** This is significant because many philosophical debates begin by assuming physical causal closure in the first instance rather than defining it in terms of ontological maxims. Ultimately, everything may rest on metaphysical assumptions, and even though the strong arguments regarding the conservation of energy and over-determination are persuasive, once physical causal closure’s assumption is granted, dualism appears impossible by definition.

**William Hasker:** Precisely, but assumptions should not determine conclusions. The question is whether the assumption itself is justified. I have seen no real rationally valid reasons to assert that physical causal closure is objectively true rather than a mere metaphysical assumption.

# **Dualism: how does interaction with the physical work?**

**Tony Sobrado:** Leaving the potential metaphysical assumption of physical causal closure behind us and focusing back on general physicalism—where it is difficult to deny the physical object of the brain because granting the status of the physical to the brain is what makes complete physicalist explanations of consciousness problematic—there still appear difficulties with the ontological realm of the mental. For instance, doesn’t a dualist also have to explain exactly how interaction occurs? Regardless of physical causal closure—and now specifically focusing on ontological interactions—how can a mental property like a thought affect a physical property like a brain and its physical processes? Doesn’t one have to ultimately still get out of that bind? You are also widely known for emergent substance dualism, so tell us in your own words what this entails for metaphysics, philosophy of mind and consciousness.

**William Hasker:** Firstly, to your question: “How can a mental property like a thought affect a physical property like a brain and its physical processes?” I think that question often asks for more than any theory of causation can provide. At some point, all explanations reach a stopping point. Why do physical causes produce physical effects? Ultimately, because that is how the world works. David Hume recognised this centuries ago. For him, we observe regularities and causal relations, but our explanations eventually terminate in basic facts about reality. The same applies to mental causation. It occurs, and we can investigate its mechanisms and correlations. We can even study the relationship between thought and the brain. Eventually, however, we arrive at a foundational fact: some things cause other things.

As for my emergent substance dualism, I believe minds emerge through the development of sufficiently complex physical structures. Human beings arise through an evolutionary process, so consciousness does not appear in isolation from biology. However, once consciousness emerges, it constitutes something genuinely distinct. The mind is not reducible to physical processes even though it arises in relation to them. Consequently, consciousness possesses its own reality and causal significance. That, in broad terms, is my position.

**Tony Sobrado:** So consciousness is neither a separate supernatural entity inserted into the physical world nor merely a by-product of neural activity. It emerges from physical systems while remaining irreducible to them.

**William Hasker:** Yes, correct: that is how I see it.

# **The limits of our understanding**

**Tony Sobrado:** One of the broader themes emerging from our discussion concerns epistemological boundaries and the unknowable. I find it interesting to note how both physicalists and dualists endorse an ultimate brute fact necessity in a Platonic sense. A completely physical reductionist and identity theorist who endorses the mental but is a substance monist says no more explanation is needed. On the other hand, a property or substance dualist working against some forms of [physical causal closure](http://iai.tv/video/science-beyond-the-physical) and working with interactive dualism may assert that both physical and mental properties coexist and combine... and that is just how the world works. Moreover, can we ever fully know what reality is and how it works? One has difficulty escaping the preeminent traps of Plato, Hume and Kant.

Metaphysical states, by necessity, are both an answer and an issue that goes beyond consciousness and matter. In physics alone, why is energy interchangeable with matter? Why does entropy always increase within a completely isolated system? The answer might be because of necessity, i.e. it cannot be any other way. Likewise, why is a mental state identical to a brain state? Or, conversely, how do mental phenomena interact with physical matter in a dualist sense? The answer in these two opposing camps might be the same: necessity—it cannot be any other way!

With this being the case, do you think the very presence of consciousness points towards a deeper metaphysical picture of reality by necessity?

**William Hasker:** I do, yes! As a theist, I believe consciousness, rationality and the intelligibility of the universe ultimately fit within a broader explanatory framework. That does not mean every philosophical question is answered. Far from it. I believe, however, that theism provides the most satisfactory overarching account of why beings such as ourselves exist and why reality possesses the structure that it does.

At the same time, one must work from both directions. We need broad metaphysical explanations, but we also need detailed investigations of mind, matter and consciousness. The challenge is to ensure that the different levels of explanation fit together coherently.
