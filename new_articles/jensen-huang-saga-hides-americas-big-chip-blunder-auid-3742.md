# Jensen Huang saga hides America's big chip blunder

*Nvidia's China problem isn't going anywhere*

**By Milton L. Mueller** | *25th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 6 mins* | *1,285 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Jensen Huang saga hides America's big chip blunder](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/jensen-huang-saga-hides-americas-big-chip-blunder.webp)

*After a whirlwind 11 days, Jensen Huang is back as CEO of Nvidia. From spies, to affairs, to lavish parties… little wonder the media couldn’t look away from the saga. But the spectacle obscures a far bigger story, argues **Milton L. Mueller**, director of the Internet Governance Project. Beijing wants to make its own chips, and has spent billions on domestic production while steering its companies away from Nvidia. Urged on by China hawks and frontier AI labs seeking protection from competition, Washington is still tightening the export controls Huang fought against. By pushing Chinese firms towards local suppliers, the US is building the very rival it fears.*

It’s striking that the most important issues related to Jensen Huang-China controversy are the ones that are not being discussed. The spy allegations, affair disclosures and reports about Huang’s parties and breathless coverage of his reinstatement - all seem designed to preempt public discussion of something far more important: are semiconductor export controls a wise policy? Do they serve the global public interest?

A perceptive observer cannot help but notice how the scandal-talk takes the answers to those questions for granted. Of course export controls are vital to our national security—no need to debate that. Let’s focus instead on how Nvidia’s trade with Asian data centers undermined the controls. Surely, this could only happen because Huang was corrupted by a Chinese spy, and a Chinese Communist Party (CCP) conspiracy must be at the root of it all.

Now observe how this interpretation skews the policy dialogue. There is no room in this narrative for the possibility that export controls are bad for US technology leadership ([as Huang argued forcefully for years](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjurAWAr_nY)) and bad for consumers. No questions are asked about whether export controls are failing to achieve their objectives. The fact that the [CCP is actively discouraging](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/17/china-tells-its-tech-companies-they-cant-buy-ai-chips-from-nvidia/) its companies from buying Nvidia chips, as China promotes domestic substitutes, is ignored. There is no discussion, for example, of China’s $47 billion Big Fund III, launched in May 2024. This was the latest step in the CCP’s attempt to achieve self-sufficiency in semiconductor production, and is consistent with its resistance to the interdependence and openness long fostered by cyberspace. That Chinese commercial companies, who have been willing customers of US products, are cut off from the best semiconductors will only help the CCP achieve the self-sufficiency it wants. And, of course, there is no mention of the numerous, obvious linkages between US export controls and the lobbying of a few big American companies, who want protection from foreign competitors.

Semiconductor export controls are the tip of a very large iceberg: the $20-$30 trillion global market for digital goods and services. The digital economy accounts for 17%-22% of global GDP and is one of the fastest-growing sectors. The US has held global leadership in this sector since the 1990s, but achieved it by [promoting competition and globalized, open markets,](https://clintonwhitehouse4.archives.gov/WH/New/Commerce/read.html) not via restrictions on exports and imports.

The arcane, [extensive](https://www.bis.gov/regulations/ear/interactive-commerce-control-list?isExpanded=&category=&keyword=) lists of controlled and licensed products coming out of the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) are the product of a poorly conceived [change in US strategy](https://iai.tv/video/rethinking-the-global-orthodoxy). It combines a nationalist industrial policy like China’s, [restrictive trade policies](https://iai.tv/video/the-end-of-free-trade-and-the-new-world-economy), and a hostile foreign policy that promotes confrontation with and decoupling from China. It has been pursued by Democratic and Republican administrations for the past ten years. Although these policies are visibly backfiring, the potent combination of corporate rent-seeking and the delusions of Washington politicians that the U.S. can unliterally control the global diffusion of digital technology keeps the government doubling down on them.

Three core premises underpin this regulatory regime. They are rarely stated out loud, yet they drive nearly every decision in Washington:

• Artificial intelligence is a weapon, and [AI development is a zero-sum arms race](https://iai.tv/video/the-hype-and-limits-of-ai) whose winner will wield unchecked geopolitical power over its rivals.  
• The US can exploit its leverage over semiconductors to stunt or retard the growth of China’s entire digital sector and thus maintain its lead in this alleged arms race.  
• US leadership in digital tech will be secured by building walls around the product areas it leads, rather than by continuing to [innovate](https://iai.tv/video/the-great-innovation-race) and succeed in global markets.

Each of these premises is false.

First, AI is an information system, not a weapon. The vast majority of the markets and applications are civilian. Like any information technology, it can play a role in weapons systems and intelligence analysis, but faster chips are not a ring of power that gives its possessor military or political dominance over everyone else. (Did massive US superiority in AI/ICT defeat Iran?) US semiconductor, cloud, and software industries dominate the world and can invest vast amounts of capital in innovation—not because they are protected by export controls, but because they lead commercial applications and markets around the world.

We’ve seen this movie before. Back in the 1990s, [the US tried to control cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export_of_cryptography_from_the_United_States), which it also characterized as a weapon. A business and civil society revolt forced it to back off, and as a result the public Internet is more secure, and US companies are leaders in the markets for digital certificates and cryptographic applications.

Second, AI development is not a race. There is no finish line. As with semiconductors, networks, and computers for the past 60-70 years, AI’s capabilities will increase over time, and various firms’ market share will wax and wane along with the quality and price of their products. The “race” idea stems from the fantasy that an all-powerful “[superintelligence](https://iai.tv/articles/nick-bostrom-a-frightened-optimist-on-the-future-of-humanity-auid-1257)” will emerge to threaten humanity, so the US had better get there first. [This thesis](https://iai.tv/video/the-real-risks-of-ai-natalie-cargill) was first promoted by figures like the philosopher Nick Bostrom, and was later institutionalized by [the Effective Altruist movement](https://iai.tv/articles/effective-altruisms-hypocritical-high-priests-jonathan-anomaly-auid-2654) that influenced both OpenAI and Anthropic. Of course, it has been [forcefully challenged](https://iai.tv/video/the-hype-and-limits-of-ai) by computer scientists and philosophers. Whether or not Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei genuinely believes this [apocalyptic story](https://iai.tv/video/the-ai-apocalypse), promoting it conveniently strengthens the case for a government-protected AI oligopoly. If frontier models pose an existential threat, then their deployment must be regulated by national security agencies, creating a bottleneck that crushes competition and entrenches leading firms like Anthropic and OpenAI.

This superintelligence fantasy has also led to efforts to stifle open-source AI. Amodei and other closed-model advocates object to open-weight systems because providers cannot monitor end users or revoke access once the code is released. In July 2026 the Trump administration was weighing measures to [block or restrict foreign open-source AI models](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/20/ai-us-china-open-source-kimi). Jensen Huang led the industry coalition that successfully pushed back against those proposals. Right after Huang was discredited, officials at the White House and Treasury are [reportedly](https://shattered.io/trump-administration-weighs-chinese-ai-disclosure-airbnb-qwen-three-sources/) advancing proposals to force US companies to publicly disclose any use of Chinese open-weight models in order to stigmatize and discourage adoption. This is anti-competitive and will cost the US economy in the long run.

Third, export controls on semiconductors are harmful to US tech leadership and to global technological progress in general. On China’s side, they have only prompted [massive investment in a domestic industry that will make its substantial market off-limits to US suppliers](https://iai.tv/articles/trump-china-and-the-end-of-western-dominance-auid-3166). Worse, US attempts to leverage “chokepoints” on compute cannot work without dragging ever-expanding parts of the digital economy into the controls. First it was advanced lithography. Then it was materials and chemicals. Then China learned that it, too, could exploit chokepoints over rare-earth minerals. Now, US cloud providers are being ordered to sever connections with the world’s second-largest economy and impose intrusive know-your-customer identity checks across their networks. None of this will secure American primacy. All it can achieve is the fragmentation of the global tech ecosystem, and the undermining of the very openness that made US leadership possible in the first place.
