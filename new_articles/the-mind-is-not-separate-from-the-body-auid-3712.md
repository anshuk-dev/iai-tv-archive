# The mind is not separate from the body

*What Heidegger misses in Descartes' philosophy*

**By Tarek Dika** | *26th October 2026* | *Read time: approx. 9 mins* | *1,739 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![The mind is not separate from the body](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/the-mind-is-not-separate-from-the-body.webp)

---

*We think that substance dualism separates the mind from the body in an irreconcilable way. This is the view that has been criticised by phenomenology – most staunchly by Heidegger. However, professor of philosophy at the University of Toronto **Tarek Dika** argues that this misses a crucial part of Descartes' philosophy, namely that the mind and body are fundamentally united. Although these two substances have different properties, we fundamentally have access to their union – from the way we feel pain to the way we move our bodies, dualism is much closer to phenomenology than we initially thought.*

The standard interpretation of Heidegger’s criticism of Descartes runs as follows: Descartes, far from breaking with Aristotelian scholasticism, interprets the ego or “I” according to the very categories Aristotle employed to describe all beings: substance and property. Whether there is a change in my mind (from one thought to another) or a change in a stone (from one temperature to another), the underlying substance (the mind, the stone) abides, while the properties (the thought, the temperature) change. For Descartes, modifications in the mind are distinguishable from modifications in a stone only by the fact that they are conscious. For Heidegger, there are no modifications which I merely consciously “clock.” Descartes failed to ask whether categories like substance and property are adequate to beings such as ourselves (“Dasein,” in Heidegger’s technical terminology).

What distinguishes Dasein from other beings is that its being is an *issue* for it; it doesn’t simply undergo changes in its properties, but must decide who and how to be in each case and in every circumstance, however mundane. If a stone changes temperature, then it has simply acquired a new property. If Dasein’s temperature changes, it hasn’t simply acquired a property, but rather experiences its existence through this change and has to decide who and how to be in relation to it. For example, if it is threatened by the fire, it flees to safety. Dasein experiences its changes *existentially*, and Dasein cannot be captured by categories designed to describe other beings. Descartes, [Heidegger argues](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-6-meFnHxBTAbkLAv/page/n21/mode/2up), “is ‘dependent’ upon medieval scholasticism and employs its terminology” to such a “profound extent” that “the way in which posterity has determined or failed to determine the ontological character of the *res cogitans*” according to “the ancient way of interpreting the being of entities” has not even been seen by him.

Secondly, Descartes’ [mind-body dualism](https://iai.tv/video/the-fantasies-of-descartes-antonio-damasio) renders the “ego” or “I” worldless; it is certain of its own existence, but not of the existence of the world. This leads to what has come to be known as “external world skepticism.”

Thirdly, Descartes’ reduction of the world to bare extension (*res extensa*) rests on an uninterrogated prioritization of the theoretical experience of the world over the average, everyday way in which the world is experienced. Every other experience of the world (including every possible theoretical representation of it) presupposes the average, everyday experience of the world from which it starts and to which it must ultimately return, and one of the principal tasks of an ontology of the world is to describe the world precisely insofar as it is given in average, everyday existence: “[The problem of how to get appropriate access to entities within-the-world is one which Descartes feels no need to raise](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-6-meFnHxBTAbkLAv/page/n63/mode/2up). Under the unbroken ascendance of the traditional ontology, the way to get a genuine grasp of what really is has been decided in advance: it lies in *νοεῖν*—‘beholding’ in the widest sense; *διανοεῖν* or ‘thinking’ is just a more fully achieved form of *νοεῖν* and is founded upon it.”

Lastly, Descartes’ interpretation of the ego as self-consciousness is inadequate. For Descartes, I not only perceive the stone; I am also conscious of myself as perceiving the stone. [All consciousness is self-consciousness](https://iai.tv/articles/the-transcendental-self-auid-1560). Furthermore, self-consciousness is self-certainty; to be conscious of myself is to know (i.e., to have no reason to doubt) that I exist. Heidegger argues that Descartes stops at the certainty of self-consciousness, without asking what it is to be or exist as a self in the first place. Who or what is the “self” of which I am conscious? The self-certainty of the ego may be sufficient for Descartes’ purposes, since he is only interested in laying foundations for the sciences. But it [isn’t sufficient for Heidegger](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-6-meFnHxBTAbkLAv/page/n21/mode/2up), since he is interested in understanding what it is to be as a self, not only in the fact that it (whatever it is) is certain of its own existence. What Descartes “left undetermined when he began in this ‘radical’ way, was the kind of being which belongs to the res cogitans, or—more precisely—the meaning of the being of the ‘sum.’ […] the absolute ‘being-certain’ of the cogito exempted him from raising the question of the meaning of the being which this entity [Dasein] possesses.”

There is, however, one part of Descartes’ metaphysics about which Heidegger remains conspicuously silent, a part which, moreover, comes very close to Heidegger’s own phenomenology: Descartes’ theory of the union of mind and body.

My body, no matter how many changes it undergoes in quantity of matter, shape, or size, remains the same body *because it remains united to the same mind*.

\_\_\_

First, the union between mind and body, [Descartes argues](https://archive.org/details/meditationsonfir0000desc_g0t1/page/64/mode/2up), is a primitive, underivable experiential fact. The ego or “I” does not merely observe events occurring in its body, but directly experiences any modifications in its body brought about by its environment. Indeed, the union between mind and body is so intimate that “I and the body compose one thing,” and if “this were not so, I […] would not feel pain when the body was hurt, but would perceive the damage purely by the intellect, just as a sailor perceives by sight if anything in his ship is broken” (ibid.). Descartes explicitly writes that the ego and the body “compose one thing,” not two. Why? If the union is merely the composite of mind and body, then that leaves open the possibility that the “ego” or “I” perceives its body “just as a sailor perceives by sight if anything in his ship is broken.” This would indeed be what Heidegger stigmatizes as the mere conscious “clocking” of events occurring in the body. [For Descartes, by contrast](https://archive.org/details/meditationsonfir0000desc_g0t1/page/64/mode/2up), the appetites, sensations, and emotions experienced by the “ego” or “I” are *immediately felt* or *suffered* by it, not only *observed* by it. They constantly reinforce its consciousness of *this* body as its *own* body, precisely because it feels its appetites, sensations, and emotions in no *other* body: “There is nothing that my own nature teaches me more vividly than that I have a body, and that when I feel pain there is something wrong with the body, and that when I am hungry or thirsty the body needs food and drink, and so on.”

Second, modifications in the body are brought about by the environment, which means that consciousness of the body *is* *ipso facto* consciousness of the environment. The union between mind and body [places the ego or “I” directly in the world](https://archive.org/details/meditationsonfir0000desc_g0t1/page/64/mode/2up): “I am also taught by nature that various other bodies exist in the vicinity of my body…” It is not permanently shut up in itself, doubting the existence of the world, but rather actively engaged in it. Its relation to the world, moreover, is not purely cognitive or theoretical, but rather through and through practical. The embodied “ego” or “I” orients itself in the world via its projects (e.g., searching for food, clothing, and shelter), and its principal (teleological) objective (its “instinct”) is self-preservation, not theoretical cognition: “…the best system that could be devised is that it [the body] should produce the one sensation which, of all possible sensations, is most especially and most frequently conducive to [the preservation of the healthy man](https://archive.org/details/meditationsonfir0000desc_g0t1/page/66/mode/2up).”

Third, the world experienced by the embodied “ego” or “I” isn’t reducible to mere extension. On the contrary, the world becomes an environment in which my body responds to affordances that bear its projects: the “ego” or “I” is taught by nature that “some of these [bodies in the vicinity of its body] are to be sought out and others avoided” (ibid.). If the “ego” or “I” places its hand in the fire and feels pain, that isn’t a deception; that is its body indicating that the fire is to be avoided, precisely because it threatens its survival. This is the average, everyday existence of the embodied “ego” or “I,” and the world experienced by it exhibits sense or meaning relative to the way it bears on its projects and, indeed, its very existence.

Fourth, it is not the case that Descartes fails to inquire into what it is to exist as a self. As we have seen, Descartes’ characterization of selfhood can only be understood through his theory of the union of mind and body. This is [an embodied self, open to the world](https://iai.tv/video/the-self-and-the-world) via its projects and possibilities, not unlike the “existential” characterization Heidegger provides of Dasein in *Being and Time*.

My body is very different than bodies in general. For example, if any other body is divided, it is no longer the same body. My body, no matter how many changes it undergoes in quantity of matter, shape, or size, remains the same body *because it remains united to the same mind*. My body can’t be identified with *any* quantity of matter: the quantity can increase and decrease and even be replaced (the matter of the body is constantly overturning and being replaced by new matter) and yet remain one and the same body: *my* body, united to *my* mind. Indeed, Descartes argues that my body, *like my mind*, “can even be called *indivisible*,” because “if an arm or leg of a man is amputated,” it remains the one and the same body. Thus, the mind constitutes the very *identity* of the body, no matter how radically the latter changes, such that *my* body cannot even be understood independently of *my* mind. This is not the sort of thing one might expect a “dualist” to say.

No doubt, Heidegger's critique of Descartes remains powerful, but it is considerably weakened once Descartes’ theory of the union of mind and body is taken into consideration. For Descartes, the world I experience is not a world of bare extension determined only by the laws of nature instituted by God and canonized in physics. It is a world that is immediately experienced in its import and meaning for my vital existence and its projects.
