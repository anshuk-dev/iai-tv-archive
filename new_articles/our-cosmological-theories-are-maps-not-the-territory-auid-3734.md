# Our cosmological theories are maps, not the territory

*The Big Bang theory is just another model*

**By Andrew Jaffe** | *23rd November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 13 mins* | *2,691 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Our cosmological theories are maps, not the territory](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/our-cosmological-theories-are-the-maps-not-the-territory.webp)

*Ignorance is the enemy of science — yet modern science, especially cosmology, cannot work without ignorance. In this article, astrophysicist **Andrew Jaffe** argues that our most successful models are those that are able to deal with incomplete information. By comparing our measurements of the universe's expansion, he illustrates why our theories are active descriptions, as subject to change as the weather. Jaffe takes us on a journey exploring the vast and unpredictable universe and along the way, highlighting those observations, which if true, could alter our entire model of the universe.*

What we know: our observable patch of the Universe has been expanding for about 14 billion years. As it has rarefied and cooled, it has also changed, from a hot, dense, and smooth soup of fundamental particles to the lumpy panoply of galaxies, stars and planets around us today.

But how can we know this? We are stuck on one particular planet, orbiting one particular star, itself located in the spiral arm of one particular galaxy. It’s a bold, perhaps ridiculous, idea: we can have a theory for the whole Universe despite having observed a vanishingly small part of it. Yet from this limited vantage point, we have built up this picture of the Universe by combining our observations of the sky with our best ideas of how the Universe works, such as [quantum mechanics](https://iai.tv/articles/new-theory-argues-quantum-physics-must-abandon-irrational-numbers-and-the-continuum-auid-3580) and Einstein’s theory of general relativity. That mix, alongside more specific ingredients like our detailed understanding of particle physics and of the formation and evolution of stars, gives us the recipe for the Big Bang.

In 1920, scientists didn’t know that the Universe was filled with galaxies like our own Milky Way, each expanding away from all the others. This understanding was made possible first by the work of [Henrietta Swan Leavitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt), who discovered that we could indirectly measure the intrinsic brightness of certain types of stars, and hence infer their distance from us—such stars are called “standard candles.” Then, [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble), looking at these stars around the sky, realized that many of them resided in very distant galaxies, and using the shift in their color due to the Doppler effect, showed that galaxies were all rushing away from each other, direct evidence of an expanding Universe.

This Big Bang, like all our scientific theories, is a model for the world—it gives us predictions for what to expect when we look through our telescopes. These models correspond to the world, [but they are not the same thing as the world](https://iai.tv/video/down-the-wormhole).

“The map is not the territory,” but at least a map purports to depict places that some cartographer has actually observed, directly or indirectly. But the cosmologists’ claim is stronger: the Big Bang is a model for the whole Universe, even parts that we’ve never observed, even parts that are so far away that we might never even be able to observe them.

[Theories make predictions](https://iai.tv/articles/good-science-requires-much-more-than-evidence-auid-3691), and if those predictions are borne out by our observations and experiments, that’s about the best we can do. But theories often come with a lot of other ideas, entities that we can’t observe, but that make the theory easier to understand. Newton’s “inverse square law” theory of gravity postulated a force acting instantaneously at a distance. Einstein’s General Relativity superseded this with a mathematical form that makes the most sense when thought of as a theory of curved space and time. This preserved Newton’s laws as an excellent approximation—his theory was a very good description, but Einstein’s is better. And neither Newton’s force nor Einstein’s geometrical picture need correspond to anything that really exists, as long as the mathematical formulae still work.

Philosophers of science have long debated these questions. Do our theories describe anything real, or are they mere descriptions of our past and future observations? As these descriptions and predictions improve, does the theory get closer and closer to the truth? How can we tell?

In my book, [*The Random Universe*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300250503/the-random-universe/), I suggest that the mathematics of probability and uncertainty are needed to answer these questions: we test our theories by asking how probable they are in light of our observations, and the better theories are more probable.

Scientific ideas are products of the human imagination, whatever we call them. “Theory” is more of a technical term, referring to fully baked sets of ideas covering some general topic (gravity, electrodynamics, the quantum world, evolution, …), but “model” refers to almost everything that we need to specify the probabilities that in turn describe our observations: the optical properties of our telescopes, the noise of our detectors, the interstellar gas and dust that can contaminate the light from distant galaxies as it passes through space en route to us. All just to test our models of gravity or cosmology. And we need to at least tentatively add another important ingredient to our model, called the Copernican Principle, going back to Copernicus’ understanding that the Earth was not the centre of the cosmos: [we don’t inhabit a special place in the Universe](https://iai.tv/video/the-centre-of-the-universe), and so we can assume that any other observers elsewhere, even very distant, would see a sky very similar to ours. This insight helps us move from our particular observations to more general claims about the Universe.

Our models and theories, however, have what scientists call “free parameters,” numbers that we have to measure—using probability to determine their likely values—to fully pin down the theory. For the Big Bang, this includes parameters describing the amount and kinds of matter and energy that make up the Universe, and especially one that describes how fast galaxies are receding from one another today. This speed is known as the Hubble constant, denoted *H*0, measured in the slightly obscure units of kilometres per second per megaparsec. The Hubble constant measures the expansion rate of the Universe today. When I was a graduate student in the early 1990s, one group of cosmologists had measured this number to be around 100 in those units, and another to be around 50, both with error bars much smaller than the difference between them. This is the kind of discrepancy that sometimes got cosmology labelled as a not particularly serious scientific endeavor.

Since then, we’ve made progress, of a sort. A group led by Wendy Freedman used the Hubble Space Telescope to make much more accurate and precise measurements of Leavitt’s standard candles and hence the expansion rate, giving a value of 72 ± 8—right between the two older groups’ numbers, and with small enough error bars to rule them both out. These “±” error bars codify our uncertainty in the result, giving the probability that it has any particular value—most probably close to 72, somewhat less likely to be as low as 64 or as high as 80, even less so to be much higher or lower. We’ve since understood the original 50 vs 100 discrepancy as a failure of the groups’ assumptions—not about the cosmological model itself, but about the other ingredients: the properties of the standard candles and of the astrophysical foregrounds which obscure our view of them and of how to account for any systematic errors of their respective measurements. And, as has become obvious in hindsight, confirmation bias: each group made choices in dealing with these effects that reinforced their measurements, keeping each around 50 or around 100 even as they claimed to be dealing with all these complications. Science is a human endeavour.

Are things any better, now that we’ve discovered that the right answer lies in between? Perhaps not. Over the last 30 years, cosmologists have started to use a new standard candle, the light from a particular type of supernova—a star exploding at the end of its life. These techniques give an expansion rate of *H*0 = 73.5 ± 0.8—the error bar has gone down by a factor of 10.

Meanwhile, we’ve also developed completely different techniques to determine the Hubble constant. Measurements of light from the very early Universe, known as the cosmic microwave background (CMB), provide an indirect measurement: the pattern of hot and cold spots in the CMB depends on the contents of the Universe and its history. Armed with this full model, we can transform a map of the CMB into measurements of *H*0. The best measurements of the CMB come from the [Planck satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_(spacecraft)). Full disclosure: I was a member of the Planck team. When we analyse Planck’s maps, we find *H*0 = 67.4 ± 0.5. We’re closer to 72 than 50 or 100, but look at those error bars: at this level of precision, 73.5 ± 0.8 is very different. Cosmologists call this discrepancy the “Hubble tension.” Something’s gotta give.

Experimental science—and, especially, interpreting the results of an experiment—is very hard. In astronomy, you need both to design the hardware and to understand how the apparatus gives only a distorted, noisy picture of what you’re trying to measure. So one solution to the Hubble tension is that one, or both, of the teams has made a mistake in the design or interpretation of their experiment. But experimentalists are also very careful: they have checked their results, checked the probabilities that they have assigned, again and again, and revisited them with other techniques. The tension remains.

A lot of scientific knowledge and modelling goes into our understanding of cosmological results, from the evolution of stars and galaxies to the electronics and chemistry needed to design the sensitive detectors collecting the light in our satellites and telescopes. The philosophers [W. V. O. Quine and Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhem%E2%80%93Quine_thesis) (separately) realized that you can’t test a scientific theory in isolation—all these ancillary models are always in the mix. [Imre Lakatos](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/) called this a “protective belt” around the more fundamental theories we are testing.

If we understand both the experimental apparatus and the necessary astrophysics, the remaining possibility is that everyone is interpreting their results correctly, but that we’ve got the cosmological model wrong. Imagine you want to measure the temperature of a room. Your friend leaves a thermometer in the middle of the room and reads it out after an hour. And you open a window from outside and stick your thermometer into the room. To compare measurements, you each need a model of the room. If the temperature is the same across the room and doesn’t change with time, then the two will agree. But in a more complicated situation—it’s cold outside and hence the air near the window is cooler than the middle of the room—you will confidently declare a temperature that doesn’t correspond to the actual temperature of the room. You need a more complicated model.

Perhaps we are in that situation: both measurements are precise, but the cosmological model is wrong. This is every scientist’s dream: breaking a model and finding a new theory which fits the data better. So far, however, we haven’t found a good way to reconcile the two measurements. The CMB measures the rate of expansion by using light from the early Universe; the standard candles use the properties of stars and galaxies in the more or less nearby Universe (over the last few billion years). So perhaps changing the rate of expansion of the Universe some time between the formation of the CMB and the standard-candle measurements can help? But so far there have been no really successful ideas—anything that moves the *H*0 measurements closer to each other disagrees with other measurements that we have made.

Do we need to be bolder still? Maybe the Big Bang itself has to go? The thing is, the Big Bang is a very successful model—it doesn’t just explain the broad facts of the expansion of the Universe, but also small details of its evolution over time, especially the detailed pattern of the CMB itself. It is very difficult to evade the idea that the Universe is about 14 billion years old, and was much hotter and denser and smoother in the distant past.

These are exactly the ingredients that make up the Big Bang, a cocktail combining astronomical observations with particle physics, gravity, and thermodynamics, the fundamental theories physicists have built over the last couple of centuries. And yet, over those same centuries, we have learned that every theory eventually breaks when examined closely enough. For now, our best description is a Universe expanding from a hot, dense, and smooth initial state. This might fail, for example, on vastly larger scales, on which some have speculated that even the laws of physics—the number of dimensions of space, or the kinds of particles that exist—might change.

Our cosmological model is woven into the quilt of our more fundamental theories of particle physics, gravity, and thermodynamics: not all cosmological models are allowed by those theories. Conversely, not all fundamental theories are compatible with our cosmological observations and the models we have created to explain those observations.

Our Big Bang model already requires ingredients that our terrestrial experiments have never seen: [dark matter](https://iai.tv/video/beyond-the-darkness) and [dark energy](https://iai.tv/video/dark-energy-and-the-universe), their existence inferred only through their large-scale gravitational effects. These are part of Lakatos’ “protective belt” around the standard Big Bang model of cosmology, able to help the theory respond to potentially worrisome data. Lakatos distinguished *progressive* changes, which make new predictions, from *degenerating* ones, which merely patch the theory to fit new data. These dark components have so far passed the test for a progressive program, predicting the statistical pattern of the CMB. Any further additions to the Big Bang to ameliorate the Hubble tension will face the same test.

But we can’t make arbitrary changes to one patch of our quilt without affecting the others. Ameliorating the tension might require a change in the kinds of particles that make up the Universe or in Einstein’s theory of gravity. Or does it tell us something about the way those two theories are expected to come together in a so-called [theory of everything](https://iai.tv/video/the-theory-of-everything-is-a-mirage)? These changes would have to be subtle: not easily observable in the kinds of experiments we build here on Earth, not observable in the first 90 or so years of cosmology as a proper science, but just coming into view with some of our observations now.

As we learn more, as we measure those free parameters, and discover that we need *new* parameters to explain phenomena that we hadn’t envisioned when we first wrote down the theory, we hope that we are getting closer and closer to a better and better description of the Universe. [Is “better” the same as “true”? Can we ever get there?](https://iai.tv/video/truth-theory-and-ultimate-reality)

As a theory is better able to explain the phenomena of the world, does that imply that its description—Einstein’s curved spacetime, for example—is closer to the truth? So-called [realists](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-realism/) hope so. Or is truth something we can never be sure of? [Empiricist philosophers](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/constructive-empiricism/) like Bas van Fraassen think that better predictions are all we can hope for, perhaps along with better experiments that can test more of the theory, eventually even those parts that were once believed unobservable. I am tempted to agree. Over the past century, our telescopes have looked at larger distances, and our microscopes at smaller distances, bringing more and more—atoms and electrons, more and more distant galaxies—into view. But there will always be phenomena we can’t observe.

Our theories are descriptions of reality, and “describe” is an active verb. Reality, like history and like life, is just one damn thing after another. Maybe our theories will always be incomplete—nature gives no guarantee that she can be captured by our descriptions. As a scientist, this might be good news—[there is always more to learn](https://iai.tv/video/our-future-theories-of-the-universe). And it also means that we’ll always need more and better data to move forward. Simply sitting and thinking will never be enough.

My suspicion goes back to the fact that experimental science is hard, and that we’ll understand (soon, I hope) that one—or more than one—of the teams measuring the Hubble constant has made a subtle error. But my hope is for something more exciting, a small fracture, if not an actual break, in the Big Bang, perhaps an indication of new physical laws, new particles, new dimensions of space and time. Even if we haven’t yet been clever enough to see how.
