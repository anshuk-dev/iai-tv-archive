# Michael Levin: Memory and intelligence do not require a brain

*Genes and evolution can’t explain life’s behaviour*

**By Michael Levin** | *4th November 2026* | *Read time: approx. 15 mins* | *2,987 words* | **[NEW ARTICLE]**

![Michael Levin: Memory and intelligence do not require a brain](https://cdn.iai.tv/assets/Uploads/_resampled/FillWzEyODAsNzIwXQ/michael-levin-memory-and-intelligence-do-not-require-a-brain2.webp)

---

*For over a century, biology has centred genes and evolution in its explanations of behaviour. But synthetic biologist **Michael Levin** is creating new kinds of lifeform, known as biobots, which he argues challenge that picture. Built from unmodified frog and human cells, Levin’s biobots behave in ways that their genomes and evolutionary history fail to predict. In this interview, he tells the IAI’s Alasdair Craig that biology must do better. The solution, he thinks, may involve taking seriously the idea that biobots have minds, and that memory, learning and purposeful behaviour can exist far beyond the brain and its neurons.*

**Alasdair Craig: Could you give us a brief overview of what you're aiming to do in regenerative medicine and cancer treatment?**

Michael Levin: Imagine that you had a way of communicating goals, anatomical goals, to groups of cells, to ask them to build whatever structure you wanted them to build. Then birth defects, traumatic injury, degenerative disease, aging, cancer, all of these things could be solved. This is the major unsolved problem of regenerative medicine. So this is what we do in my group: we work on ways to communicate anatomical goals to groups of cells.

**Something that you suggest holds science back is what you call “mind blindness,” and you talk about seeing minds everywhere. So, can you give us an example of evidence of minds where we wouldn’t expect to see them?**

Well, just to take a step back, imagine what programming a computer used to look like in the 1940s and ’50s. You’re sitting there in front of this giant thing and sort of pulling connections in and out, right? In those days, you had to interact with the hardware. Now, this is the current assumption of modern molecular medicine: all the excitement is about the hardware. Genomic editing, pathway rewiring, protein engineering, and so on are all about the hardware. And so one of the big questions is: okay, you have that hardware, and we know that in the brain, some of that molecular hardware underlies memory and learning and problem solving and all these other things. The question is, where else might it be found? [This notion that it’s only to be found in the brain is just an assumption, not an established scientific fact](https://iai.tv/articles/memory-is-not-stored-in-the-brain-auid-3420). My group applies the tools of cognitive and behavioral science to all kinds of other weird substrates, and we’ve found memory and learning capacity in cells and tissues. This opens up all kinds of novel ways to communicate with the living material to try to get it to do things that we don’t know how to micromanage it to do.

**What are [xenobots](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2112672118) and [anthrobots](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202303575), and are they evidence of [minds existing in unexpected forms](https://iai.tv/articles/patterns-are-alive-and-we-are-living-patterns-auid-2919)?**

So the background to this is that we discovered an interface, namely [bioelectricity](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2021.02.034), which is the ability of cells to join into electrical networks to process memories and goal states, and so on. We can hijack that capacity and communicate with these cells. Now, this is not in human patients yet, but in model systems, we can, for example, induce [formation of ectopic organs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22159581), normalize tumors [into normal tissue](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24830454), cause [limb regeneration](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abj2164), and [repair really profound birth defects](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-03334-5). And in all of those cases, our goal is to communicate to the cells a particular pattern-memory of what it is that they should be building.

\_\_\_

The space of possible new beings is basically endless because biology is incredibly creative, and you don’t need a lengthy evolutionary history to be able to do these things.

\_\_\_

And one of the things that we’re very interested in is: Where do pattern-memories normally come from, and what kind of pattern-memories can cells actually build to? One idea that’s out there is that these patterns come from evolution, and this is all that they can build. So, for example, the oak genome builds oak leaves, and the human genome builds humans, and so on. But what we’ve been studying is the plasticity of this process. First of all, finding out that, yes, you can put in patterns that are not natural patterns. Anthrobots and xenobots are living constructs that we make from genetically completely normal tissue. So we don’t edit the genome. We don’t put in synthetic biology circuits. We liberate the cells from their in vivo environment, and we find out that they make novel living substrates that have new behaviors, new capabilities, new forms, new gene expression, and so on, and these things have never been around before. There is no long history of evolutionary selection to be a good xenobot or to be a good anthrobot, and so that forces us to ask some very interesting questions about where their pattern-memories come from. What is the plasticity of cells, and what can they build besides the normal standard thing that they build?

**What exactly are these xenobots and anthrobots, though?**

A xenobot is what happens when you take some epithelial cells from an early frog embryo, liberating them from the rest of the embryo. You’ve isolated these cells from the signals that would normally force them to have a very boring life on the outside of a tadpole, keeping out bacteria and so on. Once you’ve removed those influences you get to find out what these cells are really capable of. They form this little round thing that uses tiny motile hairs to swim through the water, and it swims around. And it has all kinds of interesting properties. One of those interesting properties—a new property that the frog embryos don’t have—is that [it reacts to sound](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-025-08086-9). So they start to express a bunch of response genes for auditory stimuli, and they respond to sound. And the other thing they can do is, if you sprinkle a bunch of loose skin cells into the water, the xenobots will run around both individually and as a group, collecting them into little piles. The little piles become the next generation of xenobots, and then guess what they do? They go and do the same exact thing, and they repeat. And so you have multiple generations. We call this [kinematic self-replication](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2112672118). As far as I know, no other animal on Earth replicates that way.

Anthrobots are a similar story, except they’re made of adult human cells. So when we first showed them xenobots, some people said, “Well, maybe that’s some kind of very amphibian-specific thing, or maybe it’s an embryonic thing.” So I said, “Well, what’s the furthest you can get away from an amphibian or an embryo?” That would be an adult human. And so we took epithelial cells from the trachea of adult human patients, and we put them in a new environment, and they also got together into this little round thing that we call an anthrobot. It has spontaneous motion; it swims around on its own. It has a couple of interesting capacities. First of all, it expresses about 9,000 genes differently than the tracheal cells originally did. In other words, about half the genome is totally differently expressed in its new environment. Again, we haven’t touched the genetics. We haven’t put in any scaffolds or nanomaterials. This is entirely [the plasticity of the living matter](https://iai.tv/articles/organisms-are-musicians-not-machines-auid-3436). They’re younger than the cells they come from, so they actually [roll their aging clock backwards a bit](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202409330). And they have this amazing ability. If you place them on a bed of human neurons that you’ve put a big scratch through—you take a scalpel, put a big scratch through the human neurons—then the anthrobots collect in the scratch, and if you lift them up four days later, you see that what they’ve been doing is taking the two sides of the neural wound and basically healing it. So who would have known that your tracheal cells, which sit there quietly in your airway for decades, have the ability to form this self-motile little creature that can repair neural wounds?

These are just examples. The space of possible new beings is basically endless because biology is incredibly creative, and you don’t need a lengthy evolutionary history to be able to do these things.

**Do you see a challenge here for a [neo-Darwinian](https://iai.tv/articles/dawkins-neo-darwinism-is-itself-a-religious-theory-auid-3652) explanation of behavior? Is there something about the behavior of these biobots that [can’t be explained just by appealing to genetics and environment](https://iai.tv/video/the-not-so-blind-watchmaker)?**

Well, I want to be clear about a couple of things. First of all, the things that I’m [about to say](https://www.mdpi.com/2409-9287/11/5/161) are not driven just by the data from anthrobots and xenobots. This is from a very large amount of other work. What is true about the xenobots and anthrobots, like with any other system, is that if you insist on looking at the lowest molecular level, what you’re going to see is what you always see, which is chemistry doing what chemistry does. And so, if you look down at the lowest level, you say, well, there is no challenge because we see there’s plausible chemistry and physics occurring. Of course, there is. This is not magic. This is exactly the same thing that’s inside of all of us: [chemistry and physics](https://iai.tv/articles/consciousness-drives-evolution-auid-2889).

\_\_\_

Our intuitions for where minds of different degrees can be found are poorly calibrated.

\_\_\_

However, suppose you wanted to be able to predict the fact that xenobots and anthrobots are possible. What information would you need for that? Well, suppose you sequence the genome of an anthrobot. All you have ever seen is the human genome. So you would have no idea what it’s capable of. Its ability to heal neural scars, for example, will not be apparent. But this is true of normal development as well. The problem is that normal development is so reliable, and it’s at the end of such a long evolutionary history, that we kind of feel we understand what’s going on. In other words, we say, okay, everything that didn’t look like a human died out long ago, and so therefore that’s it. That’s why the human genome gives this body, and we kind of feel like we have a story. The thing with anthrobots is that there’s never been any anthrobots. There’s never been selection to be a good anthrobot. So we have to do two things as scientists. First of all, we need to say, “Here’s the genome that encodes molecular hardware in the cell-proteins. How do we go from that to knowing what the capabilities of the thing it builds will be?” We need a procedure for this. So that’s the first challenge. But, second, we have to also say something else. We have to be able to say when we paid the computational cost of the design of an anthrobot. We know when the frog and human computational costs were paid: for millions of years those embodied genomes were bashing against evolutionary selection. That’s when that was paid. But when did they learn to be a good xenobot or anthrobot? Why did that happen?

Of course, the standard paradigm replies: “Well, these things are just *emergent* from the genome.” But dig in a little bit and ask, “[Well, what does that mean](https://iai.tv/video/the-mystery-of-emergence)?" I really don’t like the word “[emergence](https://iai.tv/articles/emergence-explains-nothing-and-is-bad-science-auid-3385).” I think it just signals surprise. Great, we got surprised, but we need to do better. This seems to me to be a fundamental knowledge gap in the Modern Synthesis (Neo-Darwinism). [We need to do better](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tig.2025.04.002).

**Wouldn’t it be enough just to say, “Look, it’s just a sort of side effect”? You’ve taken the cells out of their normal environment, put them into completely different environments, and so you should expect some different behavior.**

Well, no, that’s not good enough. It’s not enough to say you should expect different behavior because the behavior could have been that the cells die, the cells crawl away from each other, the cells express different sets of genes or no new genes, and they don’t repair neural wounds. It’s not enough to just say “something weird is going to happen.” You have to be able to predict *what’s* going to happen. And by the way, all of the things that we’ve seen so far have been surprising. No one saw them coming. It’s very easy to look afterwards and say, “Well, I see how that works.” Yeah, *after* we did this, and *now* you see how it works. But no one used the conventional paradigms and saw this coming, and so we have to do better. We actually have to be able to say *why*. Where do the specific properties of these things come from?

**So is your preferred explanation to appeal to the idea that these are minds, and does this connect to your idea that there are minds everywhere?**

Yeah, let’s separate those things. So we have not yet made any specific claims about xenobots or anthrobots in terms of their cognitive capacities. This is very important. When you have a system, you cannot decide whether it has a mind based on your philosophy or definitions or your feelings about things. You have to do experiments, because you don’t know, until you’ve tested a system’s intelligence, what it actually has.

When I talk about [mind being everywhere](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2022.768201), what I mean is that the tools that we use in behavioral science and neuroscience—the way that we study memory, learning, problem solving, goal-directed activity, all these kinds of things—those same tools are potentially applicable to a very wide range of substrates. You can’t just say, “[Well, this thing isn’t a brain, it’s a cell, so it certainly can’t know anything](https://iai.tv/video/memories-are-not-stored-in-the-brain-victoria-trumbull).” What we’ve found is that if you look for memory, if you look for problem solving, if you look for goal-directed navigation of difficult problem spaces, you find them in [all kinds of other substrates and cells and tissues](https://iai.tv/articles/new-studies-suggest-consciousness-exists-in-organisms-without-brains-auid-3597). And when you find them, that gives you a new way to reprogram that system, and [interact and try to communicate with it](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2026.103691), which is much better than simply assuming that everything has to be done at the molecular level and that nothing knows what it’s doing.

**You broaden this out quite a lot, right? You talk about finding minds in lots of strange places…**

Yeah. So one crucial thing is that when I say “minds,” I don’t mean that we can assume that there’s a spirit in every rock that has the same kinds of thoughts that you have. What I’m saying is that [intelligence or cognitive capacity](iai-academy/courses/info?course=exploring-intelligence) is an extremely wide and diverse spectrum. And if you’re willing to use the tools that we have—the tools of cybernetics, of control theory, of behavioral science, of [psychoanalysis](https://iai.tv/articles/the-latest-neuroscience-may-be-proving-freud-right-auid-3646), whatever—you can apply them to all sorts of things and see what you get. It’s an empirical kind of experimental science. And so yeah, we’ve been able to do this in all sorts of systems, and in particular, we’ve been applying these tools to [very minimal systems such as sorting algorithms](https://doi.org/10.1177/10597123241269740).

You know, there’s this popular notion, that many people feel, that, “Okay, human and animal bodies obey the laws of chemistry, but there’s something more there.” We don’t know what it is. There’s no good definition for it, but we’re pretty sure that the laws of chemistry don’t tell the entire story of the human mind. That’s how most people feel. And then there’s this other region of the world, which we call machines, and these things do only what the algorithm tells them to do. *We* have some sort of poorly understood magic through which we are more than the rules of chemistry, but these machines are in a different category because they do exactly what their algorithms and what their mechanism tells them to do. But, again, this is an [assumption](https://www.noemamag.com/living-things-are-not-machines-also-they-totally-are/), and in fact it seems very dangerous to me because I think that formal models never fully describe anything. They can be useful, but algorithms and Turing machines and all those things are just formal models. It’s fine that we realize that those things are not appropriate for the entirety of cognitive behavior and biology. But I think it’s very dangerous to assume that over here, in the domain of machines, they describe the world perfectly. And when we look, we actually [find](https://www.forbes.com/sites/andreamorris/2025/11/13/the-secret-life-of-algorithms/) that, yeah, these things do what the algorithm forces them to do. But they also do some other stuff, and that is nowhere in the algorithm. And the other things that they do are not simply a matter of unpredictability or complexity. Those are easy, and everybody knows that happens. They are things that are recognizable to behavioral scientists, like problem solving and homophily and things like that, and that is a very significant fact. What it’s telling us is that our [intuitions](https://www.noemamag.com/why-we-fear-diverse-intelligence-like-ai/) for where minds of different degrees can be found are poorly calibrated, and that we need to stop making assumptions. We need to have some humility about this and actually do experiments, and when you do experiments, you make lots of discoveries.

**So the suggestion you’re making is not just that [the human brain is not a machine](https://iai.tv/articles/neuroscience-needs-a-new-paradigm-the-brain-is-not-a-machine-auid-3338), but that things that we normally think of as machines are to be thought of on the model of our minds or brains?**

Sort of. I’m not saying they’re like our brains. I’m saying that the word “machine”… well, nobody has a good definition for it. But what we typically think of as the machine, in any case, is just a formal model, and the question of whether there’s anything in the world that perfectly matches that formal model is an open question. [I don’t think anything actually matches it precisely](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2026.103668), and I think that while our brains are doing various things on the far side of the spectrum of intelligence, the lower end of that spectrum bleeds into some very unconventional substrates that we normally don’t think about, and this is why the field of diverse intelligence is here to develop rigorous tools to examine everything—materials, algorithms, all kinds of other things. Personally, I’m not sure that anything in the world answers to the description of a dumb machine, but we’ll find out through experiment.
